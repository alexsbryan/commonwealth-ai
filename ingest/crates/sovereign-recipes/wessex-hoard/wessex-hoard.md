# Catalogue entry 1 — Aldfrith penny, Series Y

A silver sceatta of Aldfrith, king of Northumbria, struck at the Eoforwic
mint. Obverse: a stylised lion walking left, tail raised over the back.
Reverse: a pellet-in-annulet within a beaded border. Weight 1.21 g,
diameter 12 mm, die axis 180 degrees. Catalogue reference Wessex Down 1. The issue is conventionally placed
in the reign of Aldfrith, that is between 685 and 704, though the narrower
dating within that reign is contested (see Halstead 2014 and Ferreira 2021
below). Two specimens from the Wessex Down hoard share an obverse die.

# Catalogue entry 2 — Series R sceatta, runic type

A silver sceatta of the Series R group, struck at the Gipeswic mint. The
obverse carries a radiate bust right with the runic legend EPA; the reverse
shows a standard with four pellets. Weight 0.95 g, diameter 11 mm. Catalogue reference Wessex Down 2. No ruler
is named on the coin and none can be inferred from the types; Series R is a
mint issue rather than a royal one. The Wessex Down hoard contained
fourteen examples, of which four are die-duplicates.

# Catalogue entry 3 — Beonna penny

A silver penny of Beonna, king of the East Angles, struck at the Gipeswic
mint by the moneyer Efe. Obverse: the runic legend BEONNA REX around a
central pellet. Reverse: the moneyer's name in a cross-and-pellets field.
Weight 1.05 g, diameter 13 mm. Catalogue reference Wessex Down 3. Beonna reigned from about 749; the coinage
is metrologically distinct from the sceattas that precede it, being struck
to a heavier standard and to a markedly higher silver fineness.

# Catalogue entry 4 — Offa gold dinar

A gold imitation dinar of Offa, king of Mercia, struck at the Lundenwic
mint. The obverse copies an Abbasid Arabic legend, blundered, with the
Latin legend OFFA REX inserted upside down relative to the Arabic. The
reverse continues the Arabic. Weight 4.28 g, diameter 20 mm. Catalogue reference Wessex Down 4. The prototype
is dated by its own Arabic legend to AH 157, that is 773 or 774, which
gives the Mercian copy a terminus post quem. Offa reigned 757 to 796.

# Catalogue entry 5 — Coenwulf mancus

A gold mancus of Coenwulf, king of Mercia, struck at the Lundenwic mint by
the moneyer Deigmund. Obverse: a diademed bust right, legend COENVVLF REX
MERCIORVM. Reverse: DE VICO LVNDONIAE in four lines. Weight 4.33 g,
diameter 21 mm. Catalogue reference Wessex Down 5. Only one specimen is known outside the Wessex Down hoard.

# Catalogue entry 6 — Series X sceatta, "Wodan/monster" type

A silver sceatta of the Series X group. The obverse shows a facing head
with radiating hair; the reverse a fantastic beast looking back over its
shoulder. Weight 1.14 g, diameter 12 mm. Catalogue reference Wessex Down 6. No ruler's name appears. Series X
is usually assigned to a mint at Ribe rather than to any English mint, and
its presence in the Wessex Down hoard is evidence of North Sea circulation
rather than of local striking.

# Catalogue entry 7 — billon sceatta, Series E "porcupine"

A billon sceatta of the Series E group, degraded standard, struck at an
unidentified continental mint. Obverse: the so-called porcupine, a curved
line of pellets over a crescent. Reverse: a standard with the letters TOT
II. Weight 0.88 g, diameter 11 mm. Catalogue reference Wessex Down 7. The silver content is visibly low; the
flan is coppery where it has worn.

# The mint at Eoforwic

Eoforwic, the Northumbrian mint at York, struck the royal sceatta coinage of
the later seventh and eighth centuries. Its output is distinguished from the
southern mints by the consistent use of an animal obverse rather than a bust,
and by a slightly heavier weight standard — the Eoforwic sceattas run about a
tenth of a gram above their southern contemporaries throughout the period.
The mint appears to have operated under direct royal control rather than
through independent moneyers, which is why no moneyer's name is found on any
Eoforwic issue before the ninth century. Its products travelled: examples turn
up in southern hoards, including this one, in numbers that argue for genuine
circulation rather than for isolated losses by travellers.

# The mint at Gipeswic

Gipeswic, at Ipswich in East Anglia, was the most productive of the English
sceatta mints. It struck the Series R group over several decades and, later,
the reformed penny coinage of Beonna. Moneyers' names appear on the Beonna
issues but not on the earlier Series R, which is the clearest sign that the
reform brought the mint under a named administration where it had previously
worked anonymously. The volume is considerable: Series R accounts for more
surviving specimens than any other English sceatta group, and the die study
underlying that count identifies well over two hundred obverse dies. The mint
sat beside a working trading settlement, and its coins are found across the
southern North Sea as well as inland.

# The mint at Lundenwic

Lundenwic, the trading settlement west of the Roman walls of London, struck
for the Mercian kings from the eighth century onward. Both the Offa gold dinar
and the Coenwulf mancus name it in their reverse legends — the mancus does so
explicitly, DE VICO LVNDONIAE, "from the trading place of London", which is
the earliest unambiguous mint signature on an English gold coin. The mint's
silver output is far larger than its gold, but the gold is what fixes its
place in the record, since the two surviving gold issues both carry a king's
name and a mint name together. Its moneyers are known by name from the reverse
legends of the reformed penny coinage.

# Aldfrith of Northumbria

Aldfrith ruled Northumbria from 685 until his death in 704. He was the first
English king to place his own name on a coinage, and the Series Y sceattas of
Eoforwic are that coinage — before him, English coins name mints, moneyers or
nothing at all, but never the ruler under whose authority they were struck.
The innovation did not immediately take: his successors reverted to anonymous
types, and it is another two generations before a royal name appears regularly
again. He was a scholar as well as a king, and the literary sources are
unusually full for a Northumbrian ruler of the period, which is part of why
his coinage can be dated at all closely.

# Offa of Mercia

Offa ruled Mercia from 757 until 796 and was the dominant English king of the
later eighth century, exercising authority well beyond Mercia itself. His
coinage includes the reformed broad penny — a heavier, wider coin that
replaced the sceatta across southern England and set the shape of the English
penny for five hundred years — and, exceptionally, the gold dinar imitation
catalogued above. The reform was administrative as well as metrological:
moneyers are named, mints are distinguished, and the portrait work is of a
quality without parallel in contemporary northern Europe. He is the ruler
against whom the coinages of the smaller kingdoms, Beonna's among them, are
conventionally measured.

# Coenwulf of Mercia

Coenwulf ruled Mercia from 796 until 821, succeeding Offa's short-lived son
and holding the Mercian supremacy together for a further quarter century. His
gold mancus is the only English gold coin of the period naming both a king and
a mint, which makes it the fixed point around which the rest of the period's
gold is arranged. His silver coinage is large and well organised, continuing
Offa's reform without substantial change, and the moneyers who struck for Offa
at Lundenwic largely continued to strike for him. The reign is well documented
in charters, and those charters are what allow the mancus to be dated by its
moneyer rather than by its style.

# Beonna of East Anglia

Beonna ruled the East Angles from about 749; the end of his reign is not
recorded and is inferred from his coinage rather than from any written source.
That coinage is the earliest English one struck to a deliberately reformed
weight and fineness standard, and it is the reason his reign can be discussed
in numismatic terms at all. Three moneyers are known from the surviving dies,
of whom Efe is much the commonest. The reform anticipates Offa's by a
generation and was probably its model, though the East Anglian coinage is far
smaller and did not outlast the reign by long. Its silver is markedly purer
than the debased sceattas circulating alongside it.

# Halstead 2014 — the die-link case for an early Aldfrith issue

Halstead argues that the Series Y sceattas of Aldfrith, catalogue reference
Wessex Down 1, belong to the first years of the reign, between 685 and 690. The argument is a die-link: the
obverse die of the Wessex Down specimens is shared with a coin from the
Ferrybridge find, and that coin was struck over an issue of Aldfrith's
predecessor whose own terminal date is fixed. The chain of dies is
therefore anchored, and the anchor sits early in the reign.

# Ferreira 2021 — the hoard context of the Aldfrith penny

Ferreira dates the same Series Y sceattas of Aldfrith to the last decade of
the reign, between 695 and 704. The argument is hoard context: in the
Wessex Down deposit the Aldfrith pennies show markedly less wear than the
Series R coins buried with them, and the Series R group is independently
dated no earlier than about 710. A coin struck in 685 could not be fresher
than a coin struck in 710. Ferreira states plainly that Halstead's die
chain must be reversed in direction.

# Nkemdirim 2018 — weight standards of the Beonna coinage

Nkemdirim dates the Beonna pennies of the moneyer Efe to the last years of
the reign, between 757 and 760. The argument is metrological: the surviving
specimens cluster at 1.05 g, a standard reached only after the earlier and
lighter Efe dies had been withdrawn, and the weight series is monotonic
across the reign.

# Vasquez 2020 — the style of the Series R runic dies

Vasquez dates the runic Series R dies to between 710 and 725 on stylistic
grounds. The radiate bust is copied from a Merovingian prototype whose own
currency is confined to those years, and the runic EPA legend is cut by a
hand that does not appear on any later Gipeswic die.

# Ferreira 2021 — Series R in the Wessex Down hoard

Ferreira places the Series R group between 715 and 740 on hoard context.
The Wessex Down deposit contains Series R coins in three distinct wear
states, which argues for a long period of issue rather than a short one.
This is a widening of the conventional dating rather than a challenge to
it, and Ferreira notes agreement with Vasquez on the early end.

# Halstead 2014 and Nkemdirim 2018 on the Coenwulf mancus

Both Halstead and Nkemdirim date the gold mancus of Coenwulf, catalogue
reference Wessex Down 5, to between 805 and 810, and for the same reason: the moneyer Deigmund is attested in the
Lundenwic charter witness lists only in those years. The two attributions
agree in date, in evidence and in grade.
