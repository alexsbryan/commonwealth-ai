### Van Inwagen’s Consequence Argument

Peter van Inwagen presents the **Consequence Argument** as "the best argument" for incompatibilism—the view that free will and moral responsibility are incompatible with causal determinism [Source: incompatibilism-arguments]. The argument is designed to show that if determinism is true, then no one has any power over the past or the laws of nature; consequently, since our actions are inevitable consequences of those factors, we have no power over them either.

Van Inwagen offers an informal statement of this argument in his book *An Essay on Free Will* (1983). While the exact formal logic varies across presentations by Ginet (1966), Wiggins (1973), and van Inwagen himself, the core structure relies on a principle often labeled "Beta" or similar transfer principles:
1.  No one has power over the facts of the past [Source: freewill].
2.  No one has power over the laws of nature [Source: incompatibilism-arguments/freewill context].
3.  If determinism is true, then our present actions are necessary consequences of the past and the laws [Source: compatibilism].
4.  Therefore, no one has power over their present actions; we cannot do otherwise than what we actually do given the past and the laws [Source: compatibilism/incompatibilism-arguments].

The argument "shook compatibilists" because it provided powerful support for the claim that if determinism implies a single inevitable future, agents lack control over how that future unfolds [Source: compatibilism]. It earned incompatibilists an important dialectical advantage by challenging compatibilists to explain exactly where the reasoning fails or why our ordinary notion of ability survives this logic [Source: compatibilism].

### The Strongest Compatibilist Response: David Lewis’s Critique

While there have been numerous replies (e.g., Lehrer 1980; Slote 1982), the most influential objection is due to **David Lewis** in his 1981 paper "The Causal Explanation of Action." Lewis argues that van Inwagen’s argument equivocates on the meaning of **"is able to break a law of nature"** [Source: freewill].

Lewis distinguishes between two senses of breaking a law:
1.  **Sense A:** Acting in violation of a universal, exceptionless law (i.e., doing something that *would* be impossible if the laws were strictly followed).
2.  **Sense B:** Performing an action such that, had you done it, the past or the laws would have been different than they actually are.

Van Inwagen assumes Sense B when he claims we cannot change the past or laws. However, Lewis argues that compatibilists can accept that we lack "regulative control" over the past and laws (in the sense that we cannot make them false) while still maintaining that we have abilities defined by counterfactuals about our own actions [Source: freewill]. Specifically, Lewis suggests that in a deterministic world, I am able to do X if *if* I were to choose/decide to do X, then I would do X—even though determinism ensures I wouldn't decide otherwise given my actual prior state. The equivocation lies in treating "ability" as requiring the power to alter the antecedent conditions (past/laws), which van Inwagen denies is possible, whereas compatibilists define ability relative to the agent's internal states and choices alone.

### Alternative Compatibilist Strategy: Rejecting PAP via Frankfurt Cases

A second major compatibilist response does not try to save the Consequence Argument’s premise about abilities but instead attacks its underlying assumption: **Principle of Alternate Possibilities (PAP)**. PAP states that an agent is morally responsible only if they could have done otherwise [Source: alternative-possibilities/compatibilism].

Harry Frankfurt developed arguments (Frankfurt-style cases) showing that moral responsibility can exist even when alternate possibilities are removed or blocked by external factors (e.g., a neuroscientist who would force Agent A to act, but doesn't need to because Agent A acts freely on their own). If PAP is false, then the incompatibilist's focus on the "ability to do otherwise" becomes irrelevant. Compatibilists can thus sidestep the Consequence Argument entirely by rejecting the premise that free will requires regulative control over multiple possible futures [Source: compatibilism/alternative-possibilities]. This approach grounds agency in other features of volition rather than counterfactual flexibility.

### Conclusion and Remaining Tensions

The debate remains active. Van Inwagen rejects the idea that this is merely a verbal disagreement about 'can', insisting he and Lewis mean exactly what ordinary speakers mean in contexts of deliberation [Source: incompatibilism-arguments]. He argues that validity of Beta (the transfer principle) is more plausible than any specific compatibilist analysis of ability, especially since simple conditional analyses had already been rejected by many philosophers [Source: incompatibilism-arguments]. Consequently, while Lewis’s equivocation objection is widely cited as the strongest direct rebuttal to the Consequence Argument's logic, it has not fully resolved the tension for all parties; many compatibilists now prefer Frankfurt-style routes or "sourcehood" accounts over trying to defend regulative control directly against van Inwagen’s premises [Source: freewill/compatibilism].

### Van Inwagen’s Consequence Argument

Peter van Inwagen, along with Carl Ginet in the late 1960s and early 1970s, articulated what has become known as the **Consequence Argument** [Source: freewill]. It is widely regarded by incompatibilists as "the best argument" for the conclusion that free will is incompatible with determinism [Source: incompatibilism-arguments]. The argument relies on a principle of power transfer—often referred to as the rule of inference *Beta*—which states that if an agent lacks control over certain facts (like the past or laws of nature), they also lack control over any logical consequences of those facts [Source: compatibilism; Source: abilities].

Van Inwagen’s informal presentation in his 1983 book, *An Essay on Free Will*, proceeds through three main steps:
1. **The Premise of Determinism:** If causal determinism is true, then our present actions are necessary consequences of events in the remote past and the laws of nature.
2. **Powerlessness Over Past/Laws:** We have no power over the events of the remote past (they happened before we were born) and no power over the laws of nature (we cannot break them).
3. **Transfer of Powerlessness:** Because we lack control over the premises (past + laws), we consequently lack control over their logical consequences—namely, our own actions [Source: moral-responsibility].

Therefore, if determinism is true, human beings do not possess free will or moral responsibility because they could never have done otherwise than what they actually did [Source: incompatibilism-arguments; Source: freewill]. Van Inwagen insists this debate is about "ordinary speakers" meaning in contexts of deliberation, rejecting the idea that compatibilists and incompatibilists are merely using different definitions of 'can' [Source: incompatibilism-arguments].

### The Strongest Compatibilist Response: David Lewis’s Critique

The most influential objection to van Inwagen's argument comes from **David Lewis (1981)**, who contended that the Consequence Argument relies on an equivocation regarding the phrase **"is able to break a law of nature"** [Source: freewill]. 

Lewis distinguished between two distinct senses of ability when it comes to laws of nature:
1.  **Ability to violate:** The power to perform an action that would constitute a direct violation or breach of a natural law (e.g., lifting oneself by one's bootstraps). Under this sense, no agent has such an ability; if you did it, there was no law broken.
2.  **Counterfactual ability:** The capacity for things to be different in such a way that *if* they were, a law would have been different (or not existed) at the time [Source: freewill].

Van Inwagen’s argument assumes we lack control over the past and laws because we cannot "break" them. However, Lewis argued that while we may indeed lack the power to violate laws directly, determinism does not entail that our actions are fixed consequences of unchangeable laws in a way that removes freedom. Specifically, compatibilists can argue that an agent has the ability to do otherwise if, had she chosen differently, either her action or the prior state of the world/laws would have been different—without requiring her to perform a miraculous violation of physics [Source: abilities; Source: freewill]. 

By exposing this equivocation, Lewis attempts to show that one *can* be able to break a law (in the counterfactual sense) even if one cannot simply choose to break it outright. This allows for a form of "regulative control" where agents are responsible for their actions because those actions track with what they could have done in alternative scenarios, without needing to defy physical necessity directly [Source: compatibilism].

### Other Compatibilist Strategies and Critiques

While Lewis’s critique is prominent, other compatibilists have adopted different strategies to address the Consequence Argument:
*   **Rejecting Alternative Possibilities:** Some modern compatibilists sidestep the argument entirely by rejecting the premise that free will requires an ability to do otherwise (the Principle of Alternative Possibilities). Using arguments developed by Harry Frankfurt (1969), these theorists argue that moral responsibility depends on reasons-responsiveness or sourcehood rather than regulative control [Source: compatibilism; Source: freewill]. 
*   **Conditional Analyses:** Earlier attempts relied on "Conditional Analyses" (originally proposed by Hume) to define abilities. However, van Inwagen and others noted that even if a compatibilist provides such an analysis, it does not answer the incompatibilist's core claim—that determinism makes those very conditions impossible for us to satisfy in practice [Source: incompatibilism-arguments; Source: abilities].
*   **The N\*** Repair: Some analyses suggest the original Consequence Argument relies on logical principles like *Agglomeration*, which have been challenged as invalid, suggesting the argument requires repair or refinement to remain sound [Source: incompatibilism-arguments].

Ultimately, while many compatibilists find the Consequence Argument unsound (often via Lewis’s route), its impact was profound. It "set the agenda" for contemporary theories of free will by forcing compatibilists to provide rigorous accounts of what control actually means if not the ability to break laws of nature [Source: compatibilism].

### Reliabilism and Gettier Cases
The traditional Justified True Belief (JTB) analysis fails because it allows for epistemic luck [Source: epistemology]. To address this, reliabilists argue that justification depends on the reliability of one’s belief sources [Source: epistemology]. 

However, simple process reliabilism struggles with Gettier cases where a belief is produced by a generally reliable faculty but happens to be true only due to luck in a specific instance. For example, in the "barn-facades" case, Henry uses normal vision—a reliable cognitive process—to identify a barn. He sees the one real barn among many facades and forms a true belief. Yet, his belief does not qualify as knowledge because it was formed merely through luck; had he looked at a facade, he would have been wrong [Source: epistemology].

To handle such cases, virtue reliabilists (such as Ernest Sosa) refine the analysis by distinguishing between holding a belief *because of* an epistemic competence and having a belief that is correct *because of* that competence [Source: reliabilism]. In Gettier scenarios like Lehrer’s Nogot/Havit case—where a subject believes someone owns a Ford based on misleading testimony—the subject exercises an epistemic competence but fails to be correct due to that competence. The challenge for reliabilists is defining this "correctness" relation so it rules out luck without being overly restrictive [Source: reliabilism].

### The Lottery Paradox and Reliability
The lottery paradox presents a significant problem for whether reliability alone suffices for knowledge. 

**The Structure of the Paradox:**
In D.C. Makinson's formulation (originating from Raymond Wilder), if one accepts propositions with high probability, one rationally believes each individual ticket in a 100-ticket lottery will lose. By agglomeration—believing both p and q implies believing p AND q—one arrives at the justified belief that *all* tickets will lose. This contradicts the known fact that one ticket must win [Source: epistemic-paradoxes].

**Implications for Reliabilism:**
Lottery cases suggest that even beliefs formed by highly reliable processes do not constitute knowledge. If an agent forms a belief based on statistical evidence (e.g., reading results in a newspaper or calculating odds), they may have strong justification derived from a generally reliable source. However, as noted regarding lottery propositions, we fail to know them when our only grounds are high probability rather than something that "establishes" their truth [Source: closure-epistemic]. 

Reliabilists like Juan Comesaña argue that reliabilism can handle these problems through probabilistic accounts of reliability [Source: epistemology-latin-america]. Yet, the paradox highlights that mere reliability—defined simply as producing mostly true beliefs—is insufficient if it does not account for modal safety. Knowledge requires believing because of something that safely indicates the truth or rules out relevant alternatives; relying on statistical likelihood in a lottery fails this test because the belief is easily false in nearby possible worlds (where your specific ticket wins) despite being statistically reliable across the population [Source: closure-epistemic][Source: knowledge-value].

### Conclusion
Reliabilists address Gettier cases by demanding that truth be non-accidentally connected to cognitive competence ("correctness due to competence"). However, the lottery paradox demonstrates that even highly reliable processes are inadequate for knowledge when they rely solely on high probability rather than safe indication or conclusive reasons. Thus, reliability must be supplemented with modal constraints like safety or sensitivity to fully account for epistemic luck.

> Reconstruct Kripke's causal-historical theory of reference and explain

### arm A — judge 0.86, missing ['qua-problem'], sources 37 chunks

Saul Kripke’s causal-historical theory of reference, primarily articulated in *Naming and Necessity* (1980), posits that the reference of a proper name is not determined by descriptive content associated with it by speakers, but rather by an objective historical connection to its referent [Source: meaning]. This account was developed as a direct alternative to descriptivist theories, which held that names function like disguised descriptions or clusters of properties [Source: theoretical-terms-science; Source: word-meaning].

### The Core Mechanism: Baptism and Transmission
The standard case for how a name refers involves two distinct stages: **reference fixing** and **reference borrowing**.

1.  **Initial Baptism:** Reference is initially fixed via a "dubbing" event where a language user gives a name to an object, often through ostension (pointing) in perceptual contact with the target [Source: reference; Source: rigid-designators]. Kripke describes this as an inaugurating stipulation—a naming ceremony that tyrannically governs all subsequent use of that specific instance of the name [Source: names]. This act establishes the referent without requiring the dubber to possess any precise knowledge or correct descriptions of the entity's nature [Source: word-meaning].
2.  **Causal-Historical Chain:** Once baptized, the name is passed from speaker to speaker through communicative exchanges. Subsequent speakers succeed in referring to the object not by knowing who it is, but by being appropriately linked to previous users in a chain of communication stretching back to the initial dubbing [Source: reference; Source: natural-kinds]. Speakers "borrow" their reference from those earlier in the chain, provided they intend to use the name as used by the speakers they are picking it up from (i.e., intending to refer to the same individual) [Source: word-meaning].

This model implies that names function like mere tags with no descriptive content or meaning mediating their reference [Source: concepts]. Consequently, even if all associated descriptions turn out to be false (e.g., discovering Aristotle was not Plato’s student), the speaker still refers to Aristotle because the causal-historical link remains intact [Source: theoretical-terms-science].

### Handling Uncertain Initial Baptisms
Kripke acknowledges that complications arise when we cannot identify a single, clear "baptismal ceremony" for widely used names. The theory handles this through two primary mechanisms found in the literature:

**1. Re-use and New Introductions**
When a name is applied to a new entity by an individual who inherits it from a historical tradition but intends to refer to something else entirely, Kripke argues this constitutes a *new* introduction of the name (or at least a new use). For example, if someone calls their cat 'Napoleon' after hearing the name historically associated with the French general, they have introduced a new specific name linked to the cat [Source: reference]. In such cases, there are effectively multiple distinct uses or "homonyms" sharing the same form, each governed by its own causal chain originating from different baptisms [Source: names; Source: reference].

**2. The Role of Intent and Community Reliance**
For ancient figures like Aristotle where no single living witness exists today, the theory relies on the collective intentionality of the linguistic community. Speakers do not need to know *which* specific historical speaker in the past baptized Aristotle; they only need to be part of a continuous communicative network that traces back to those original users [Source: word-meaning]. If speakers intend to refer to whatever entity was originally dubbed 'Aristotle', their use is linked to that initial baptism via the chain of communication, even if intermediate links are lost or uncertain [Source: reference].

However, Kripke admits that distinguishing between "relevant" causal connections (those that fix reference) and "irrelevant" ones has proven difficult to fully theorize [Source: reference]. For instance, merely hearing about Napoleon does not allow one to baptize a new object as 'Napoleon' in the same way; intent plays a crucial role in determining whether a name's transmission preserves its original reference or creates a new one.

### Handling Reference Shifts
The strict version of Kripke’s theory posits that once a name is baptized, no subsequent attribution can alter its reference—each change requires the institution of a *new* specific name with a new inaugural event [Source: names]. Under this view, there is no room for "reference shift" within a single continuous use of a name.

However, later developments and critiques acknowledge scenarios where reference may effectively shift or become ambiguous:

**1. Multiple Bearers and Contextual Selection**
When multiple individuals share the same name (e.g., 'David Cameron'), pure causal chains alone cannot determine which individual is being referred to in a given utterance. Some direct reference theorists have suggested treating such names similarly to indexicals, where context selects an individual from the set of possible referents established by the various baptismal histories associated with that form [Source: reference]. This preserves the causal theory’s core idea—that baptisms fix the *set* of potential referents—while using contextual features to select one specific bearer.

**2. Reference Change via New Baptism**
If a community collectively decides to use a name for something different than its original referent (e.g., re-applying 'phlogiston' or shifting scientific terms), Kripke’s framework interprets this not as a shift in the old name's reference, but as the creation of a new naming event. The old chain remains intact for historical discourse, while a new chain is established for current usage [Source: names]. This avoids the problem of "empty" theoretical terms by treating them as having been baptized with no actual referent from the start, rather than referring and then losing their target [Source: realism-theory-change; Source: thomas-kuhn].

**3. Critiques and Hybrid Models**
Critics note that purely causal accounts struggle to explain how scientists today successfully refer to entities like electrons when they have no direct causal contact with the original baptism (if any) and may hold false beliefs about them. To address this, hybrid models incorporate descriptive elements alongside causal chains, allowing descriptions to play a role in fixing reference for theoretical terms while maintaining that the core reference is anchored historically [Source: theoretical-terms-science; Source: thomas-kuhn]. Additionally, Gareth Evans argued that reference can shift if speakers lose adequate information linking their use back to the initial dubbing, suggesting that mere causal connection is insufficient without an informational link [Source: realism-theory-change].

### Summary
Kripke’s theory resolves the problem of meaning by replacing it with history: names refer because they are linked via a chain of communication to an original act of naming. Uncertain baptisms are handled by emphasizing speaker intent and community-wide chains rather than individual knowledge, while apparent shifts in reference are typically analyzed as either new introductions of names or contextual selections among multiple bearers established by distinct baptismal histories.
