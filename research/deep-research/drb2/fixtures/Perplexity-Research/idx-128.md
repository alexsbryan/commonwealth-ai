# The Impact of Protein Intake on Muscle and Bone Health in Older Adults

## 1. Summary of Existing Recommended Protein Intakes

### International and Supranational Organizations

**World Health Organization (WHO), Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), and United Nations University (UNU)** base their protein recommendation on nitrogen balance studies and have set the requirement at **0.8 g/kg body weight per day** for older adults, which is identical to the recommendation for younger adults. This conservative approach concludes that physiological protein requirements do not increase with age.[2]

**European Food Safety Authority (EFSA)** recommends **0.83 g/kg body weight per day** for healthy adults of all ages, including older adults. Like the WHO/FAO/UNU recommendation, this is derived from nitrogen balance studies and currently available data are considered insufficient to specifically determine protein requirements for older adults that differ from younger adults.[2]

**PROT-AGE Study Group**, an expert consortium focusing on protein and aging, recommends an **average daily intake of 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg body weight per day** for older adults to maintain and regain lean body mass and function. For older adults with acute or chronic disease, they recommend **1.2 to 1.5 g/kg body weight per day**. This recommendation is based on evidence from metabolic and functional parameters rather than nitrogen balance studies alone, reflecting concerns about anabolic resistance in aging.[3]

**European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN)** aligns closely with PROT-AGE, recommending **1.0 to 1.2 g/kg body weight per day** for healthy older people and **1.2 to 1.5 g/kg body weight per day** for those who are malnourished or at risk of malnutrition due to acute or chronic illness.[1]

### National and Regional Recommendations

**Nordic Countries** (Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, and Sweden) updated their protein recommendations in 2012 to **1.2 g/kg body weight per day** and maintained this recommendation in their 2023 guidelines, corresponding to approximately 15-20 E% (energy percentage).[1][115][121]

**Germany, Austria, and Switzerland** (represented by the D-A-CH societies) revised their protein recommendations in 2019 to **1.0 g/kg body weight per day** for adults over 65 years, representing an increase from the previously recommended 0.8 g/kg/day.[1][125]

**Ireland** updated its guidelines in 2021, recommending older adults at risk of frailty, sarcopenia, or undernutrition consume a **minimum of 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg body weight per day**.[1]

**United Kingdom** maintains the Reference Nutrient Intake (RNI) at **0.75 g/kg body weight per day** for older adults, arguing that current evidence is insufficient to justify higher recommendations.[1][114]

**Netherlands** concludes that evidence remains insufficient to increase the recommendation above **0.8 g/kg body weight per day**, maintaining the standard RDA despite evidence from other countries and expert groups.[1]

### Basis for Variation in Recommendations

The differences in recommended values among organizations reflect varying methodological approaches and evidentiary standards. Traditional recommendations based on nitrogen balance studies (WHO, EFSA, UK) yield the 0.8 g/kg/day figure, reflecting the minimum intake to maintain nitrogen equilibrium. However, expert groups including PROT-AGE and ESPEN argue that nitrogen balance methodology may not adequately capture the complex physiological needs of aging populations, particularly regarding maintenance of muscle mass and physical function. These groups emphasize evidence from randomized controlled trials and functional health indicators, demonstrating that intakes of 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day provide superior outcomes for muscle function, strength, and independence in activities of daily living.[98]

## 3. Research on the Impact on Bone Health

Recent research has increasingly focused on the relationship between protein intake and bone health in older adults, moving beyond simple associations to examine the clinical significance of dietary protein for fracture prevention.

**Weaver et al. Study (2021)**

Ashley Weaver and colleagues conducted a prospective analysis of the Health, Aging, and Body Composition (Health ABC) Study, examining 2,160 community-dwelling older adults (mean age 73.5 ± 2.8 years; 51.5% women; 35.8% Black). Their research sought to determine associations between protein intake and bone health across diverse populations.[39]

|---|---|---|---|

### Interpretation of Bone Health Research

The evidence from Weaver et al. and Kemmler et al. demonstrates that protein intake plays a meaningful role in bone health maintenance in older adults. Weaver's epidemiological findings suggest that higher protein intakes (≥15% of total energy intake) are associated with better bone density and protection against vertebral fractures—a clinically significant outcome. The Kemmler study extends this evidence by showing that when protein supplementation is combined with appropriate resistance training, it can not only maintain bone density but also simultaneously improve muscle mass and strength, addressing the dual problem of osteosarcopenia in older men.

The studies support the contention that protein recommendations for older adults may need to exceed traditional levels (0.8 g/kg/day) for optimal skeletal health, particularly when combined with resistance exercise. However, it is important to note that protein alone is insufficient; the combination with calcium, vitamin D, and resistance exercise provides the most comprehensive approach to bone health maintenance.

## 4. The Importance of Protein Quality

### Animal Protein vs. Plant Protein

The quality of dietary protein, not merely the quantity, significantly influences its capacity to support muscle and bone health in older adults. Protein quality is determined by its amino acid composition and digestibility—factors that differ substantially between animal and plant sources.

**Animal Protein Characteristics**

Animal-derived proteins (from meat, poultry, fish, eggs, and dairy products) possess several advantages for older adults. They contain all nine essential amino acids in optimal proportions and exhibit high digestibility, with protein digestibility-corrected amino acid scores (PDCAAS) approaching 1.0. Animal proteins are particularly rich in leucine, an amino acid with special significance for muscle protein synthesis. Furthermore, animal-based protein sources are typically less bulky than plant-based alternatives, making them advantageous for older adults who may experience early satiety or have reduced food intake capacity—a common problem affecting approximately 30% of community-dwelling older adults.[103]

Recent meta-analytic evidence indicates that animal protein produces superior outcomes for muscle mass gain compared to plant protein. One 2025 analysis found that compared to animal protein, plant protein resulted in lower muscle mass following intervention (standardized mean difference = -0.20; 95% CI: -0.37 to -0.03; p = 0.02).[60] Similarly, animal protein was found superior for lower body strength in older adults, though not for upper body strength, suggesting site-specific advantages of animal protein.[68]

**Plant Protein Characteristics**

Plant-based proteins (from legumes, grains, nuts, and seeds) present both advantages and limitations. While promoting numerous health benefits through fiber content and bioactive compounds, plant proteins have reduced digestibility and often incomplete amino acid profiles. Most plant proteins are deficient in one or more essential amino acids—particularly limiting in lysine and methionine, with ranges of 1.4-6.0% and 0.2-2.5% respectively compared to animal sources.[111]

However, 2024 research suggests that plant protein consumption beyond 31 g/day may enhance physical performance and reduce frailty risk in older individuals, indicating potential benefits when adequate quantities are consumed.[57] A key distinction exists between whole plant foods and plant protein isolates; the latter undergo heat treatment that inactivates antinutrients and improves digestibility to levels approaching animal proteins.[99]

### Special Considerations for Plant-Based Diets

**Strategies for Adequate Protein Intake**

When promoting plant-based diets in older adults, several evidence-based strategies are essential:

1. **Complementary protein combination**: Consuming complementary plant proteins throughout the day (legumes with grains, for example) provides complete amino acid profiles when the individual meals lack certain amino acids.

2. **Increased total quantity**: To achieve equivalent amino acid delivery, plant-based diets typically require 10-20% higher total protein intake than animal-based approaches, accounting for lower digestibility and amino acid ratios.

3. **Protein isolation products**: Plant protein isolates (soy, pea, or legume-based) offer improved digestibility and amino acid completeness comparable to animal proteins, making them valuable components of plant-based dietary strategies.

4. **Attention to fiber intake**: While fiber provides health benefits, excessive fiber can reduce mineral bioavailability and protein absorption. Older adults require careful monitoring of fiber intakes when transitioning to plant-based diets to prevent micronutrient malabsorption.

5. **Micronutrient supplementation**: Plant-based diets frequently lack adequate vitamin B12, iron, and potentially vitamin D and calcium without careful planning or supplementation—nutrients particularly important for bone health in older adults.

### The Critical Role of Essential Amino Acids

**Leucine: The Primary Regulator**

Among the 20 amino acids that compose proteins, leucine occupies a uniquely important position in muscle protein metabolism in aging populations. Older adults require approximately 2% more protein per meal than younger individuals to achieve equivalent muscle protein synthesis rates—a phenomenon termed "anabolic resistance."[58] Leucine partially addresses this resistance through multiple mechanisms.

Leucine serves not merely as a substrate for muscle protein synthesis but also as a signaling molecule that activates the mTORC1 complex (mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1). This activation stimulates translation and protein synthesis while simultaneously suppressing proteolysis (protein breakdown). At the molecular level, leucine activates phosphorylation of 4E-BP1 (eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1), facilitating the initiation of protein synthesis.[58]

The branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs)—of which leucine is the most important—are metabolized primarily in skeletal muscle rather than the liver, allowing direct local regulation of muscle protein metabolism. Additionally, leucine stimulates GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide 1) secretion from intestinal L-cells, enhancing glucose-stimulated insulin release and improving glucose disposal in muscle cells, thereby promoting the anabolic state necessary for protein synthesis.[58]

**Valine and Isoleucine**

While less extensively studied than leucine, valine and isoleucine (the other branched-chain amino acids) play complementary roles in muscle metabolism. Together with leucine, these amino acids are preferentially oxidized in muscle tissue and contribute substantially to energy metabolism during protein catabolism. The balance of these three BCAAs influences overall protein turnover kinetics.[64][67]

**Tryptophan: Bone Health and Beyond**

Emerging research has identified tryptophan as a potentially important amino acid for bone health in older adults. Recent 2024 evidence indicates that increased dietary tryptophan intake may be associated with lower risk of lumbar spine bone mineral density decline among individuals aged 50 years and older.[102] The mechanisms likely involve tryptophan's role in immune modulation and its conversion to molecules affecting osteoclast and osteoblast function, though the specific pathways remain incompletely understood.

### Protein Distribution Across Meals

Recent research emphasizes not only total daily protein intake but also the distribution of protein across meals. The anabolic response to protein ingestion in older adults exhibits a "threshold effect"—requiring a minimum of approximately 25-30 grams of high-quality protein per meal to optimally stimulate muscle protein synthesis. This differs from younger individuals who show dose-responsive increases at lower intakes.[129]

Consequently, distributing 1.2 g/kg body weight across three meals yields approximately 10-12 g protein per meal in a typical 60-kg woman—insufficient to trigger maximal anabolism. Many experts recommend ensuring at least one, preferably two, meals containing 30-40 grams of high-quality protein daily to optimize muscle maintenance in older age. This consideration particularly influences dietary planning in the context of plant-based diets, where achieving 30-gram protein portions requires thoughtful food combinations and potentially the inclusion of protein-enriched or fortified products.

### Integration of Protein Quality with Existing Recommendations

The emphasis on protein quality and amino acid composition integrates with the quantitative recommendations from expert groups. The PROT-AGE recommendation of 1.0-1.2 g/kg body weight per day is optimized when that protein consists predominantly of animal sources or high-quality plant proteins with complete amino acid profiles. When animal-based proteins form the foundation of the diet, achieving these targets with good bioavailability is straightforward. However, when transitioning to plant-based diets, the same quantitative targets become more challenging to meet effectively and require deliberate selection of complementary plant proteins, consideration of larger portion sizes, and potentially inclusion of plant protein isolate products to ensure adequate amino acid delivery and optimize muscle and bone health outcomes.
