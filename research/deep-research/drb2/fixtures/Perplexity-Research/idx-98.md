## Educational Activities and Teaching Philosophy

From **1977 to 1981**, Mary Lou Williams served as **Duke University's first artist-in-residence**, a position that represented a significant institutional commitment to jazz education during the final years of her life. She held multiple teaching responsibilities, including directing the **Duke Jazz Ensemble** and co-teaching a **History of Jazz course** alongside **Father Peter F. O'Brien**, who would later compile her recorded History of Jazz album. Her jazz history classes became extraordinarily popular, with students reporting long waiting lists to enroll.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

### Teaching Format and Methodology

Williams' educational approach fundamentally rejected conventional academic structures. Rather than delivering formal lectures using traditional pedagogical methods, she employed what students described as a **story-based, intuitive, and experiential teaching style**. Her instruction centered on **demonstration and observation**, reflecting her belief that learning jazz required physical, embodied knowledge rather than abstract intellectual understanding.[^1]

One of her most distinctive teaching practices involved **intimate physical proximity**. Pianist Sumito Tonooka, one of her Duke students, recalled: "One of the ways she would have me learn was to sit literally right next to her while she played. You're connecting with the physicality of what she's doing; it's not intellectual, it's a physical, dynamic experience. That was how she was taught—sitting on her mother's lap and listening, watching her, being that close. It was powerful because you're not only observing close at hand, you're also feeling the intensity."[^1]

Bill Cunliffe, another Duke student, described her teaching as "very intuitive" and emphasized that "it wasn't academically set up; it was just her telling stories and playing music. It was a lot of hanging out with her and watching her, seeing how she thought about music and hearing great stories about Bud Powell, Erroll Garner, Dizzy." This reflects her core pedagogical belief that students learned best by absorbing music through observation and narrative rather than through formal analysis.[^1]

Williams taught through **demonstration and musical performance**, reinforcing her principle that learning should be rooted in the actual experience of playing and hearing music played authentically. She frequently told her students: "I watch," illustrating her belief that careful observation and absorption of musical practice was essential to understanding jazz.[^5]

Her teaching philosophy emphasized that music revealed the inner self of the musician. She articulated this conviction through the statement: **"Anything you are shows up in your music – jazz is whatever you are, playing yourself, being yourself, letting your thoughts come through."** This philosophy positioned music as a direct manifestation of personal integrity and authenticity, making dedication to craft and honest self-expression fundamental to her educational mission.[^6]

## Jazz Tree Analysis

### Structure and Symbolism

Mary Lou Williams created one of the most influential visual representations of jazz history through the **Jazz Tree diagram**, a collaborative illustration with artist **David Stone Martin**. This diagram served as a pedagogical tool for her lectures and educational demonstrations, becoming a powerful symbol of her historiographical vision.[^7]

The tree's structure operated on multiple symbolic levels:

**The Roots**: The foundation of the entire tree is explicitly labeled **"SUFFERING"**. Williams rooted jazz history in **the African-American experience of suffering**, originating in and continuing from the legacies of slavery and the slave trade. This wasn't merely an abstract label but represented her core conviction that jazz emerged from and perpetually carried the spiritual and emotional weight of African-American historical trauma and resilience.[^8][^9][^10]

**The Trunk**: The main trunk of the tree displays, **in ascending order**, the core developmental lineage of jazz as Williams understood it:[^11][^8]

- **Spirituals** (at the base)
- **Ragtime**
- **Kansas City Swing**
- **Bebop** (at the top)[^9][^8]

This vertical sequence represents Williams' conception of how jazz evolved developmentally, with each stage building upon and emerging from the previous one, creating a coherent historical narrative of continuous evolution.

**The Blues**: Rather than occupying a single position on the developmental timeline, **the blues run alongside the trunk on both sides**, originating in the roots and ascending through all stages. This placement reflects Williams' understanding of the blues as **not merely another era but as an integral and perpetual element** woven throughout all jazz development. The blues provided the emotional and harmonic foundation for every subsequent jazz style, existing simultaneously with and within each developmental stage.[^8][^9]

**The Dead Branches**: On the left side of the tree extend **barren, broken branches** representing what Williams called **"soulless exercises"** and dead ends in jazz development. These branches are labeled with terms representing musical paths she rejected as inauthentic or spiritually empty:[^12]

- **Commercial rock**[^9][^12]
- **Black magic**[^13][^12][^9]
- **Avant-garde** (particularly as it manifested in what she viewed as nihilistic forms)[^13][^12][^9]
- **Cults**[^12]
- **"Exercises"** (implying musical studies divorced from authentic expression)[^9][^12]

These dead branches visually represented Williams' belief that certain musical developments represented departures from jazz's spiritual essence, characterized by commercialization, a lack of genuine feeling, and abandonment of jazz's foundational traditions.

## Jazz Historiography

### Core Philosophy and Origins

Mary Lou Williams constructed a distinctive jazz historiography centered on **suffering as the existential source of the music**. In her recorded "History of Jazz" album (1970), she explained the blues and spirituals through this lens: **"This is the suffering. It's also healing to the soul. Without the..."** This statement encapsulates her belief that suffering provided both the motivational and emotional foundation for jazz, while simultaneously serving a redemptive, healing function.[^14]

Her historiographical vision positioned jazz as **"the only true American art form"**, uniquely rooted in African-American experience and inextricable from African-American history. After her conversion to Catholicism in 1957, she integrated this spiritual framework with her understanding of jazz's origins, seeing the music as inherently **carrying spiritual content** rooted in African-American redemptive traditions. She stated: **"Jazz is important music. It should be played in churches and everywhere, because of the spiritual contents of it."**[^2][^15]

### The Foundational Stages

**Spirituals**: Williams understood spirituals as the deepest root of jazz development. These were not merely musical forms but **expressions of spiritual suffering and transcendence**, created within African-American enslaved and post-enslaved communities as both lament and spiritual affirmation. She traced jazz's ultimate origins to this spiritual tradition, making the spiritual the primary genealogical source of all subsequent jazz development.

**Blues**: The blues held a similarly foundational status in Williams' historiography. She understood the blues as expressing **"the spiritual feeling that arises out of suffering"**, connecting them directly to the African-American experience while acknowledging their distinct articulation of redemptive spirituality. Unlike some jazz historians who treated blues as one style among many, Williams positioned the blues as **the affective and harmonic constant** running through all jazz development, providing the emotional vocabulary that all jazz musicians would draw from.[^16]

**Ragtime**: Williams understood ragtime as representing the **transition from blues and spiritual traditions into more formally organized musical structures**. While ragtime introduced compositional sophistication and rhythmic complexity, Williams maintained that it remained rooted in the blues sensibility and African-American musical traditions from which it emerged.

**Kansas City Swing**: Williams personally participated in developing **Kansas City Swing** during the 1930s as a pianist and arranger for Andy Kirk's orchestra. In her historiography, Kansas City Swing represented the synthesis of blues sensibility with sophisticated big-band orchestration, creating a style that maintained spiritual and blues-based authenticity while achieving greater harmonic and structural complexity. She saw Kansas City Swing as representing jazz at a point of maturation—retaining its spiritual roots while developing greater musical sophistication.[^17]

**Bebop**: Williams viewed bebop as the evolutionary culmination of the main trunk of jazz development. Notably, **she was one of the few swing-era musicians to embrace bebop's innovations**, and she **mentored many of bebop's founding figures**, including Thelonious Monk, Charlie Parker, and Dizzy Gillespie. In her historiography, bebop represented the **logical continuation of jazz's evolution**, not a rupture. Bebop musicians, in her view, maintained connection to jazz's spiritual and blues-based foundations while exploring new harmonic and rhythmic possibilities. Unlike critics who viewed bebop as a radical break with jazz tradition, Williams understood it as the music's natural evolution, provided musicians maintained their connection to foundational traditions.

### What Jazz Is NOT

Williams' historiography was as much defined by what she **rejected** as what she **embraced**. She maintained strong positions against musical developments that she saw as betraying jazz's essential nature:

**Rock Music**: Williams **"hated rock 'n' roll as a commercial evil"**. She distinguished sharply between jazz—rooted in African-American suffering and spiritual traditions—and rock, which she associated with commercial exploitation and spiritual emptiness. This criticism reflected her broader concern about **commercialization that severed music from authentic spiritual and cultural roots**.[^18]

**Avant-Garde Jazz**: While Williams performed with Cecil Taylor and acknowledged that musicians like Taylor and John Coltrane could play "within the tradition" if they chose, she was explicitly **critical of the avant-garde movement** as a whole. In liner notes for her album "Embraced" (the result of her 1977 Carnegie Hall concert with Cecil Taylor), she described the avant-garde as filled with **"hate, bitterness, hysteria, black magic, confusion, discontent, empty studies, musical exercises by various European composers, sounds of the earth, no ears, not even relative pitch"**—though she hastened to add that she was "crazy about African styles in dress".[^19]

Her criticism of the avant-garde centered on what she perceived as its **rejection of jazz's foundational harmonic and tonal traditions** and its abandonment of the **"established patterns"** that connected jazz to its spiritual heritage. She saw the extreme avant-garde as representing a kind of **musical nihilism** that severed connection to the suffering and redemption that gave jazz meaning.

## Evaluations of Specific Styles and Musicians

### Bebop Pioneers and Her Mentorship

Williams' relationship with bebop's founding figures was not merely one of historical observation but of **active mentorship and collaboration**. She worked closely with three central figures in bebop's development:

**Thelonious Monk**: Williams played a crucial pedagogical role in Monk's development. She emphasized teaching him and other young bebop musicians proper **piano technique and the importance of striking the piano with authority**. Mary Lou Williams said of Bud Powell: **"He idolizes Monk"**, indicating her understanding of the hierarchical relationships among these musicians. She recognized that Monk "really used to blow on piano," understanding his innovative approach while working to ground it in proper technical foundation. Her mentorship represented an effort to ensure that bebop innovation remained connected to solid musical fundamentals rather than becoming mere technical display.[^20][^21]

**Bud Powell and Charlie Parker**: Williams was particularly invested in developing Powell's piano technique and artistic confidence. She believed these young musicians were "too good not to have the kind of piano sound they should," and she worked to help them develop the physical and technical mastery necessary to express their musical innovations. Her mentorship of Parker, who was also part of her circle in mid-1940s New York, reflected her broader commitment to ensuring that bebop's revolutionary harmonic ideas remained grounded in authentic expression.[^22]

**Dizzy Gillespie**: Williams mentored Gillespie during the crucial period when bebop was emerging as a coherent movement. Along with Monk and Parker, Gillespie was part of the group of young musicians who visited her apartment, where she fostered an informal "salon atmosphere" for intense musical and intellectual exchange.[^20]

### Specific Musicians and Her Evaluations

**Miles Davis**: Williams recognized Miles Davis as an important figure in bebop and post-bebop development. She included him among "the heavyweights" who sought her counsel, indicating her respect for his musical intelligence and his connection to the bebop tradition.[^23][^24]

**Erroll Garner**: Williams held Garner in high regard as a pianist, frequently mentioning him in interviews alongside other great pianists. She grouped him with figures like Fats Waller, Thelonious Monk, and Bud Powell—all musicians she deeply respected.[^25]

**Duke Ellington**: While Williams acknowledged Ellington's significance, she positioned herself as operating within a continuous tradition that Ellington also represented. Ellington famously described her music as **"perpetually contemporary"**, a phrase that suggested both acknowledged equality and recognition of her innovative impulses.[^26]

### Her General Critical Standard

Williams' evaluations of musicians operated according to a consistent standard: **dedication, authenticity, and connection to jazz's foundational traditions**. She was, according to one contemporary musical assessment, **"intensely self-critical"** and had **"scant regard for musicians who, in her opinion, lack sufficient dedication to their instruments."**[^27]

This critical stance wasn't arbitrary; it reflected her conviction that **music revealed the inner character of the musician**. As one observer noted: Williams expected "great things" of musicians, functioning "in a way, she reminds one of a mother with her children, alternately scolding or praising them, trying to teach them, trying to instill her beliefs in them," yet she was also "a hard taskmistress, demanding and intolerant."[^27]

Her harshest criticisms were reserved for musicians who she perceived as lacking **either technical foundation or spiritual authenticity**. When discussing contemporary musicians who claimed to follow in Coltrane's footsteps, she stated: **"See the guys that they're patterned after like Coltrane and all that, they would never be a Coltrane because they don't know anything about the Basie."** This comment encapsulates her conviction that **jazz innovation required deep knowledge of foundational styles**—in this case, Count Basie's Kansas City Swing—rather than merely imitating a later innovator's surface characteristics.[^28]

### Cecil Taylor and the Avant-Garde Paradox

Williams' decision to perform a dual-piano concert with avant-garde pioneer **Cecil Taylor at Carnegie Hall in 1977** represents an important complexity in her musical philosophy. Despite her public criticism of the avant-garde, she recognized that **Taylor and Coltrane possessed genuine artistic integrity** and could "still, if they wanted to, play 'within the tradition.'"[^19]

This suggests that Williams distinguished between **avant-garde experimentation rooted in authentic artistic vision** (which she could respect even if she didn't embrace it) and **avant-garde as commercial nihilism or technique divorced from feeling** (which she rejected entirely). Her willingness to collaborate with Taylor, despite her reservations about his stylistic approach, reflected a commitment to **reconciliation between different camps of jazz** while maintaining her own aesthetic principles.

### Teaching Philosophy Regarding Musicians

In her Duke teaching, Williams shared stories about musicians like Bud Powell, Erroll Garner, and Dizzy Gillespie to illustrate broader principles about musical authenticity and development. She wasn't simply providing historical information but using musician examples to teach **moral and aesthetic lessons**—that jazz required dedication, that innovation must be grounded in tradition, and that **the quality of one's music directly reflected the quality of one's commitment and character**.

Her pedagogical use of musician examples positioned jazz history not as a sequence of detached facts but as a **narrative of human commitment, spiritual struggle, and artistic integrity** pursued against significant obstacles. By frequently discussing the "great stories" of musicians' development and artistic choices, she transformed jazz history into a teaching tool for broader lessons about artistic dedication and authentic expression.

Mary Lou Williams' educational work from 1977 to 1981 represented the culmination of her distinctive approach to jazz history and pedagogy. Her teaching at Duke University, her Jazz Tree diagram, her recorded History of Jazz, and her mentorship of individual musicians all articulated a coherent vision of jazz as **music rooted in African-American suffering and spiritual tradition**, evolving through specific historical stages while maintaining connection to its foundational sources. She valued musicians who demonstrated **dedication, technical mastery, and authentic spiritual expression**, while rejecting commercialization, technical display divorced from feeling, and innovations that severed connection to jazz's heritage. Her legacy as an educator extended beyond technical instruction to encompass a moral and spiritual vision of what jazz could and should be—a medium of authentic human expression grounded in African-American history and serving redemptive spiritual purposes.[^3][^15][^2][^16][^28][^6][^8][^19][^27][^1]
