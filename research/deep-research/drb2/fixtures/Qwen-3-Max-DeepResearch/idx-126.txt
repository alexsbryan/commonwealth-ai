Between 24-Hour Movement Guideline
Adherence and Overweight/Obesity in Children
and Adolescents
Overview of Scientific Findings on Correlation and Risk
The scientific evidence available as of May 2023 presents a robust and consistent conclusion
regarding the relationship between adherence to the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines (24HMG) and
the risk of overweight and obesity in children and adolescents. These guidelines, which integrate
recommendations for physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep into a single framework,
represent a paradigm shift from traditional public health messaging by considering the entire 24-hour
    13
day . The body of research overwhelmingly indicates a significant inverse correlation; higher
adherence to these integrated guidelines is consistently associated with a lower prevalence of
                                                                             68
overweight and obesity and more favorable body composition indicators . The vast majority of
included studies have identified a statistically significant positive association, while none of the
provided sources reported a study finding no correlation or a positive correlation where greater
adherence was linked to a higher risk of obesity. One study noted the absence of a significant
correlation between simultaneous adherence to all three guidelines and obesity indicators, but it
simultaneously found strong associations for individual behaviors or partial combinations,
                                                                5
reinforcing the overarching theme of a beneficial relationship .

A systematic review and meta-analysis encompassing 63 studies with over 387,000 participants
confirmed that overall adherence to all three components of the guidelines—moderate-to-vigorous
physical activity (MVPA), limited screen time, and adequate sleep duration—is associated with lower
                                                                                                  79
BMI z-scores, reduced waist circumference, and decreased odds of being overweight or obese .
This meta-analysis established a dose-response gradient, where meeting fewer guidelines was
                                                                         8
associated with progressively higher odds of unfavorable weight status . For instance, a U.S.-based
cross-sectional study using nationally representative Youth Risk Behaviour Survey data found that
adolescents who met zero, one, or two of the three guidelines had significantly higher odds of having
an unfavorable weight status compared to those who met all three (Odds Ratios of 1.38, 1.42, and
                   8
1.18, respectively) . Similarly, a multinational study involving nearly 7,000 children aged 9-11 years
demonstrated that children who met all three recommendations were 70% less likely to suffer from
                                                         14
obesity than those who met none (Odds Ratio of 0.28) . Another large-scale Chinese study
involving over 34,000 children and adolescents also found that meeting none of the guidelines was
significantly associated with a 26% higher risk of being overweight or obese (adjusted Odds Ratio of
         4
1.26) . These findings are further corroborated by other national surveys in China, which reported
similar associations, showing that meeting none, only MVPA, or only screen time was significantly
                                               34
linked to a higher risk of overweight/obesity .

The strength of this correlation varies across different populations and study designs, but the
directionality remains remarkably consistent. In a longitudinal study following adolescents from the
National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health (Add Health) cohort into adulthood, meeting all
three guidelines during adolescence was associated with a 24% lower risk of developing abdominal
                                                    2 12
obesity later in life (Incidence Rate Ratio of 0.76) . While this same study did not find a significant
association with general obesity defined by a BMI of 30 kg/m² or higher, the powerful protective
effect against abdominal obesity is a critical finding, as central adiposity is a key predictor of long-
                              2 12
term cardiometabolic disease . Furthermore, a Finnish longitudinal study observed that meeting
guidelines was longitudinally associated with a lower waist circumference, although this association
became statistically non-significant after adjusting for body fat percentage, suggesting that body
                                                                                      17
composition is a primary mediator of the guidelines' impact on health outcomes . The Canadian 24-
Hour Movement Guidelines themselves were developed through a rigorous process that included
four systematic reviews, and the fourth review concluded that profiles characterized by high physical
                                                                                                          13
activity, high sleep, and low sedentary behavior generally had more desirable measures of adiposity .
This foundational evidence supports the global adoption of these integrated guidelines by countries
                                                                                           18
including Australia, South Africa, New Zealand, and parts of the Asia-Pacific region .

The specific conclusions drawn from studies that found a correlation are multifaceted, highlighting
not just the overall benefit but also the importance of each individual component within the
integrated framework. The core conclusion is that the health benefits appear to be cumulative. Each
additional recommendation met contributes to a healthier weight profile. A study in Ontario, Canada,
examining life stress and self-esteem among students, found a clear dose-response gradient: meeting
more recommendations was significantly associated with better mental health outcomes, reinforcing
                                                               15
the idea that a holistic approach yields superior results . However, some research suggests that the
integrated model may not always capture the full picture perfectly. A Brazilian study of adolescents
aged 14-19 found no significant correlation between simultaneous adherence to all three guidelines
                                                           5
and obesity indicators (BMI and body fat percentage) . Yet, this same study found important sex-
specific associations when looking at partial combinations, such as a negative association between
physical activity adherence and body fat in boys, indicating that even without full compliance, certain
                                                                         5
behaviors can still exert a powerful influence on body composition . This nuance is crucial; while
meeting all three is ideal, interventions targeting any single component are likely to yield positive
health benefits. The evidence strongly suggests that efforts to combat childhood and adolescent
obesity must move beyond promoting isolated activities like sports and instead embrace a
comprehensive strategy that addresses the totality of a young person's daily movement patterns.

Summary of Key Studies on 24-Hour Movement Guidelines and
Weight Status
An examination of key studies provides granular detail on how adherence to the 24-Hour Movement
Guidelines correlates with weight status across diverse populations and geographic locations. These
studies, ranging from large-scale national surveys to focused longitudinal cohorts, collectively build a
compelling case for the guidelines' relevance. The first major insight comes from a series of large-
scale studies conducted in China, which highlight both the severity of the issue and the power of the
guidelines. A 2021 cross-sectional study analyzing data from the Physical Activity and Fitness in
China—The Youth Study (PAFCTYS) involved over 114,000 children and adolescents with a mean
                   3
age of 13.75 years . The researchers found that only 5.12% of participants met all three components
of the guidelines. Critically, they discovered that meeting all three was associated with significantly
                                           3
lower odds of being overweight or obese . Specifically, compared to those who met all three,
individuals who met none of the guidelines had 25% higher odds of being overweight or obese
(Odds Ratio [OR] = 1.25). This study also revealed a dose-response effect, where each additional
                                                                                                     16
recommended behavior met was associated with a 12% lower odds of being overweight or obese .
A separate 2019 national cross-sectional study in China, involving over 34,000 children and
adolescents aged 6–17, yielded similar findings. Only 2.1% met all three guidelines, and meeting
none, only MVPA, or only screen time was significantly associated with a higher risk of overweight/
                                                                         4
obesity, with adjusted Odds Ratios of 1.26, 1.33, and 1.35, respectively . Both Chinese studies
underscore the extremely low level of guideline adherence and its direct, measurable link to
population-level weight status.

In North America, multiple studies reinforce these conclusions. A 2023 cross-sectional study using
data from the Youth Risk Behaviour Survey (YRBS) analyzed over 40,000 U.S. adolescents aged
         8
14–17 . This study found that only 4.88% of participants met all three guidelines, while 12.35%
met none. The core finding was a clear dose-response relationship: meeting fewer guidelines was
                                                              8
associated with higher odds of an unfavorable weight status . Adolescents who met zero guidelines
had 38% higher odds of being overweight or obese compared to those who met all three (OR =
     8
1.38) . The study also noted that the association was stronger in boys than girls and particularly
                                                                                                         8
evident among younger adolescents (ages 14–15) and White and Hispanic/Latino participants .
Another landmark study, leveraging the Add Health cohort, provides powerful longitudinal evidence
from the United States. This study followed adolescents aged approximately 15 years at baseline into
                                  2 12
early adulthood (ages 24–32) . It found that meeting all three guidelines during adolescence was
associated with a 24% lower risk of developing abdominal obesity in adulthood (Incidence Rate
                       12
Ratio [IRR] = 0.76) . This demonstrates that healthy movement behaviors during the teen years
                                                                  2 12
have lasting consequences for metabolic health decades later . Even meeting screen time
recommendations alone during adolescence was independently associated with a lower risk of
                                               12
abdominal obesity in adulthood (IRR = 0.84) .

The international scope of the research further solidifies these findings. The International Study of
Childhood Obesity, Lifestyle and the Environment (ISCOLE) is a prominent example, providing
data from over 6,000 preadolescent children (9-11 years) across 12 socio-culturally and economically
                  14
diverse countries . The ISCOLE study found that children who met all three recommendations
                                                                                               14
were 70% less likely to suffer from obesity compared to those who met none (OR = 0.28) . This
study also highlighted that low MVPA and high sedentary time were both independently associated
                                                                                                    14
with higher odds of obesity, and a combination of both was associated with even greater risk . In
Europe, a Finnish longitudinal study (the PANIC study) followed children aged 6–8 years and
found that meeting physical activity guidelines, either individually or in combination with others, was
                                                                                          17
longitudinally associated with lower cardiometabolic risk score and waist circumference . Although
these associations became non-significant after adjusting for body fat percentage, the initial findings
support the broader conclusion that favorable movement profiles are linked to better health
             17
outcomes . Conversely, a 2023 cross-sectional study of Brazilian adolescents (14-19 years) presented
a more nuanced result, finding no significant correlation between simultaneous adherence to all three
                                                                  5
guidelines and obesity indicators (BMI, body fat percentage) . However, this study did identify
important sex-specific relationships, such as a negative association between physical activity
adherence and body fat in boys, and several other associations for partial combinations of guidelines
5
 . This highlights that while full compliance is optimal, partial adherence can still be beneficial and
may manifest differently depending on demographic factors like sex. Finally, a Slovakian intervention
study, while not directly testing the 24HMG, provides evidence on the impact of increasing MVPA.
A 24-month after-school physical activity program resulted in significantly smaller increases in BMI,
BMI z-score, body fat percentage, and fat mass index in normal-weight 6- to 7-year-old boys
compared to a control group, demonstrating the potential for targeted interventions to positively
                             11
influence body composition .
Quantitative Assessment of Associations with Body Composition
Indicators
Beyond binary classifications of overweight and obesity, a substantial body of research provides
quantitative assessments of how adherence to the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines relates to
continuous body composition indicators. These metrics—including BMI z-score, waist
circumference, body fat percentage, and fat mass index (FMI)—offer a more granular understanding
of the guidelines' impact on adiposity. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have been instrumental
in synthesizing these quantitative findings, allowing for robust statistical comparisons across
numerous studies. A key meta-analysis focusing on the relative time spent in different movement
behaviors found that spending more time sleeping and engaged in moderate-to-vigorous physical
                                                                              7 10
activity (MVPA) was consistently associated with lower levels of adiposity . Specifically, reallocating
time to MVPA from any other behavior was shown to be favorably associated with health outcomes
6
    . The meta-analysis calculated that spending 60 more minutes per day in MVPA was associated with
                                                                          7
a 0.27 unit lower BMI z-score, an effect considered clinically meaningful . Similarly, spending 4
hours more in sleep relative to other behaviors was associated with a similarly favorable change in
                7
BMI z-score . These findings provide concrete evidence that replacing sedentary time with MVPA is
a highly effective strategy for improving body composition.

The inverse relationship between MVPA and adiposity is one of the most robustly documented
associations in the literature. The meta-analysis found that time spent in MVPA relative to other
                                                                                             7
behaviors was significantly and inversely associated with BMI z-score (z = -2.39, p = 0.017) . This
association remained strong even after accounting for heterogeneity among studies (I² = 89.7%),
                                                                                      7
indicating a consistent effect across different populations and measurement methods . However, the
same analysis found that MVPA was not significantly associated with body fat percentage or FMI,
                                                                                                        7
suggesting that its primary impact may be on overall body mass rather than specifically on fat mass .
In contrast, sedentary time showed a positive association with adiposity. Spending more time
sedentary relative to other behaviors was positively associated with BMI z-score (z = 2.87, p = 0.004)
                                               7
and body fat percentage (z = 2.26, p = 0.024) . Light-intensity physical activity (LPA) also appeared
to contribute to higher adiposity, with LPA relative to other behaviors being associated with higher
                                                            7
BMI z-score, waist circumference, and body fat percentage . This suggests that simply reducing
sedentary time may not be enough; actively engaging in MVPA is a more potent strategy for
combating excess adiposity. A cross-sectional study in China involving over 4,900 primary school
children aged 6–12 found that higher adherence to the guidelines was significantly associated with
                                                    16
lower BMI z-scores and lower body fat percentage . The strongest individual association was
observed for physical activity, which alone was linked to significantly lower BMI z-scores and body
                    16
fat percentage .

Waist circumference, a key indicator of central adiposity and cardiometabolic risk, has also been
shown to be strongly influenced by movement behaviors. A meta-analysis confirmed that time spent
sleeping relative to other behaviors was inversely associated with waist circumference (z = -2.51, p =
         7                                                                                          7
0.012) . Sedentary time was positively associated with waist circumference (z = 2.87, p = 0.004) .
The longitudinal Add Health study provided some of the most compelling evidence, finding that
adolescents who met all three guidelines had a β = -1.92 cm reduction in waist circumference 14
                        2 12
years later in adulthood . Meeting screen time recommendations alone was associated with a β =
-1.62 cm reduction, and meeting screen time combined with physical activity was associated with a β
= -2.25 cm reduction, demonstrating the powerful long-term effects of these behaviors on
                               2 12
abdominal fat distribution            . In the Finnish PANIC study, meeting guidelines was longitudinally
                                                                            17
associated with lower waist circumference at a 2-year follow-up . However, this association lost its
statistical significance after adjustment for body fat percentage, again pointing to body composition
                                       17
as a key intermediary pathway . A study in Slovakia on a physical activity intervention found that
boys in the intervention group had a significantly smaller rise in body fat percentage and fat mass
index over 24 months compared to controls, further supporting the role of MVPA in preventing the
                           11
accumulation of fat mass . Collectively, these quantitative findings demonstrate that the 24-Hour
Movement Guidelines provide a powerful framework for influencing a range of anthropometric and
body composition indicators, with MVPA, limited sedentary time, and adequate sleep all playing
distinct and critical roles.

Longitudinal Evidence on the Lifelong Impact of Early Adherence
While many studies establish a strong cross-sectional link between adherence to the 24-Hour
Movement Guidelines and favorable weight status, longitudinal research provides the most
compelling evidence for causality and the long-term health implications of these behaviors. The few
existing longitudinal studies are therefore critically important, as they reveal how movement patterns
during childhood and adolescence shape health trajectories well into adulthood. The most extensive
and impactful of these is the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health (Add Health) cohort
                                 2 12
study in the United States              . This landmark research followed a large sample of adolescents from
                                                                                                2 12
grades 7-12 (mean age ~15 years) through to early adulthood (ages 24-32) . The findings from this
study are profound, demonstrating that the habits formed during the teenage years have a durable
and protective effect against abdominal obesity later in life. Specifically, adolescents who met the
screen time recommendations alone, in combination with physical activity, or with all three
                                                                                                            2 12
guidelines (including sleep) had significantly lower waist circumferences 14 years later . The
magnitude of this effect is clinically significant: meeting all three guidelines during adolescence was
associated with a beta coefficient of -1.92 cm, meaning a 1.92 cm reduction in waist circumference in
           2 12
adulthood . This underscores that the integrated approach of managing all three behaviors is far
more effective than focusing on any single component in isolation.

Beyond just waist circumference, the Add Health study also quantified the long-term risk of
developing obesity. Meeting all three 24-hour movement guidelines during adolescence was
associated with a 24% lower risk of developing abdominal obesity in adulthood (Incidence Rate
                      2 12
Ratio [IRR] = 0.76)          . This finding is particularly noteworthy because it persisted even after
                                                                                                                      12
adjusting for a wide range of covariates, including sex, race/ethnicity, region, and parental income .
The study further broke down the contributions of individual components, finding that meeting
screen time recommendations alone was associated with a lower risk of abdominal obesity in
adulthood (IRR = 0.84), and meeting both screen time and physical activity recommendations
                                                                            2 12
together yielded an even stronger protective effect (IRR = 0.86)                   . This highlights the unique and
independent role of limiting recreational screen time, a behavior often overlooked in traditional
health promotion. Interestingly, the same study did not find a significant association between
                                                                                           2 12
adherence to the guidelines and general obesity defined by a BMI of 30 kg/m² or higher . This
discrepancy suggests that the guidelines may be particularly effective at protecting against the
accumulation of visceral fat (which drives abdominal obesity) rather than subcutaneous fat, or that
the influence of adolescent behaviors on BMI becomes less pronounced over longer periods due to
confounding factors like aging and lifestyle changes. Nonetheless, the protective effect against central
adiposity is a powerful argument for promoting these guidelines during adolescence.

Other longitudinal studies, though perhaps shorter in duration, add to this growing body of evidence.
The Finnish Prospective Investigation of the Nutrition and Cardiometabolic Health in Children
                                                                  17
(PANIC) study followed children aged 6–8 years for two years . The study found that meeting
physical activity guidelines, either alone or in combination with others, was longitudinally associated
                                                                                                  17
with lower cardiometabolic risk scores and lower waist circumference at the 2-year follow-up .
While these associations weakened after adjusting for body fat percentage, the initial findings support
                                                                                                       17
the conclusion that favorable movement behaviors are linked to better health outcomes over time .
The study measured body fat percentage via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), providing a
                                               17
high-fidelity assessment of body composition . Another longitudinal study by Chemtob et al. found
that children who did not meet any of the guidelines had a BMI z-score that was 1.66 standard
                                                                                     8
deviations higher in early adolescence compared to those who met all components . This provides
another piece of evidence linking poor movement behaviors in youth to poorer weight outcomes
later on. The tracking of behavioral phenotypes from childhood into adolescence and adulthood
                                                    14
further strengthens the case for early intervention . Behaviors like low physical activity and high
sedentary behavior tend to persist over time, carrying with them an increased risk for obesity and
                                      14
related comorbidities throughout life . Therefore, the long-term perspective provided by these
longitudinal studies is essential for framing the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines not merely as a set of
recommendations for the present, but as a blueprint for building lifelong health and preventing
chronic disease. The evidence clearly indicates that investing in healthy movement patterns during
the formative years of childhood and adolescence yields significant returns in terms of improved
body composition and reduced risk of obesity-related conditions in adulthood.


Component-Specific Contributions to Obesity Risk
While the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines are designed as an integrated framework, a deeper analysis
of the research reveals the distinct and powerful contributions of each individual component—
physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep—to the risk of overweight and obesity.
Understanding these component-specific effects is crucial for designing targeted and effective public
health interventions. The evidence strongly suggests that no single behavior operates in isolation;
instead, they exist in a dynamic interplay where behaviors in one domain can displace or influence
behaviors in another. For instance, excessive recreational screen time can lead to displacement of
                                                                               14
both physical activity and sleep, creating a cascade of negative health effects . The research
consistently shows that moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) is a cornerstone of healthy
body composition. Multiple studies have demonstrated a strong inverse association between MVPA
                           7 16
and adiposity indicators          . A meta-analysis found that time spent in MVPA relative to other
                                                                                                             7
behaviors was significantly and inversely associated with BMI z-score (z = -2.39, p = 0.017) . The
ISCOLE study found that a low level of MVPA was associated with higher odds of obesity (OR
     14
0.49) . In a cross-sectional study of Chinese primary school children, physical activity was the
                                                                                           16
strongest individual correlate of lower BMI z-score and body fat percentage . Furthermore, a 10-
minute increase in MVPA was found to be inversely associated with the odds of metabolic syndrome
                                                   14
(OR 0.88), highlighting its systemic benefits . The mechanisms are multifaceted, involving energy
expenditure, improved insulin sensitivity, and potentially better dietary choices, as children with high
                                                                                     14
MVPA levels are more likely to consume more fruits and vegetables .

Sedentary behavior, particularly recreational screen time, has emerged as a particularly insidious risk
factor for adiposity, independent of physical activity levels. The evidence linking screen time to
obesity is robust and supported by multiple lines of inquiry. The Add Health study found that
meeting screen time recommendations alone during adolescence was independently associated with a
                                                                    2 12
lower risk of abdominal obesity in adulthood (IRR = 0.84)                  . This effect was so strong that it was
                                                                                                                  2 12
comparable to the effect seen when combining screen time with physical activity (IRR = 0.86) .
This suggests that limiting screen time is a powerful standalone strategy. The mechanisms behind this
association are complex. Screen time can displace sleep, as blue light exposure from screens can
                                                                   14
interfere with melatonin production and delay sleep onset . It can also lead to increased food intake
                                                                                                             14
through distraction, encouraging mindless eating while watching television or using devices . The
meta-analysis confirmed that time spent sedentary relative to other behaviors was positively
                                                                                                                         7
associated with BMI z-score (z = 2.87, p = 0.004) and body fat percentage (z = 2.26, p = 0.024) .
The ISCOLE study also found that high sedentary time was associated with higher odds of obesity
           14
(OR 1.19) . Given that sedentary time increases with age, from about 6 hours per day at age 6 to
                                                                                                14
nearly 9 hours at age 14, addressing this trend is critical for obesity prevention .

Sleep duration stands as the third pillar of the 24-Hour Movement Framework, and its contribution
to metabolic health is increasingly recognized as vital. The meta-analysis found that time spent
sleeping relative to other behaviors was inversely associated with both BMI z-score (z = -2.30, p =
                                                              7
0.021) and waist circumference (z = -2.51, p = 0.012) . This indicates that ensuring adequate sleep is
a key factor in maintaining a healthy weight. Sleep deprivation can disrupt hormonal regulation of
appetite, leading to increased levels of ghrelin (the hunger hormone) and decreased levels of leptin
                                                                                14
(the satiety hormone), which can drive overeating and weight gain . The ISCOLE study noted that
children with overweight or obesity spend less time in MVPA compared to their healthy-weight
                                                              14
peers, and this is often coupled with insufficient sleep . A cross-sectional study in China involving
over 4,900 children found that meeting the sleep duration recommendation was one of the three key
                                                                        16
behaviors associated with lower odds of overweight/obesity . The integrative nature of the
guidelines is therefore paramount. Compositional analyses show that profiles characterized by high
physical activity, high sleep, and low sedentary behavior are generally associated with more desirable
                                                         13
measures of adiposity and cardiometabolic health . The optimal health benefits may be achieved by
                                                                                                        13
strategically reallocating time—for example, by replacing sedentary behavior with MVPA . This
integrated perspective is crucial for public health messaging, moving beyond simplistic advice like
"exercise more" to a more holistic message about balancing all aspects of a child's 24-hour day to
support healthy growth and development.


Global Patterns of Adherence and Implications for Public Health
The scientific evidence surrounding the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines is complemented by
extensive research on global adherence rates, which paints a concerning picture of widespread non-
compliance among children and adolescents. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 63 studies
involving over 387,000 participants from 23 countries found that globally, only 7.12% of children
                                                                           1 21 22
and adolescents aged 3–18 met all three components of the guidelines       . Shockingly, 19.21% of
participants met none of the recommendations, indicating a significant portion of the youth
                                                                                        1 21
population is not adhering to any of the fundamental pillars of a healthy day . During the
COVID-19 pandemic, adherence rates dropped even further, falling below 5.0% in some reports,
                                                                                               6
highlighting how disruptions to routine can negatively impact these crucial behaviors . This
extremely low level of compliance represents a major public health challenge and underscores the
urgency of implementing effective interventions.

Adherence to the guidelines is not uniform across the globe and exhibits significant variations by age,
sex, and geography. A clear pattern emerges where adherence declines sharply with age. Among the
total sample, preschoolers had the highest adherence rate at 11.26%, followed by children at 10.31%,
                                                          1 6 22
and then plummeting to just 2.68% among adolescents . This dramatic drop-off during the
teenage years is a critical finding, suggesting that adolescence may represent a window of
vulnerability where unhealthy movement patterns become entrenched. Gender differences are also
apparent, with girls showing significantly lower adherence rates than boys across multiple studies
                             1 21 22
(3.75% vs. 6.89% overall)     . Geographically, there are stark disparities. South America had the
lowest adherence to all three guidelines (2.93% meeting all) and the highest proportion of youth
                         1 21 22
meeting none (31.72%)   . In contrast, higher adherence was found to be positively correlated with
a country's Human Development Index (HDI), suggesting that socioeconomic factors, urbanization,
                                                                               1 6 21
and cultural norms play a significant role in shaping movement behaviors . Countries with higher
HDI, such as those in North America and Oceania, generally show better adherence compared to
                                       6
regions like South America and Asia .

These global patterns have profound implications for public health policy and practice. The near-
universal failure to meet the guidelines indicates that current strategies are insufficient. Interventions
must be multi-pronged, addressing not just schools but also families, communities, and policymakers.
The sharp decline in adherence during adolescence calls for targeted programs aimed at this specific
age group to help them navigate the challenges of academic pressure, social life, and technology use
while maintaining healthy routines. The gender gap in adherence suggests that interventions may
need to be tailored to address the specific barriers and motivations of girls. The strong correlation
between HDI and adherence points to the need for policies that address structural inequities. This
could include creating safe, accessible parks and recreational facilities in underserved communities,
implementing regulations on screen time for children, and promoting family-friendly work policies
that allow parents to engage in physical activities with their children and maintain consistent bedtime
routines. Furthermore, the lack of valid and reliable tools for assessing integrated 24-hour movement
                                                        6
behaviors remains a significant methodological hurdle . Developing and disseminating standardized
protocols, possibly integrating objective measures like accelerometers with validated questionnaires,
is essential for accurately monitoring progress and evaluating the effectiveness of interventions. In
conclusion, the scientific evidence as of May 2023 provides an unambiguous mandate: the 24-Hour
Movement Guidelines are a scientifically sound framework for preventing overweight and obesity in
children and adolescents. The consistent, inverse correlation between adherence and adiposity, the
powerful longitudinal evidence of long-term benefits, and the alarming global rates of non-adherence
all point toward the urgent need for a collective, society-wide effort to translate these guidelines into
everyday reality for every young person.
