The Impact of Protein Intake on Muscle and
Bone Health in Older Adults
Summary of Existing Recommended Protein Intakes
The landscape of dietary protein recommendations for older adults has undergone a significant
evolution, reflecting a paradigm shift from biochemical equilibrium to physiological function. The
central debate revolves around the adequacy of the traditional Recommended Dietary Allowance
(RDA) of 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day (g/kg/day), a value established
decades ago by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Food and Agriculture Organization
       1 17
(FAO) . This foundational recommendation was derived primarily from meta-analyses of nitrogen
balance studies conducted in the early 2000s, which aimed to establish the minimum amount of
                                                                            13
protein required to maintain nitrogen equilibrium in healthy young adults . However, subsequent
reanalyses have revealed a critical limitation: these studies included a very small number of older
participants, with only 54 individuals aged 60 years or older in the most recent analysis, leading to
insufficient statistical power to differentiate protein requirements between younger and older
              1 13
populations . Consequently, while the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) formalized this
figure as a Population Reference Intake (PRI) of 0.83 g/kg/day for all adults in 2012, its relevance to
                                                                                 1 18
the specific needs of an aging demographic has been increasingly questioned . This methodological
critique forms the bedrock of the argument for higher protein intakes, which are now being
advocated by numerous expert groups and national bodies based on a growing body of evidence
linking protein consumption to the maintenance of lean body mass and physical function.

In stark contrast to the conservative EFSA recommendation, a broad consensus among several
authoritative international and regional bodies supports significantly higher protein intakes for older
adults. The PROT-AGE Study Group, convened by the European Union Geriatric Medicine Society
(EUGMS), stands out as a pioneering voice in this movement. In their 2013 recommendations, they
advocate for an average daily protein intake of at least 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older
                                                                                          14 15 16
individuals (>65 years) specifically to maintain and regain lean body mass and function     . Their
rationale is rooted in the understanding of age-related changes in protein metabolism, including
                                                                                                     14 15
reduced anabolic responses to protein ingestion and high splanchnic extraction of amino acids .
Recognizing that illness increases metabolic demand, the group further recommends higher intakes
of 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day for older adults experiencing acute or chronic disease, and even up to 2.0 g/
                                                               16 17
kg/day in cases of severe illness or marked malnutrition . Similarly, the European Society for
Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) aligns closely with these recommendations, advising at
least 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older people and 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day for those who are
                                                        1 13
malnourished or at risk due to acute or chronic illness . These guidelines emphasize clinical
nutrition support and are explicitly focused on preserving muscle health, representing a departure
                                                       13
from the more general approach of the earlier RDAs .
This trend towards higher protein recommendations is also evident at the national level, though not
universally adopted. The Nordic countries represent one of the earliest adopters of elevated
guidelines, having updated their protein recommendation to 1.2 g/kg/day in 2012 and reaffirming
this level in their 2023 Nordic Nutrition Recommendations (NNR 2023) for food planning purposes
                                                      1 18 19
to prevent declines in physical functioning    . This position is supported by evidence suggesting
that protein intakes in the range of 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day may be optimal for older adults, especially
given age-related decreases in energy intake that can make it difficult to meet absolute protein needs
                                                 20
within a lower percentage of total energy . In direct opposition, some German-speaking countries—
Germany, Austria, and Switzerland—revised their national guideline in 2019 to recommend a lower
                                                                      1 13
intake of 1.0 g/kg/day for adults over 65 years old . Meanwhile, other nations like the United
Kingdom and the Netherlands continue to adhere to the official RDA of ≥0.8 g/kg/day, citing a
                                                                 13
lack of sufficient evidence to warrant an increase . This divergence highlights the ongoing scientific
and political debate about the threshold of evidence required to revise long-standing dietary
guidelines. A notable example of this nuance is seen in Ireland, where the Food Safety Authority
advised in 2021 that older adults at risk of frailty, sarcopenia, or undernutrition should consume a
minimum of 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day, effectively tailoring the recommendation to a vulnerable subgroup
                                      1 13
rather than applying it universally          .

The recommendations related to osteoporosis management further illustrate this evolving
perspective. The International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF) and the European Society for Clinical
and Economic Aspects of Osteoporosis and Osteoarthritis (ESCEO) initially recommended 1.0 g/
kg/day of protein for osteoporosis management in 2013, but this specific value was removed in their
                                                                                                    1 13
2019 update, replaced with a more general advisory to consume 'sufficient dietary protein' . More
recently, in 2023, the French Rheumatology Society and the Osteoporosis Research and Information
Group issued the first specific dietary recommendations for osteoporosis, advising at least 1.0–1.2
g/kg/day of high-quality animal protein as part of a balanced diet containing adequate calories,
                         1 13
calcium, and vitamin D . This represents a sophisticated integration of protein quantity and quality
within a broader nutritional framework for bone health. Conversely, some reviews have concluded
that increasing protein intake above 0.8 g/kg/day provides insufficient evidence for health benefits
                                                                                                           13 20
in terms of muscle strength, physical performance, or bone health without concurrent exercise . A
2022 systematic review by the Health Council of the Netherlands, for instance, found that while
increased protein combined with exercise had a possible beneficial effect on lean body mass, protein
                                                          13
alone likely had no effect on these outcomes . However, this review notably excluded frail,
hospitalized, or care-home residents, limiting its applicability to more robust community-dwelling
             1 13
older adults . The following table summarizes the key differences in recommended protein intakes
across various organizations and countries.

Research on the Impact on Muscle Health
A substantial body of evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrates that
increasing protein intake, particularly when combined with physical activity, plays a crucial role in
mitigating age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia). The findings consistently indicate that while protein
is a necessary component for muscle maintenance, it is not sufficient on its own for significant
improvements in muscle strength and physical function. Instead, a synergistic effect emerges when
protein supplementation is paired with resistance exercise training, leading to tangible gains in lean
body mass, muscle strength, and overall physical performance. This conclusion is strongly supported
by a series of well-regarded studies, including the ProMuscle trials, the PROMISS trial, and the
CALM trial, which collectively provide a comprehensive picture of the impact of protein on muscle
health in older adults.

The ProMuscle series of studies, conducted in the Netherlands, provides a clear hierarchy of effects
based on the combination of protein and exercise interventions. The original ProMuscle trial (2012)
involved four arms: milk protein concentrate (31g/day) and/or resistance exercise training for 24
       1
weeks . The results showed that increased lean body mass was observed only in the group receiving
                               1
both protein and exercise . Interestingly, improvements in muscle strength and physical
performance were seen in both the protein-only and exercise-only groups, suggesting that protein
may offer a modest benefit for performance even in the absence of exercise, but that exercise is a
                                   1 13
critical driver for strength gains . Building on this, the ProMuscle in Practice (PiP) trial (2020)
investigated the effect of increasing protein intake to 25g per main meal in conjunction with twice-
                                                                  5 10
weekly supervised progressive resistance exercise over 12 weeks . This intervention resulted in
significant improvements in lean body mass, muscle strength, and the Short Physical Performance
                                                                             56
Battery (SPPB) score compared to a control group receiving usual care . Subgroup analyses from
PiP revealed interesting nuances; participants aged 75 years or younger experienced significantly
greater gains in lean body mass compared to those older than 75, indicating an age-related difference
                       5
in muscle response . Furthermore, women demonstrated significantly greater improvement in leg
press strength compared to men, despite similar adherence rates, highlighting potential sex-based
                           5
differences in response . The ProMuscle Implementation Pilots (IP) further explored the real-world
effectiveness of tailored nutrition advice aiming for 20–25g of protein per main meal, combined
with resistance exercise. This pilot study demonstrated significant improvements in chair-rise
performance and leg strength, suggesting that flexible, practitioner-led implementation can yield
                  17
strong results .

Further reinforcing the synergy between protein and exercise, the PROMISS trial (2022) examined
the effect of personalized dietary advice to increase protein intake to at least 1.2 g/kg/day, with or
                                                                  12
without advice on timing protein intake around physical activity . After six months, participants in
the intervention group showed significant improvements in 400-meter walking time and leg
                                                                                  1 12
extension strength compared to a control group that received no dietary advice . This finding
underscores that achieving a target protein intake, regardless of specific timing strategies, can
positively impact physical function in older adults. Finally, the CALM (Counteracting Age-related
Loss of Skeletal Muscle Mass) trial (2021) provided definitive evidence for the necessity of
combining protein with exercise. This one-year RCT evaluated protein supplementation (20g twice
                                                                         1
daily) with or without resistance exercise training against a placebo . The results were unequivocal:
quadriceps size and leg strength improved significantly in the group that received both exercise and
protein supplementation. Critically, there was no improvement in the protein-only group compared
to the placebo group, demonstrating a clear lack of efficacy for protein supplementation alone in this
           1 13
context . While baseline protein intakes in these five key RCTs were often above the 0.8 g/kg/day
RDA, the beneficial effects on muscle health were still observed, particularly when protein
                                                                   13
supplementation was combined with resistance exercise training . This suggests that even
individuals with suboptimal but not critically low intakes can benefit from targeted interventions, and
that the greatest benefits would likely be seen in those starting from a lower baseline.

Collectively, these trials build a compelling case for a dual-pronged approach to combating
sarcopenia. Simply increasing protein intake is insufficient to stimulate meaningful muscle growth
and strength. Resistance exercise acts as the essential trigger, creating a demand signal that allows the
additional dietary protein to be utilized for muscle repair and synthesis. Therefore, effective
interventions must integrate both components to achieve optimal outcomes for muscle health in
older adults.


Research on the Impact on Bone Health
The relationship between protein intake and bone health in older adults is complex and multifaceted,
with evidence pointing towards a protective effect, particularly concerning fracture risk, while the
impact on bone mineral density (BMD) remains less conclusive. Observational studies have provided
consistent signals of benefit, showing that higher habitual protein consumption is associated with
better baseline BMD and a lower incidence of fractures over time. Interventional studies, particularly
those involving exercise, further support a positive role for protein, although the direct synergistic
effect of combining protein supplementation with exercise for improving BMD is not yet firmly
established by high-quality randomized controlled trials. The overall effect appears highly contingent
on the presence of other key nutrients, most notably calcium and vitamin D, which modulate
protein's influence on bone metabolism.

Key observational studies published after 2019 have strengthened the link between protein and bone
integrity. A prospective cohort study by Weaver et al. (2021), part of the Health, Aging, and Body
Composition Study, found that older adults with a higher protein intake (1.1±0.4 g/kg/day) had
significantly higher hip and lumbar spine BMD at baseline compared to those with a lower intake
                     13
(0.8±0.3 g/kg/day) . Beyond baseline measures, the most striking finding was a 64% reduction in
the risk of developing vertebral fractures over a five-year period for those in the highest protein
                               13
tertile compared to the lowest . This suggests that a higher protein intake confers a protective
advantage against fragility fractures, a major consequence of osteoporosis. Another large prospective
cohort study corroborated these findings, showing that higher dietary protein intake (≥15% of total
energy intake) was associated with 1.8%-6.0% higher mean BMD at the hip, whole body, and lumbar
                                                          3
spine compared to lower intake (<13% of total energy) . Although this study did not find that
protein intake affected the rate of BMD change over 4–5 years, the robust association with reduced
                                                                                               3
vertebral fracture risk highlights its importance in maintaining bone structure and resilience .

Interventional evidence, while supportive, presents a more nuanced picture. The Kemmler et al.
(2020) study is a prominent example of a successful intervention. Over an 18-month period, older
men with osteoporosis and sarcopenia participated in a program combining high-intensity dynamic
resistance exercise with whey protein supplementation to achieve a target intake of 1.5–1.6 g/kg/
    1
day . At the end of the study, the intervention group showed significantly higher BMD at the lumbar
                                                                                                   1
spine and total hip compared to a control group that consumed 1.2 g/kg/day without exercise . This
again points to a powerful synergy between protein and mechanical loading from exercise in
promoting bone health. However, the translation of this evidence into general practice is complicated
by the findings of a systematic review and meta-analysis assessing the effects of combined protein
                                                                       2
and exercise interventions on bone mineral content (BMC) or BMD . This comprehensive review,
which identified ten RCTs, concluded that there was currently insufficient evidence to draw robust
                                                                    2
conclusions due to high heterogeneity and serious risks of bias . The authors noted that many
studies were underpowered, had short durations (mostly less than 28 weeks, which may be too brief
to detect measurable changes in bone turnover), and failed to adequately control for confounding
                                       2
factors like baseline calcium intake .

A critical factor that emerges from the literature is the interaction between protein and calcium
status. Multiple sources suggest that the benefits of protein on bone health are most pronounced
                                  20
when calcium intake is adequate . One study indicated that high animal protein intake could
increase fracture risk if calcium intake was low (<800 mg/day), whereas it may reduce fracture risk
                                                20
when calcium intake exceeded 800 mg/day . A randomized controlled study by Dawson-Hughes
and Harris provided mechanistic insight, finding that the association between higher protein intake
and less bone loss was only significant among participants who were also receiving calcium (500 mg/
                                                     20
day) and vitamin D (17.5 μg/day) supplements . This underscores that protein should be viewed as
part of a holistic nutritional strategy for bone health, where adequate co-factors are non-negotiable
prerequisites for realizing its full potential. The following table summarizes the core details of the

In summary, while observational data robustly links higher protein intake to better bone health and
reduced fracture risk, the evidence from interventional trials, particularly regarding BMD, is more
tentative. The existing RCT evidence base is limited by methodological shortcomings and a lack of
long-term studies designed to measure skeletal outcomes. Nevertheless, the overwhelming consensus
supports the inclusion of sufficient protein as part of a comprehensive strategy for maintaining bone
health in older adults, a strategy that must also prioritize adequate calcium and vitamin D intake.


The Importance of Protein Quality
Beyond the quantity of protein consumed, the quality of that protein is a critical determinant of its
biological value, particularly for older adults who experience a phenomenon known as anabolic
resistance—their muscles become less responsive to the stimuli that normally promote protein
synthesis. Animal proteins are generally considered superior to most plant proteins in this regard,
possessing characteristics that make them more effective at building and maintaining muscle and
bone tissue. This distinction poses a significant challenge when advising older adults to adopt plant-
based diets, necessitating careful planning to ensure nutritional adequacy and compensate for
inherent differences in digestibility and amino acid profiles.

The superiority of animal proteins stems from three key factors: digestibility, amino acid
composition, and the presence of antinutritional factors. Animal proteins, sourced from meat, fish,
dairy, and eggs, are typically more easily digested and contain all indispensable (essential) amino acids
                                                           23 25
in proportions that closely match human requirements . Plant proteins, conversely, often have
lower digestibility due to structural components like fiber and the presence of antinutritional factors
                                                                                                   19 22 23
such as phytates and protease inhibitors, which can hinder the absorption of amino acids        . More
importantly, most plant proteins are deficient in one or more essential amino acids. For example,
cereal grains like wheat and maize are typically low in lysine, while legumes like soybeans and lentils
                                                                                22 29
are limited in sulfur-containing amino acids like methionine and cysteine . This incomplete amino
acid profile means that plant proteins are less efficient at stimulating muscle protein synthesis (MPS)
22
 . This differential effect has been directly measured in older adults. A crossover randomized trial
comparing whole-food meals found that a MEAT meal (lean beef) resulted in significantly higher
postprandial MPS rates over a 6-hour period compared to a PLANT meal (soy, chickpeas, etc.) with
                                           28
identical total protein and energy content . This difference was driven by markedly higher plasma
                                                                                              28
concentrations of essential amino acids and, crucially, leucine, following the meat meal . Another
study confirmed that animal-based whole foods (lean pork, whole eggs) produced significantly
greater increases in plasma EAA concentrations than plant-based equivalents (black beans, almonds)
                                                                                   26
in older adults, correlating with their enhanced potential to stimulate MPS .

Leucine, a branched-chain amino acid, is repeatedly highlighted as a key regulator of MPS. Animal
proteins, particularly dairy products like whey and casein, tend to have a higher leucine content
                                   22 26
compared to most plant proteins . To maximally stimulate MPS in older adults, who require a
higher per-meal stimulus, a threshold of approximately 2.5–2.8 g of leucine per meal is needed,
                                                                        14
which is often easier to achieve with high-quality animal proteins . While strategies exist to improve
the quality of plant proteins, such as blending complementary sources (e.g., combining legumes with
cereals to create a complete amino acid profile), fortifying with limiting amino acids, or using isolated
                                                       22 25
protein concentrates, these solutions add complexity           . For older adults, who may already face
                                                                                                              1 25
challenges with appetite and digestive efficiency, these practical considerations are significant                    .
Simply replacing animal protein with plant protein without strategic planning risks delivering a
                                                                                                1 24
suboptimal dose of essential amino acids, potentially compromising muscle and bone health              .

This concern extends beyond muscle to bone health. Observational data has linked higher serum
concentrations of various essential amino acids to better bone outcomes. Specifically, higher levels of
valine, leucine, isoleucine, and tryptophan were associated with a slower rate of hip BMD decline
                1
over four years . Furthermore, higher tryptophan levels were linked to fewer major osteoporotic
fractures, and higher valine levels were associated with a lower risk of hip fracture, a connection that
                                               1
has been replicated in multiple cohort studies . Given that plant proteins are often lower in these
same amino acids, ensuring adequate intake through a varied diet or fortified products becomes even
more critical for bone health. Replacing animal proteins with plant proteins in Nordic diets, for
instance, has been modeled to decrease intakes of vitamin B12 and iodine, and markers indicated
                                                                  20
enhanced bone turnover with a possible increased fracture risk . Therefore, when promoting plant-
based diets for older adults, it is imperative to focus not just on total protein quantity but also on
strategies to enhance quality, such as consuming a diverse array of plant foods, incorporating
fortified products, and educating individuals on the principles of protein complementation. Without
this careful attention, the promotion of plant-based diets could inadvertently lead to nutrient
deficiencies that undermine the very goals of healthy aging.
