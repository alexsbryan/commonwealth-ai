### 1.1 Indonesia

**Mandatory Schemes:**

**Taspen - JP (Jaminan Pensiun)** - Public Sector

- **Type:** Pay-As-You-Go Defined Benefit (PAYG DB)
- **Coverage:** Mandatory for all civil servants and public sector employees
- **Contributions:** Employee contributes 4.75% of salary; employer contribution varies
- **Benefit Formula:** 2.5% annual accrual rate on final basic salary[^1][^2]
- **Key Features:** Provides defined benefits with minimum 45% and maximum 80% of final basic salary after qualifying service[^3]

**BPJS Ketenagakerjaan - JP (Jaminan Pensiun)** - Private Sector

- **Type:** Asset-backed Defined Benefit (Asset-backed DB)
- **Coverage:** Mandatory for private sector employees
- **Contributions:** Employee 1%, Employer 2% (capped at IDR 10,547,400 as of March 2025)[^4][^5]
- **Benefit Formula:** 1% annual accrual rate on weighted average annual salary adjusted for inflation[^3]
- **Key Features:** Minimum benefit IDR 399,700/month (2025); maximum IDR 4,792,300/month (2025)[^6]

**BPJS Ketenagakerjaan - JHT (Jaminan Hari Tua)** - Old Age Security

- **Type:** Asset-backed Defined Contribution (Asset-backed DC)
- **Coverage:** Mandatory for all private sector employees[^4]
- **Contributions:** Employee 2%, Employer 3.7% of monthly wage (no salary cap)[^7][^4]
- **Key Features:** Lump-sum benefit paid at retirement, resignation, or permanent disability; partial withdrawals allowed (10% or 30%) under certain conditions[^4]

**Voluntary Schemes:**

**DPPK (Dana Pensiun Pemberi Kerja)** - Employer Pension Funds

- **Type:** Various (can be DB or DC)

- **Contributions:** Varies by employer design

**DPLK (Dana Pensiun Lembaga Keuangan)** - Financial Institution Pension Funds

- **Type:** Defined Contribution (DC)
- **Coverage:** Voluntary individual pension schemes offered by financial institutions[^8]
- **Contributions:** Individual voluntary contributions


### 1.2 Malaysia

**Mandatory Schemes:**

**EPF (Employees Provident Fund / KWSP)**

- **Type:** Asset-backed Defined Contribution (Asset-backed DC)
- **Coverage:** Mandatory for all Malaysian citizens and permanent residents employed in the private sector and non-pensionable public servants; mandatory for foreign workers from October 2025[^9][^10]
- **Contributions:**
    - Malaysian employees below age 60: 11% employee, 12-13% employer (13% if salary ≤ MYR 5,000)[^11][^12]
    - Malaysian employees age 60-75: 0% employee, 4% employer[^11]
    - Foreign employees: 2% employee, 2% employer (from October 2025)[^10][^13]
- **Account Structure:** Divided into Account 1 (70% - retirement), Account 2 (30% - pre-retirement withdrawals), and new Account 3 (10% - flexible withdrawals from 2024)[^14]
- **Key Features:** Members can withdraw at age 55; basic savings target of MYR 240,000 by age 55 (proposed to increase to MYR 390,000 by 2028)[^15][^16]

**JPA Public Sector Pension**

- **Type:** Pay-As-You-Go Defined Benefit (PAYG DB)
- **Coverage:** Mandatory for civil servants and pensionable public sector employees[^3]
- **Contributions:** Non-contributory (fully government-funded)
- **Benefit Formula:** Minimum benefit MYR 1,000 with at least 25 years of service; maximum 60% of final salary after 30 years[^3]

**Voluntary Schemes:**

**i-Saraan**

- **Type:** Defined Contribution (DC)
- **Coverage:** Voluntary scheme for self-employed and informal sector workers[^17][^18]
- **Contributions:** Voluntary contributions with government matching increased to 20% (up to RM 600/year, lifetime cap RM 6,000 from 2026)[^18][^17]

**i-Suri**

- **Type:** Defined Contribution (DC)
- **Coverage:** Voluntary scheme for housewives[^17]
- **Contributions:** Voluntary with government matching support


### 1.3 Pakistan

**Mandatory Schemes:**

**CSPS (Civil Service Pension Scheme)**

- **Type:** Pay-As-You-Go Defined Benefit (PAYG DB)
- **Coverage:** Mandatory for all federal and provincial civil servants[^19][^20]
- **Contributions:** Non-contributory (fully government-funded from general revenues)[^19]
- **Benefit Formula:** 2.33% annual accrual rate; 70% replacement rate after 30 years of service[^20][^19]
- **Salary Base:** Average salary over past 1-3 years (varies by province)[^3]
- **Key Features:** Minimum benefit PKR 10,000-17,000 (varies by province); employees eligible at age 60 or after 25 years of service[^21][^19]

**EOBI (Employees' Old-Age Benefits Institution)**

- **Type:** Pay-As-You-Go Defined Benefit (PAYG DB)
- **Coverage:** Mandatory for private sector employees in establishments with 10 or more employees[^22][^23]
- **Contributions:** Funded through mandatory employer and employee contributions[^22]
- **Benefit Formula:** 2% annual accrual rate based on average minimum wage over past 12 months[^3]
- **Key Features:** Minimum pension PKR 11,500/month (effective January 2025); maximum PKR 30,000/month; requires 15 years for pension or 2 years for lump sum[^24][^25][^3]
- **Recent Reform:** 15% pension increase approved in 2025; new formula-based system introduced for future retirees[^25][^26][^24]

**Voluntary Schemes:**

**VPS (Voluntary Pension System)**

- **Type:** Defined Contribution (DC)
- **Coverage:** Voluntary individual pension scheme regulated by SECP[^27][^28]
- **Contributions:** Voluntary individual contributions with tax incentives
- **Key Features:** Retirement age between 60-70 years or after 25 years of contribution; lump sum or regular pension payments available[^27]

**Provincial Pension Funds**

- **Type:** Various (transitioning to DC in some provinces)
- **Coverage:** Provincial government employees[^20]
- **Contributions:** Varies by province; some introducing contributory schemes


### 1.4 Philippines

**Mandatory Schemes:**

**GSIS (Government Service Insurance System)**

- **Type:** Pay-As-You-Go Defined Benefit (PAYG DB)
- **Coverage:** Mandatory for all government employees and officials[^29][^30]
- **Contributions:** Employee 12%, employer varies[^30]
- **Benefit Formula:** 2.5% annual accrual rate on average salary over past 36 months[^3]
- **Key Features:** Maximum benefit 90% of average salary; requires 15 years for pension or 3 years for lump sum[^3]
- **Retirement Age:** 60 (optional), 65 (mandatory); proposed reduction to 56 for optional retirement[^31][^32]
- **Special Programs:** "Magic 87" formula (age + years of service = 87) for earlier retirement eligibility[^32][^33]

**SSS (Social Security System)**

- **Type:** Pay-As-You-Go Defined Benefit (PAYG DB) + Mandatory Provident Fund (DC component)
- **Coverage:** Mandatory for private sector employees; voluntary for self-employed, non-working spouses, and OFWs[^34][^35][^36]
- **Contributions (2025):** Employee 5%, Employer 10%, total 15% of Monthly Salary Credit (MSC)[^37][^38][^39]
- **MSC Range:** Minimum PHP 5,000, Maximum PHP 35,000 (2025)[^38][^39]
- **Benefit Formula:** 2% annual accrual rate on average salary over past 60 months[^3]
- **Key Features:** Minimum benefit is higher of 40% of average salary or PHP 1,200 (10 years) / PHP 2,400 (20 years); salary capped at PHP 20,000/month in formula[^3]
- **Pension Reform:** Historic 3-year pension increase program starting September 2025[^34]

**SSS - Mandatory Provident Fund (MPF)**

- **Type:** Asset-backed Defined Contribution (Asset-backed DC)
- **Coverage:** Mandatory for contributions exceeding PHP 20,000 MSC[^38]
- **Contributions:** Portion of SSS contributions above PHP 20,000 MSC allocated to individual MPF accounts[^38]
- **Benefits:** Retirement, permanent total disability, and death benefits based on accumulated value[^38]

**Voluntary Coverage:**

- Self-employed persons, voluntary members, non-working spouses, OFWs can participate voluntarily[^35][^36][^40]

## Section 3: Analysis of Main Drivers of Retirement Savings Protection Gaps

Based on the comprehensive data collected on pension systems across these seven Asian countries, several critical structural factors drive retirement savings protection gaps:

### 3.1 Inadequate Contribution Periods and Recent Reductions

A major driver of protection gaps is the **short minimum contribution period** required for pension eligibility, which has been **further reduced** in recent reforms:

**Recent Reductions Creating Gaps:**

- **Vietnam** reduced the minimum from 20 years to 15 years (effective July 2025), with females receiving only 45% replacement rate at 15 years and males receiving just 40% at 15 years[^65][^64][^61]
- **Philippines** requires only 10 years for SSS pension (down from historical standards), with minimum benefit of 40% or PHP 1,200-2,400[^3]
- **Indonesia** (BPJS-JP) requires 15 years, down from previous standards[^3]

**Impact:** Workers contributing for the minimum period receive inadequate replacement rates (40-45%) that fall far below the internationally recommended 66-75% replacement rate needed for retirement adequacy. The recent reductions, while improving access, exacerbate benefit adequacy problems.[^73]

### 3.2 Restrictive Earnings Caps in Benefit Formulas

Multiple countries impose **salary caps** that have remained **fixed for decades**, becoming increasingly restrictive and reducing expected replacement rates:

**Most Severe Caps:**

- **Thailand SSF:** THB 15,000/month cap **unchanged since 1999**—after 26 years of wage growth, this cap now covers far fewer workers adequately[^3]
- **Philippines SSS:** PHP 20,000/month cap **unchanged since at least 2019**[^3]
- **Indonesia BPJS-JP:** IDR 10,547,400 cap indexed to GDP growth but may not keep pace with wage inflation[^5]
- **Vietnam:** 20 times reference level cap on earnings[^3]

**Impact:** As wages grow faster than these caps are adjusted, an increasing proportion of workers receive benefits calculated on artificially low earnings bases, systematically reducing their retirement income relative to pre-retirement wages. Middle-income workers become particularly vulnerable as inflation erodes the real value of capped benefits.

### 3.3 Low Accrual Rates for Private Sector Workers

There exists a **significant disparity** between public and private sector pension accrual rates:

**Public vs. Private Sector Gap:**

- **Indonesia:** Public sector (Taspen) 2.5% vs. Private sector (BPJS) 1.0% annual accrual[^3]
- **Pakistan:** Public sector (CSPS) 2.33% vs. Private sector (EOBI) 2.0%[^3]
- **Thailand:** Public sector (CSP) 2.0% vs. Private sector (SSF) 1.3-1.5%[^3]
- **Philippines:** Public sector (GSIS) 2.5% vs. Private sector (SSS) 2.0%[^3]

**Impact:** Private sector workers accumulate pension benefits at 40-60% the rate of public sector workers, creating substantial inequity. After 30 years, a private sector worker in Indonesia receives only 30% replacement rate compared to 75% for public sector workers with identical service periods.

### 3.4 Gender Disparities in Retirement Age and Benefits

Several countries maintain **differential treatment by gender** that creates protection gaps:

**Retirement Age Disparities:**

- **Sri Lanka EPF:** Females retire at 50 vs. males at 55 (though proposed to equalize at 60)[^48][^47]
- **Vietnam:** Females retire at 56-60 (by 2035) vs. males at 62 (by 2028)[^74][^72][^61]
- **Pakistan:** Lower retirement ages for females in some schemes[^3]

**Benefit Formula Differences:**

- **Vietnam:** Females receive 45% at 15 years vs. males receiving only 40% at 15-20 years—but females must still reach only 30 years for maximum 75% vs. males requiring 35 years[^64][^61]

**Impact:** Lower retirement ages for women, combined with longer life expectancy, result in longer retirement periods with smaller accumulated savings. While appearing beneficial, earlier retirement with fewer contribution years creates greater vulnerability to poverty in old age, especially given women's typically lower lifetime earnings.

### 3.5 Fragmented Coverage Leaving Informal Workers Unprotected

The most severe protection gap stems from **systematic exclusion of informal sector workers**:

**Coverage Gaps by Country:**

- **Pakistan:** Over 90% of workers in informal sector with no mandatory coverage; EOBI covers only establishments with 10+ employees[^75][^76]
- **Philippines:** 92% of Indian workers and large portions across Southeast Asia lack formal pension coverage[^77]
- **Thailand:** Informal workers must voluntarily join Article 40 (low uptake) or wait for proposed National Pension Fund[^76]
- **Indonesia:** Massive informal sector with limited access to BPJS programs[^76]
- **Vietnam:** Voluntary SI scheme has very low uptake among informal workers despite government subsidies[^67]

**Mandatory Coverage Restrictions:**

- Most mandatory schemes cover only **formal employment relationships**
- **Establishment size thresholds** (e.g., Pakistan's 10+ employees) exclude micro-enterprises
- **Self-employed and agricultural workers** largely excluded from mandatory systems

**Impact:** The majority of the working population in these countries—street vendors, domestic workers, agricultural laborers, gig workers, and those in micro-enterprises—accumulate zero formal pension savings. This creates a massive adequacy gap affecting hundreds of millions of workers who will reach old age with no retirement income beyond family support or minimal social pensions.[^73][^77][^76]

### 3.6 Misaligned Retirement Ages and Life Expectancy

Countries face a critical **mismatch between retirement ages and increasing longevity**:

**Life Expectancy vs. Retirement Age Gap:**

- **Malaysia:** Life expectancy 76.5 (men) to 78.6 (women) with retirement age 55, creating 21-24 year retirement period that EPF balances struggle to cover[^78][^73]
- **Thailand:** Life expectancy 75 with retirement age 55, creating 20-year gap[^79]
- **Sri Lanka:** Women retiring at 50 face 27+ year retirement period[^47]

**Gradual Increases Still Insufficient:**

- **Indonesia:** Increasing to 65 by 2043—but life expectancy rising faster[^70][^71]
- **Vietnam:** Increasing to 62 (males) by 2028 and 60 (females) by 2035, but life expectancy already exceeded these ages[^69][^61]

**Impact:** Extended retirement periods strain defined contribution balances and PAYG system sustainability. The EPF's basic savings target of MYR 240,000 translates to only RM 1,000 monthly for 20 years—inadequate for rising living costs. Low accrual rate DB systems cannot provide adequate benefits when workers spend 20-30 years in retirement.[^80][^15]

### 3.7 Benefit Formula Design Creating Systematic Inadequacy

Multiple benefit formula features systematically reduce pension adequacy:

**Final Salary vs. Career Average Issues:**

- **Indonesia public sector (Taspen):** Final basic salary excludes allowances that may constitute 40-50% of total compensation[^2]
- **Pakistan CSPS:** While using average of 1-3 final years appears generous, allowances added post-retirement actually create over-100% replacement rates for some—unsustainable and inequitable[^81][^19]
- **Vietnam transition:** Shift to full career average for new entrants reduces benefits for those with steep late-career salary growth[^63]

**Minimum Service Thresholds:**

- **Thailand:** 25 years required for CSP pension (vs. 15 for private sector SSF)—creates cliff effect[^3]
- **Pakistan:** 10-25 years required for CSPS depending on province—inconsistent protection[^3]
- **Malaysia JPA:** 10 years minimum but maximum benefit requires 30 years[^3]

**Lump Sum vs. Pension Option Problems:**

- Countries offering lump sums for those below minimum years (Indonesia, Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam) provide one-time payments that workers often exhaust quickly, leaving them unprotected in later old age[^3]

**Impact:** These formula features create multiple pathways to inadequacy: workers with interrupted careers receive lump sums instead of pensions; career breaks push workers below minimum thresholds; salary base definitions exclude significant earnings; and early-career average systems disadvantage workers with back-loaded wage profiles.

### 3.8 Contribution Rate Inadequacy in Funded Schemes

Several defined contribution systems have **contribution rates too low** to generate adequate retirement savings:

**Insufficient DC Contribution Rates:**

- **Malaysia EPF:** 11-13% (employee) + 12-13% (employer) = 23-26% total appears adequate, but Account 2 pre-retirement withdrawals and low returns reduce retirement accumulation[^12][^9]
- **Indonesia JHT:** 2% (employee) + 3.7% (employer) = 5.7% total is **grossly insufficient** for adequate retirement savings[^4]
- **Thailand EWF:** 0.25% + 0.25% = 0.5% (2025-2030) provides minimal supplementary savings[^51][^54]
- **Pakistan VPS:** Voluntary nature with limited uptake[^27]

**Comparison to Adequacy Standards:**

- Actuarial studies suggest 15-20% lifetime contribution rates needed for 70% replacement rate
- **Indonesia's 5.7% JHT** would require 40-50+ years of contributions to approach adequacy
- **Thailand's 0.5% EWF** serves only as minimal severance supplement, not meaningful retirement income

**Impact:** Low mandatory contribution rates in DC systems systematically ensure inadequate retirement savings. Workers reaching retirement age with decades of contributions still face insufficient balances. The problem compounds for workers with interrupted careers or late-career entry into formal employment.

### 3.9 Non-Indexed Benefits and Erosion of Adequacy

Fixed benefit levels and non-indexed parameters create **progressive erosion of adequacy**:

**Fixed Nominal Caps:**

- **Pakistan EOBI:** Maximum PKR 30,000/month cap, while minimum wage PKR 37,000—pensioners receive less than working-age minimum wage[^26]
- **Indonesia BPJS-JP:** Minimum/maximum benefits indexed to prices but wages grow faster[^6][^3]

**Discretionary Indexation:**

- **China:** Discretionary adjustment creates uncertainty[^82][^83]
- **Thailand:** Combination wage/price indexation subject to negotiation[^82]

**Valorisation Issues:**

- Most countries use **price indexation** for past earnings rather than wage indexation[^83][^82]
- Creates systematic reduction in replacement rates as real wages grow over careers

**Impact:** Pensioners experience declining purchasing power relative to active workers. Initial replacement rates of 45-50% erode to 30-35% effective rates within 10-15 years of retirement as prices rise faster than pension adjustments. Fixed caps on benefits and earnings bases become increasingly restrictive, excluding more workers from adequate protection.

### 3.10 Institutional Fragmentation and Portability Gaps

**Multiple separate systems** within countries create portability and coverage gaps:

**System Fragmentation:**

- **Thailand:** GPF (public DC) + CSP (public DB) + SSF (private DB) + PVD (private voluntary DC) + EWF (new mandatory DC) = 5 separate systems with different rules[^50][^51][^3]
- **Indonesia:** Taspen (public) + BPJS (private mandatory) + DPPK (employer voluntary) + DPLK (individual voluntary) = 4 main systems[^1][^8][^2]
- **Pakistan:** CSPS (federal/provincial public) + Provincial funds + EOBI (private) + VPS (voluntary) = fragmented provincial variations[^19][^20]

**Portability Problems:**

- Workers moving between public/private sectors often lose benefits or cannot transfer credits
- **Thailand:** Moving from Article 33 to self-employment requires switching to voluntary Article 40 with different benefit structure[^52]
- **Philippines:** Separate GSIS and SSS systems not integrated—public-to-private transitions problematic[^29]

**Impact:** Career mobility between sectors results in fragmented, inadequate coverage. Workers accumulate shorter periods in multiple systems, failing to reach minimum thresholds in any single system. The lack of portability particularly affects younger workers with non-linear career paths, women with career interruptions, and those transitioning from informal to formal employment.

***

## Conclusion

The retirement savings protection gaps in these seven Asian countries stem from fundamental structural problems in pension system design rather than merely insufficient funding. The reduction of minimum contribution periods (Vietnam, Philippines), decade-old frozen earnings caps (Thailand, Philippines), systematic exclusion of informal workers (Pakistan, Indonesia), low accrual rates for private sector workers (Indonesia 1.0%, Thailand 1.3%), inadequate DC contribution rates (Indonesia JHT 5.7%), and misaligned retirement ages all combine to create a **multi-dimensional adequacy crisis**.

The most vulnerable populations—informal sector workers, women with interrupted careers, private sector employees, and those in the lower-middle income brackets above poverty thresholds but below earnings caps—face systematic exclusion or inadequate benefit levels. Even workers with full formal careers increasingly find that earnings caps restrict their benefits while contribution periods prove insufficient for adequate replacement rates.

Addressing these protection gaps requires comprehensive reform beyond incremental adjustments: earnings caps must be indexed to wage growth; minimum contribution periods must be balanced with adequate accrual rates to ensure sufficiency; informal sector coverage must expand through innovative matching schemes and simplified administration; and system fragmentation must be reduced to enable portability across changing career paths. Without such structural reforms, hundreds of millions of workers in these countries will reach retirement age with inadequate or non-existent pension protection, creating significant fiscal, social, and humanitarian challenges in rapidly aging societies.
