A Comprehensive Analysis of Competition in the
Public Cloud Computing Market
Market Structure and Competitive Dynamics
The global market for public cloud computing infrastructure and platform services, encompassing
Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) and Platform as a Service (PaaS), is characterized by an extreme
                                                                                12
degree of concentration among a handful of dominant technology firms . Competition authorities
across Europe, Asia, and North America have conducted extensive investigations into this sector,
consistently finding that the market structure fails to exhibit the characteristics of a well-functioning
                           6 7 10
competitive environment             . Instead, it presents a landscape where a few "hyperscale" providers
                                                                                                  3
command a majority of the market, while other players struggle to gain significant traction . The
primary hyperscalers identified in these studies are Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure,
and Google Cloud, which collectively account for the vast majority of revenue and growth in the
       1 5 20
sector . Beyond these giants, the market includes a diverse array of other key players, such as pure-
play cloud providers, specialized secure service providers, and major traditional IT operators who
                                      13
have pivoted to cloud services . However, their collective market share remains marginal when
contrasted with the dominant trio, highlighting the structural imbalances that define the industry.

The market structure is not merely concentrated but also remarkably stable over time, suggesting that
                                                                                 2
competitive pressures are insufficient to challenge the established leaders . This stability is reinforced
by formidable barriers to entry and expansion, which prevent new or smaller competitors from
                                                                                          78
effectively challenging the incumbent position of AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud . The high fixed
costs associated with building and maintaining global-scale data centers, coupled with the immense
capital investment required for research and development, create an environment where only the
                                                                     28
most financially powerful corporations can compete at scale . This has led to a situation where
competition is often described as a "competition-for-the-market," where clients typically commit to a
single provider for each distinct workload, resulting in limited multi-homing and reduced competitive
                                           2
pressure on any given incumbent . The dynamics of the market are further shaped by strategic
mergers and acquisitions, joint ventures, and deep technological partnerships, which hyperscalers use
to consolidate their market power, acquire innovative technologies, and stifle potential future
                1
competition . For instance, the French Competition Authority noted a trend of aggressive
acquisitions by hyperscalers in France, while European operators engage in such activity less
                                                                 1
frequently, potentially exacerbating market concentration .

The dominance of the hyperscalers is clearly reflected in market share data collected by various
competition authorities. These figures, though sometimes varying slightly based on methodology and
scope, paint a consistent picture of a highly concentrated market. In France in 2021, for example,
AWS commanded 46% of revenues, Microsoft Azure held 17%, and Google Cloud accounted for
                      1 4 5 22
approximately 8-10%       . Together, these three providers captured 80% of the growth in spending
on public cloud infrastructures and applications that year, indicating they were capturing nearly all
                                  12
the benefits of market expansion . Similar patterns are observed across different jurisdictions. In
the United Kingdom, the combined market share of AWS and Azure was estimated at 80% in 2022,
                                            22
with Google Cloud holding a 5-10% share . More recent data from the UK's Competition and
Markets Authority (CMA) for 2024 shows a similar duopoly, with both AWS and Microsoft holding
                                                                                            7 8 17 29
between 30% and 40% of the IaaS market, and Google Cloud accounting for 5-10%       . The
market in South Korea appears even more dominated by a single player, with AWS holding
                                                                                                                         10 22
approximately 70% of the market in 2021, followed by Microsoft Azure at 12% and Naver at 7% .
In Japan in 2020, AWS held a commanding 40-50% share, with Azure at 10-20% and NTT at 5-10%
22

This structural reality, as documented by multiple competition authorities, forms the backdrop for
the numerous anti-competitive practices being scrutinized. The very architecture of the market—
dominated by a few colossal players with immense financial capacity and integrated digital
                                                                                                             12
ecosystems—creates an environment where competition is stifled before it can truly begin . The
hyperscalers' ability to leverage their market power across adjacent sectors, invest heavily in
innovation without fear of displacement, and impose significant switching barriers on customers
fundamentally alters the nature of competition in the digital economy. Consequently, regulators are
grappling with whether existing competition laws are sufficient to address these systemic failures or if
                                                                                                25
new, more targeted regulatory frameworks are necessary to restore a level playing field . The
ongoing investigations by the CMA in the UK, the Autorité de la concurrence in France, and the
KFTC in South Korea reflect a growing international consensus that the current state of competition
                                                                               2 6 10
in the public cloud market is deeply flawed and requires urgent intervention            .
Drivers of High Market Concentration
The extreme market concentration observed in the public cloud IaaS and PaaS sectors is not an
accident of history but is actively reinforced by a combination of formidable structural barriers and
powerful network effects that create a near-impenetrable fortress around the leading hyperscalers:
                                                                27
Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud . These forces work in concert to deter
new entrants, hinder the expansion of existing rivals, and entrench the market dominance of the
                                                                                          2
incumbents, ensuring that the competitive landscape remains largely static over time . The primary
drivers of this concentration can be categorized into two overarching themes: the immense economic
and logistical challenges of entry, which give rise to powerful economies of scale, and the self-
reinforcing value loops created by network effects that make platforms more valuable as they attract
                                       2 10
more users, developers, and partners          .

One of the most significant barriers to entry is the staggering capital investment required to build and
                                                  27
operate a global-scale cloud infrastructure . Establishing a credible cloud presence involves
constructing massive data centers, acquiring advanced networking equipment, and investing heavily
                                                                                8
in research and development to keep pace with rapid technological change . The financial capacity
                                                                                                    1
needed is so substantial that it acts as a natural filter against all but the wealthiest corporations . For
instance, one analysis notes that building cloud infrastructure in France alone requires an investment
of €100–200 million and takes five to six years, a timeline and cost out of reach for most potential
                  2
competitors . These high fixed costs translate directly into powerful economies of scale; as
hyperscalers serve more customers, the per-unit cost of providing cloud services decreases
significantly, allowing them to offer lower prices and broader service portfolios than smaller rivals
                      28
could ever match . This dynamic creates a virtuous cycle for the incumbents: their scale allows them
to invest more in R&D, which leads to better products and services, attracting even more customers
and further solidifying their cost advantage. This makes it exceptionally difficult for new entrants to
gain a foothold, as they would need to achieve a critical mass of customers quickly to become
profitable, a task made nearly impossible by the established players' price competitiveness and feature
         7
breadth .

Complementing these economic barriers are powerful network effects that create a strong pull
                               10
towards the largest platforms . The value of a cloud platform increases exponentially with the
                                                                           2
number of users, developers, and third-party applications built upon it . When a large number of
businesses adopt a particular cloud provider, it attracts a corresponding influx of developers and
Independent Software Vendors (ISVs) who want to build applications and solutions for that large
             28
user base . This, in turn, enriches the ecosystem with more tools, integrations, and services, making
the platform even more attractive to new customers, thereby creating a self-reinforcing cycle of
        10
growth . These network effects are particularly potent when a hyperscaler's cloud services are tightly
integrated with its dominant adjacent software markets, such as Microsoft's ubiquitous Office
                                                       18 28
productivity suite and Windows operating system . For many enterprises, the prospect of seamless
integration, compatibility, and access to a vast library of pre-built applications is a powerful incentive
to choose Azure over a rival platform, even if the rival offers superior performance or lower pricing
                               28
on a purely technical basis . The Korean Fair Trade Commission (KFTC) study highlighted that for
client companies in South Korea, the main reasons for concentrating their spending with a single
provider were service quality (cited by 42.9%), the wide range of solutions and services (40.2%), and
                                                                                                  10
reputation (38.6%), all of which are direct outcomes of these powerful network effects .

Beyond these fundamental economic and network-driven factors, hyperscalers actively employ
strategic maneuvers to maintain and strengthen their market positions. Mergers and acquisitions are a
key tool in this arsenal, used to eliminate potential threats, acquire innovative technologies, and
                           1
expand service offerings . The French Competition Authority noted a trend of aggressive
acquisitions by hyperscalers in France, which may consolidate market power and reduce competition
1
. Furthermore, strategic partnerships, such as the collaboration between AWS and Salesforce or
Microsoft and Oracle, can create powerful alliances that raise concerns about anti-competitive
                                            12
behavior depending on their structure . Joint ventures, like Bleu—a collaboration between
Capgemini and Orange based on Microsoft Azure—may also consolidate market power among
                                                                            1
dominant players, limiting opportunities for smaller firms to thrive . This active management of the
competitive landscape through strategic investments and partnerships ensures that the path to
becoming a serious challenger in the cloud market is fraught with obstacles, both structural and
strategic. The combination of these forces results in a market structure that is not only highly
concentrated but also resilient to change, posing a significant challenge for competition authorities
seeking to foster a more dynamic and contestable environment.


Overwhelming Switching Barriers and Customer Lock-In
A central theme emerging from market studies conducted by competition authorities worldwide is
the existence of overwhelming switching barriers that effectively lock customers into the cloud
                                       2 6 22
platforms of dominant hyperscalers . This phenomenon of customer lock-in severely weakens
competitive pressure, as providers face little risk of losing customers even if their prices exceed
                                                      24
competitive levels or their service quality declines . The barriers to switching are not singular but
rather a multifaceted combination of severe technical hurdles, restrictive contractual terms, and
punitive financial disincentives, creating a formidable "switching wall" that makes migration between
                                                                      128
providers exceptionally difficult, costly, and risky for businesses         . Less than 1% of cloud customers
                                                                                                       7 24
switch providers annually, a statistic that starkly illustrates the depth of this lock-in effect              .

Technical barriers represent the most fundamental obstacle to supplier switching. Hyperscalers
design their platforms with unique Application Programming Interfaces (APIs), data formats, and
                                                                                           1 22
service architectures, which are often incompatible with those of their competitors . Consequently,
migrating an application from one cloud to another is rarely a simple matter of transferring files; it
often requires significant re-engineering, code rewriting, and testing to ensure the application
                                                 1
functions correctly on the new platform . This process is particularly acute with foundational
services like AWS S3 object storage and Platform-as-a-Service (PaaS) databases, where
interoperability challenges disproportionately affect smaller competitors and make it difficult for
                                                            1
customers to move away from the incumbent provider . The lack of standardized, open interfaces
across the industry means that customers build their IT systems natively within a specific cloud's
                                                                              8
ecosystem, creating deep dependencies that are hard to break . This issue is compounded by what is
known as "data gravity," where the sheer volume of data accumulated on a single platform makes its
subsequent transfer prohibitively expensive and complex, further entrenching the incumbent
                  8
provider . The result is a fragmented technological landscape where services are not easily
                                                                                                                   8 11
substitutable, reinforcing vendor-specific dependencies and limiting true multi-cloud adoption                             .

In addition to these profound technical challenges, hyperscalers impose significant contractual and
financial barriers that discourage customers from exploring alternatives or adopting multi-cloud
                                                                                                    2 10
strategies. A primary source of friction is the opacity and complexity of pricing models . Providers
typically unilaterally determine their pricing structures, which are based on a multitude of usage
metrics, making it exceedingly difficult for clients to predict their total costs or accurately compare
                                            10
offerings from different vendors . According to a survey by the KFTC, only 20.1% of client
companies in South Korea found it easy to predict their cloud service costs, with the main difficulties
being the complexity of pricing systems (50.3%) and the unpredictability of data transmission volume
                 10
(49.5%) . This lack of transparency weakens price competition and undermines customers' ability to
                                                 10
make informed purchasing decisions . Furthermore, standard form contracts are prevalent in the
B2B cloud market, giving most customers little to no negotiating power, except for the largest
                          3
enterprise clients . These contracts often contain clauses that tie customers to the provider's
                                                                                                                       10 22
ecosystem and can obscure additional costs, increasing the risk of suboptimal service selection .
The combination of opaque pricing and non-negotiable contractual terms creates a significant power
                                                                                                                3 10
imbalance between hyperscalers and their clients, further cementing their market dominance                             .

Perhaps the most direct and widely criticized financial barrier is the practice of charging high egress
       1 2 22
fees            . These fees are levied for the transfer of data out of a cloud provider's infrastructure, while
                                     1 10
data ingress is typically free . Competition authorities in France, the Netherlands, and the UK have
examined these practices extensively, finding that the fees charged by hyperscalers are often grossly
                                                             22
disproportionate to the actual cost of data transfer . One report notes that these fees can be up to
8000% higher than actual costs, representing a direct financial penalty for customers who wish to
                                                                  22
switch providers or implement a multi-cloud strategy . The Dutch Authority for Consumers and
                                                                                                           22
Markets (ACM) has considered whether these fees could constitute an abuse of dominance . While
providers justify egress fees as a mechanism for cost recovery, regulators view them as a deliberate
                                                                                  9
tool to entrench their market position and discourage competition . By making data portability
expensive, hyperscalers increase the effective switching costs for customers, locking them into their
                                                                       8 24
platform regardless of the quality or price offered by rivals . Even when hyperscalers remove egress
fees for customers switching to a competitor, evidence suggests this does not lead to a material
                                                                                                                               13
increase in switching rates, implying that other technical and contractual barriers remain dominant .
The EU's forthcoming Data Act is a direct response to this problem, mandating improved customer
switching capabilities and the establishment of European interoperability standards to enhance
                                                        25
compatibility and facilitate supplier switching . Without such regulatory interventions, the
combination of technical incompatibility, opaque pricing, and punitive egress fees will continue to
create a deeply entrenched market structure where customer choice is severely constrained.
Pricing Mechanisms and Loyalty Incentives
Beyond the structural barriers and technical complexities that impede supplier switching, competition
authorities have identified several pricing mechanisms and promotional incentives that function as
                                                                                                                    127
powerful tools to entrench the market dominance of hyperscalers and disadvantage their rivals .
Two of the most significant areas of concern are the widespread use of egress fees, which act as a
direct financial penalty for leaving a provider's ecosystem, and the deployment of large-scale cloud
credit programs, which serve as extreme loyalty rebates that lock in customers early in their cloud
             2 22
journey . While these practices may offer short-term benefits to some customers, such as lowering
initial adoption costs, they are viewed by regulators as exclusionary tactics that distort competition
and foreclose market access for equally efficient competitors who cannot afford to match the
                                             1 22
financial firepower of the hyperscalers             .

Egress fees are a particularly contentious issue because they directly penalize customers for exercising
                                                                                   1 22
their right to switch providers or adopt a multi-cloud strategy . As previously noted, providers
charge for the outbound transfer of data from their cloud infrastructure while typically offering free
                                10
inbound transfers . The magnitude of these fees is a point of intense debate. The UK's Competition
and Markets Authority (CMA) found that egress fees charged by AWS, Microsoft, and Google are
                                                                                                                          8
around 5-10 times higher than those charged by some smaller providers like OVHcloud and Oracle .
More alarmingly, authorities have cited instances where these fees are marked up by as much as
                                                              22
8000% above the actual cost of data transfer . This practice transforms the decision to switch
clouds from a simple business calculation into a potentially ruinous financial undertaking, especially
                                       8
for customers with large datasets . The CMA's own analysis found that for some customers, the
egress fees incurred when switching providers could amount to 15–30% of their annual cloud
                                                        24
spend, a significant deterrent to migration . While providers argue that these fees are justified as a
cost-recovery mechanism for bandwidth and infrastructure, regulators see them as a commercial
barrier designed to limit competitive responsiveness and weaken supplier competition for rivals'
                    79
customers . The practice disproportionately affects smaller customers and those with large data
                                                                                                     7
storage requirements, as the fees are highest relative to their total cloud spending . Although the
removal of egress fees prompted by the EU Data Act has been noted, it did not lead to a significant
uptick in switching rates, suggesting that the underlying technical and contractual barriers remain the
                                                         13
primary impediments to supplier switching .

Another powerful tool used by hyperscalers to lock in customers is the provision of substantial cloud
                                                              1 22
credits, particularly to startups and scale-ups                      . These programs offer customers hundreds of
                                                                                                                     1
thousands of euros worth of discounted or free cloud services over a period of months or years .
For example, in France, cloud credits can amount to up to $200,000 over two years, while in the UK,
Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud offer credits of up to $150,000 and $100,000 per year,
                         1 22
respectively . While these credits can help lower the barrier to entry for new customers and
encourage experimentation with the provider's platform, they also function as extreme loyalty rebates
2 22
       . Smaller competitors are unable to match these offers profitably, creating a significant barrier to
                                                17
market access for equally efficient rivals . By offering such generous credits, hyperscalers effectively
subsidize the adoption of their ecosystem, encouraging customers to build their workloads on their
platform from the outset. This early commitment makes it far more difficult and costly for those
customers to switch to a rival provider later on, as they would lose the value of the credits already
         2 22
received . Some programs are even conditional on exclusivity clauses or substantial purchase
commitments, further cementing the relationship and acting as a powerful deterrent to exploring
                     22
alternative solutions . The French Competition Authority has raised concerns that these practices
risk locking customers into a provider’s ecosystem, especially when combined with training
                                                                      3
programs for developers that build skills specific to that platform . While the CMA in the UK does
not currently consider these credit programs harmful to competition, it has acknowledged that this
could change if the value or scope of the credits increases in the future, signaling a continued
                             7
watchfulness on this issue .

These pricing and incentive structures are part of a broader set of practices that undermine fair
competition. Committed spend agreements (CSAs), where customers agree to a minimum level of
                                                                 68
spending in exchange for deeper discounts, are also widespread . While CSAs themselves may not
be inherently anti-competitive, they incentivize customers to concentrate their spending with a single
provider to maximize savings, which reduces the incentive to adopt multi-cloud strategies and
                                        78
weakens competitive pressure on rivals . The combination of punitive egress fees, which penalize
movement, and generous cloud credits, which reward loyalty, creates a powerful feedback loop that
reinforces the market position of the hyperscalers. This dynamic is exacerbated by the lack of
transparency in pricing, which makes it difficult for customers to understand the full cost of their
                                                                              2 10
cloud consumption and hinders their ability to compare offerings effectively . Ultimately, these
mechanisms shift the balance of power decisively in favor of the incumbents, making it exceedingly
difficult for new or smaller providers to gain a foothold in a market where the rules are written by
the most powerful players.


Restrictive Licensing Practices and Ecosystem Leveraging
The most detailed and consequential area of regulatory scrutiny in the public cloud market revolves
around the software licensing and bundling practices of dominant hyperscalers, with Microsoft
                                                 2 6 22
emerging as the central focus of investigation . Competition authorities, particularly the UK's
Competition and Markets Authority (CMA), have concluded that Microsoft leverages its market
power in adjacent software markets—in particular, PC productivity software and server operating
                                                                              7 18 27
systems—to unfairly disadvantage its rivals in the cloud infrastructure space . This is achieved
primarily through restrictive Bring-Your-Own-License (BYOL) policies and the tying of its
communication and collaboration product, Microsoft Teams, to its dominant Office 365 and
                     14 19
Microsoft 365 suites . These actions are seen as a sophisticated form of leveraging dominance,
where a company's monopoly power in one market is used to erect barriers to entry and foreclose
                                               27 30
competition in a separate but related market           .

                                                                                                    19 20
The core of the controversy stems from Microsoft's changes to its BYOL policy in October 2019 .
Prior to this date, customers who owned licenses for Microsoft software like Windows Server and
SQL Server could deploy those licenses on any compatible third-party cloud infrastructure, including
                                                                       19
AWS and Google Cloud, without repurchasing them . This practice, known as true BYOL, allowed
customers to leverage their existing software investments and avoid vendor lock-in. However,
Microsoft introduced significant restrictions that designated AWS, Google, Alibaba, and Microsoft
                                               19
itself as "Listed Providers" . Under the new terms, running certain Microsoft software on these
listed providers became prohibitively expensive, forcing customers to either pay an additional fee of
80-100% of the original license price or repurchase the software entirely for use on non-Azure
              19 20
clouds                . For instance, AWS claims that this policy made running Windows Server up to four times
                                                                                                             18 21
more expensive on non-Azure clouds compared to running it on Azure . This differential pricing
directly raises the cost of competing against Azure for any customer with a significant Microsoft
                                                                                                                 7 25
workload, creating a powerful financial disincentive to use rival platforms . The CMA has
provisionally determined that these practices partially foreclose AWS and Google from competing
effectively, especially for workloads involving Microsoft software, thereby harming competition in
                                             7 18 30
the cloud services market   . AWS estimates that 50% of Microsoft enterprise workloads on Azure
would migrate to AWS if these prohibitive licensing costs were removed, highlighting the significant
                                                                                          18 21
impact of this policy on customer choice and market dynamics                                      .

Beyond licensing, regulators are also intensely focused on Microsoft's bundling of Microsoft Teams
with its productivity suites. Following a complaint from Slack Technologies, the European
Commission opened a formal antitrust investigation in July 2023, alleging that Microsoft illegally tied
                                                                        2 14 35
Teams to Office 365 and Microsoft 365 subscriptions       . The practice involved automatically
including Teams in these suites without offering a standalone option, granting it a massive
distribution advantage and restricting competition from rival communication and collaboration tools
14 35 38
           . The Commission issued a Statement of Objections in June 2024, concluding that this conduct
                                                                                                      2 35
likely breached Article 102 TFEU by abusing its dominant position . Microsoft responded by
unbundling Teams for new customers in the EEA and globally, but regulators deemed these
                                                                                  35 37
remedies insufficient to address the competition concerns . This case exemplifies the broader
regulatory concern that dominant tech firms leverage their control over essential software to
dominate adjacent markets, such as cloud collaboration, by making it difficult for competitors to gain
                       36
a foothold . The integration of Teams with Office 365 is seen as a classic example of tying, where a
dominant product (Office 365) is used to promote and protect a weaker one (Teams), hindering
                                                           36 38
innovation and reducing customer choice                            .

Microsoft vigorously disputes these allegations, arguing that its practices are legitimate business
                                                                                                                        18 23
decisions and that competition authorities have misjudged the market dynamics . Microsoft's
defense rests on several key arguments. First, it contends that rivals like AWS and Google have
ample profit margins to absorb the higher input costs from its SPLA licensing agreements and
                                     18 23
remain competitive . An economic analysis commissioned by Microsoft suggests that even under a
worst-case scenario, Google would still have healthy gross margins when accounting for SPLA costs
13
  . Second, Microsoft argues that the cloud market is highly dynamic and competitive, driven by rapid
innovation in artificial intelligence, and that excessive regulation could stifle this progress and harm
                       13 17
consumers                      . Third, it points out that Linux is the dominant operating system in the cloud (over
                                                                                                                                19 23
75% share), suggesting that its restrictive licensing policies are irrelevant for most workloads                                        .
Finally, Microsoft cites survey data showing that only a minority of customers perceive licensing
terms as a primary factor in their cloud provider choice, arguing that the impact of its practices is
               13 23
overstated . Despite these counterarguments, the weight of evidence presented by the CMA and
supported by competitors like Google indicates that Microsoft's licensing and bundling strategies are
                                                                        17 29
having a demonstrable adverse effect on competition . The ongoing regulatory battles over these
practices are pivotal, as they will set important precedents for how regulators address ecosystem
leveraging by dominant technology firms in the future.


Regulatory Scrutiny and Future Outlook
The collective findings of competition authorities across the globe signal a definitive conclusion: the
public cloud computing market is failing to function competitively, necessitating significant
                                2 6 10
regulatory intervention . This consensus has manifested in a wave of market investigations, formal
inquiries, and proposed legislative reforms aimed at dismantling the structural barriers and addressing
                                                                                       5 16
the exclusionary practices that entrench the dominance of hyperscalers . The future outlook for the
market is therefore defined by a tension between the industry's drive for innovation and the
regulators' mandate to enforce fair competition. The outcome of these regulatory actions will likely
reshape the competitive landscape, potentially altering how hyperscalers do business, interact with
customers, and compete with one another.

In the United Kingdom, the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) has launched a
comprehensive market investigation into public cloud infrastructure services, initiated after a referral
                                                            6 26
from the communications regulator Ofcom . The CMA's provisional findings, published in January
2025, identified several adverse effects on competition (AECs), including high market concentration,
significant switching barriers, and the anti-competitive impact of Microsoft's software licensing
            6 7 25
practices            . The CMA's final decision report, expected in August 2025, is anticipated to recommend
                                                 6 17
proactive regulatory measures . A key recommendation is to initiate a Strategic Market Status
(SMS) investigation under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers (DMCC) Act, which
                                                                                     7 16
could lead to the designation of Microsoft and AWS as "gatekeepers" . Such a designation would
subject them to ex ante obligations requiring, for example, greater transparency in pricing, fair
treatment of third parties, and enhanced data portability, aiming to curb their ability to leverage
                                         26 29
dominance across markets . The CMA has also recommended using its existing powers to impose
binding remedies, such as requiring fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory licensing terms to
                                                                   26
address the discriminatory pricing that harms rivals .

Similarly, the French Autorité de la concurrence has issued a final opinion condemning the market's
failure to compete, citing customer lock-in, opaque pricing, and exclusionary practices like egress fees
                          25
and cloud credits . The authority endorsed the EU's Digital Markets Act (DMA) and the upcoming
Data Act as crucial tools to remedy these market failures, calling for stronger enforcement of
                                                                                12
interoperability standards and provisions to reduce lock-in risks . It has signaled its intent to use
competition law to investigate potential breaches by cloud providers, indicating that litigation
                                                        3
investigations may follow its market study . In South Korea, the Korea Fair Trade Commission
(KFTC) concluded its market study by recommending closer examination of providers' transaction
practices, particularly concerning self-preferential treatment and unfavorable terms imposed on
           11
customers . The KFTC plans to monitor international legislative efforts and assess effective
measures to strengthen competitive pressures in the Korean market, emphasizing the importance of
                                              10
ensuring data portability and interoperability . At the European level, the European Commission is
conducting a formal antitrust investigation into Microsoft's bundling of Teams with its productivity
suites, issuing a statement of objections in June 2024 and continuing its market testing to assess the
                                             2 22 35
adequacy of Microsoft's proposed remedies        . The Commission is also closely monitoring
Microsoft's cloud licensing practices following complaints from European cloud service
                20
organizations .

Looking ahead, the future of competition in the cloud market will be shaped by the implementation
of these regulatory initiatives. The EU's Digital Markets Act (DMA), which applies broadly to cloud
computing services, represents a landmark piece of legislation that could fundamentally alter the rules
                                                   16
of engagement for the largest digital platforms . If designated under the DMA, hyperscalers would
be subject to a range of conduct obligations designed to prevent abusive practices and ensure fair
access to their platforms for third parties. This could include rules preventing them from using
customer data without consent, requiring interoperability with competing services, and prohibiting
                     2 16
self-preferencing . The EU Data Act, expected to take effect from late 2025, will further bolster
competition by mandating improved customer switching capabilities and establishing European
interoperability standards, directly addressing the technical and contractual barriers that currently
                      25
lock in customers . These regulations, combined with the ongoing market investigations in the UK
and France, signal a decisive shift towards more proactive and interventionist approaches to
regulating the digital economy.

However, the path forward is not without challenges and counterarguments. The hyperscalers
themselves have expressed strong opposition to what they view as disproportionate regulation that
                                              17 29
could stifle innovation and deter investment . They argue that the cloud market is dynamic and
highly competitive, driven by rapid advancements in AI, and that regulatory intervention could make
                                                                                               17 29
their home countries "global outliers" with negative consequences for their competitiveness .
Industry groups like ICLE have also cautioned against broad designations of firms as "strategic
market status" under the DMCC Act, arguing that such interventions should be targeted and
proportionate, distinguishing between legitimate business justifications and actual anti-competitive
       9
effects . Despite these objections, the evidence gathered by competition authorities suggests that the
market is not self-correcting and that significant government action is required to restore a level
playing field. The ultimate success of these regulatory efforts will depend on their ability to
effectively dismantle the technical, contractual, and financial barriers to entry and switching, thereby
fostering a more vibrant and innovative ecosystem where customer choice and fair competition are
paramount.
