Asian Nations: A Comparative Analysis of System
Design and Coverage
National Pension System Profiles
The national pension systems across Indonesia, Malaysia, Pakistan, the Philippines, Sri Lanka,
Thailand, and Vietnam exhibit significant diversity in their structure, coverage, and regulatory
frameworks. These systems are primarily composed of mandatory and voluntary schemes designed
to provide retirement income security. Mandatory schemes are typically linked to formal employment
and require contributions from both employers and employees, while voluntary schemes offer
supplementary savings options for those not covered by mandatory programs or for those wishing to
enhance their retirement nest egg. The design of these systems reveals a common challenge: ensuring
adequate and sustainable retirement income for a growing proportion of aging populations while
managing fiscal pressures and extending coverage to vast informal economies. This section provides
a detailed profile of the key pension schemes available in each country as of early 2025.

Comparative Analysis of Mandatory Defined Benefit Schemes
The effectiveness of any pension system hinges critically on the design of its defined benefit (DB)
schemes, which promise a specific retirement income. The parameters governing these schemes—
such as accrual rates, salary bases, benefit caps, and service requirements—are fundamental drivers of
retirement adequacy. An analysis of the mandatory DB schemes in the selected Asian countries
reveals a consistent pattern of structural features that often result in replacement rates below
internationally recommended standards. These designs reflect historical policy choices but
increasingly pose challenges in the face of rising life expectancy and demographic shifts.

The mandatory DB schemes in this region generally employ a career-average formula, where the final
pension amount is a percentage of the worker's average earnings multiplied by years of service.
However, the specific mechanics vary significantly. In Indonesia, the BPJS – JP scheme for private-
sector employees provides a clear example of a simple accrual-based formula: the annual pension is
calculated as 1% of the weighted average annual salary earned during the contribution period,
                                         6 137
adjusted annually for inflation . This straightforward approach ensures predictability but limits the
potential for high replacement rates unless combined with very long careers. The scheme sets explicit
minimum and maximum monthly benefits, which were IDR 300,000 and IDR 4,792,300 respectively
                                                                                                6 54
as of March 2025, creating a cap on pension size regardless of salary history . A minimum of 15
years of contributions is required to qualify for the monthly pension, otherwise a lump sum is paid
6 32
  . Similarly, the public sector scheme for civil servants under Taspen offers a capped replacement
rate of up to 80% of their final basic salary per year of service, though this is supplemented by a
                                    40
mandatory DC component .

Pakistan's EOBI scheme utilizes a slightly different calculation for its Old-Age Pension: the benefit is
determined by multiplying 2% of the insured worker's average monthly earnings in the last year of
                                                                                       8 137
employment by the total number of years of insurable employment . This formula places
significant weight on recent earnings. The scheme has a minimum monthly pension of PKR 11,500,
increased from PKR 10,000 in January 2025, and a maximum benefit is set at PKR 30,000 for long-
                       96 104
serving contributors            . As with Indonesia, a 15-year contribution record is the threshold for
                                                                              78
receiving a monthly pension instead of a lump sum gratuity . The public sector pension system, in
contrast, is a tax-financed DB plan where benefits are determined by a complex set of rules tied to
                                                                                    3 109
rank and length of service, with no direct employee contributions                           .

In the Philippines, the GSIS for public sector employees calculates the basic monthly pension as
37.5% of the revalued average monthly compensation (RAMC) for the first 15 years of service, plus
                                                                    72 141
an additional 2.5% for each year of service beyond that                      . This results in a maximum pension of
                                                          115 168
60% for officers with 30 or more years of service       . The RAMC is a crucial factor, as it adjusts
past salaries for inflation and other factors, making the benefit dependent on the entire career
                                                           144
trajectory rather than just recent earnings . The SSS for the private sector combines a defined
benefit element with a defined contribution one, but its primary retirement payout is calculated using
a formula that compares two values: a base amount plus a small percentage of average earnings, or
                                                                 72 143
40% of average earnings, whichever is higher . This structure can lead to higher replacement rates
for some workers compared to pure DB schemes.

Sri Lanka's Public Servants’ Pension Scheme (PSPS) is a classic pay-as-you-go DB plan where
benefits are not pre-funded by individual accounts but are paid directly from current government
                     12 150
revenues                      . The pension accrues based on service years, with a typical replacement rate of around
                                                                                                                       168
2% per year of service, leading to a maximum of 60% for those with 30+ years of service . The
actual pension amount is highly dependent on the "pensionable pay," which is defined by various
                                                                                                                                       12
circulars and regulations, and the "retirement channel" (normal retirement, medical invalidity, etc.) .
                                                                                                 12
The scheme is financed from current revenues rather than an invested fund . The private sector
does not have a comparable mandatory DB scheme; instead, private workers rely on DC plans like
                                                                 14
the EPF, which provides a lump sum payout .

Thailand's Social Security Fund (SSF) Article 33 scheme for private sector workers provides a
                                                                                                  64 91
lifetime monthly pension to those who have contributed for at least 15 years . The benefit formula
is calculated as 20% of the average wage over the last 60 months of employment, plus an additional
1.5% for each year of service beyond the initial 15 years, up to a maximum replacement rate of 50%
64 146
   . This reliance on a short, final-period average salary is a notable feature, as it may undervalue the
earnings of workers whose wages grew substantially over their careers. If a worker has fewer than 15
                                                                                                                             64 146
years of contributions, they receive a lump-sum gratuity equal to the total contributions made .
The civil service pension system is also a non-contributory, tax-financed DB scheme, providing
                                                                                                          64 155
either a lump sum or a lifetime annuity based on years of service and final salary                                 .

Vietnam's Viet Nam Social Security (VSS) scheme uses a formula that pays 45% of the insured
person's average monthly covered earnings for the first 20 years of contributions for men (or 15
                                                                                                                                  23
years for women), plus an additional 2% for each subsequent year, with a maximum cap of 75% .
This structure aims to reward longer careers but is still subject to a significant ceiling. The scheme is
undergoing reforms to improve sustainability, including lowering the required contribution period to
15 years and adjusting the benefit formula to be less generous per year of service beyond a certain
         23 25
point            .

These diverse formulas highlight a common theme: the structural limitations of many mandatory DB
schemes. Low accrual rates, reliance on short-term salary averages, and explicit benefit caps combine
to produce replacement rates that are often insufficient to maintain a decent standard of living in
retirement. For instance, actuarial studies indicate that for average earners, net replacement rates in
Sri Lanka are as low as 22.9% for women and 34.6% for men, well below the 40% adequacy
                                                                                           137
threshold recommended by the International Labour Organization (ILO) . While the Philippines
boasts one of the highest gross replacement rates at 72.4%, this is partly due to its unique hybrid
                                                    137
model and may not be sustainable . The inherent design of these systems, particularly the pay-as-
you-go models in the public sector of Pakistan and Sri Lanka, poses significant long-term fiscal risks

as populations age           .

Retirement Access Points Across Asia: A Cross-Country
Comparison
The normal retirement age is a critical parameter in any pension system, defining the earliest point at
which individuals can access their retirement savings. This age, along with provisions for early
retirement, shapes financial planning, labor market participation, and the overall sustainability of
pension funds. Across the seven analyzed Asian countries, there is a wide divergence in retirement
ages, reflecting different demographic realities, economic conditions, and policy priorities. Some
nations exhibit significant gender disparities in retirement eligibility, while others are actively
implementing phased increases to align with rising life expectancies and ensure fiscal solvency.

Indonesia has a structured, gradual increase in its retirement age. As of January 1, 2025, the normal
retirement age for accessing the mandatory Jaminan Pensiun (JP) program is 59 years for both men
            30 47
and women           . This age is scheduled to increase incrementally by one year every three years until it
                         55 132
reaches 65 by 2043                . This planned adjustment is a direct response to the country's aging
                                                     39 49
population and increasing life expectancy                    . For the mandatory JHT defined contribution scheme,
                                        6                                                                                6
the access age is lower, at 56 . Voluntary schemes have an even lower access age of 50 . This tiered
system creates a complex environment for workers, with the most favorable retirement terms offered
by the mandatory DC scheme.

Malaysia presents a dual-track system for retirement access. For members of the mandatory

Employees Provident Fund (EPF), the primary retirement access point is at age 55                                        . At this age,
                                                                                                                                83
balances from Accounts 1 and 2 are consolidated into Akaun 55, allowing for full withdrawal .

However, the official minimum retirement age for Malaysian citizens is 60 . For public sector
                                                                                                   115
employees under the KWAP scheme, retirement is compulsory at age 60 . Early retirement options
exist but often come with penalties; for example, retiring before age 60 from the KWAP scheme

EPF withdrawal age could improve retirement adequacy, highlighting ongoing debates about
                                                                    134 167
balancing access with long-term savings security                              .
In Pakistan, a notable gender disparity exists in retirement ages. For the private sector EOBI scheme,
                                                                                                              78

                                                                                                              10

                                                                                         160

The Philippines maintains equal retirement ages for men and women across its major pension
schemes. For both the SSS and GSIS, the optional retirement age is 60, with mandatory retirement at
         72 143

60 to 63 for private-sector employees and from 65 to 67 for public-sector employees, starting in 2025

  . This reform is explicitly aimed at aligning retirement with rising life expectancy and ensuring the

Sri Lanka also has distinct retirement ages for its public and private sectors. The mandatory
retirement age for both public and private sector employees is 60 years, having been raised from 55

Thailand exhibits a clear distinction between its private and public sectors. The normal retirement
age for private sector workers under the mandatory Social Security Fund (SSF) Article 33 scheme is
                                                                   64 91
55 years, with a requirement of at least 15 years of contributions . In stark contrast, public sector
employees and recipients of the universal Old-Age Allowance (OAA) have a retirement age of 60
64 151
   . This difference underscores the fragmentation of the Thai pension system. The World Bank has
noted that Thailand's retirement age of 55 is among the lowest in Asia and OECD countries, creating
                                                                                               146

Vietnam is in the midst of a significant transition regarding retirement age. In 2022, the average
                                                                                   137

government has enacted legislation to gradually increase these ages to ensure sustainability. The
reform, effective July 1, 2025, schedules an increase of 4 months per year for men until the

retirement age reaches 62 by 2028, and 3 months per year for women until it reaches 60 by 2035 .
This proactive, long-term approach demonstrates a commitment to aligning the retirement age with
demographic trends.

This comparative overview reveals that while there is no regional consensus on retirement age, a
clear trend is emerging toward later retirement. Countries like the Philippines, Vietnam, and
Indonesia are actively implementing or planning phased increases. This policy lever is a crucial tool
for addressing the dual challenges of aging populations and the financial sustainability of pay-as-you-
go pension systems. However, the persistence of gender disparities in countries like Pakistan and the
sharp divide between public and private sectors in Sri Lanka and Thailand indicate areas where
further harmonization and reform are needed to create a more equitable and robust retirement
security framework.

Systemic Drivers of Inadequate Retirement Security
The significant retirement savings protection gaps observed across Indonesia, Malaysia, Pakistan, the
Philippines, Sri Lanka, Thailand, and Vietnam are not merely incidental outcomes but are deeply
rooted in the structural design and implementation of their respective pension systems. An analysis
focusing on systemic drivers reveals four primary interconnected factors: inadequate coverage that
excludes a majority of the workforce, particularly in the informal economy; inherent flaws in benefit
formulas and contribution rates that limit retirement income adequacy; premature access to
retirement funds that erodes long-term capital; and unsustainable funding models threatened by
demographic pressures. Together, these drivers create a precarious foundation for retirement security
in the region.

The most profound driver of protection gaps is the failure of pension systems to achieve universal
coverage. In virtually all seven countries, a substantial portion of the working-age population,
especially those in the informal sector, lacks access to any form of structured retirement savings.
Pakistan stands out with the lowest coverage, where only 6% of the population aged 15-64 is covered
by old-age income support, and only about 12.4% of the labor force is registered with the EOBI
3 137 138
            . Similarly, Sri Lanka and Indonesia report coverage rates of 17.6% and 16.5% of the population,
                    137
respectively . Even in more advanced economies like Malaysia, coverage is limited to 32.8% of the
                   137
population . This exclusion is often institutionalized through eligibility criteria tied to formal
employment. In Pakistan, the EOBI Act originally applied to firms with five or more workers,
though a 2012 court ruling pushed this threshold to ten, excluding the vast majority of small
                  8 10
businesses               . In Indonesia, small enterprise workers were historically excluded from the mandatory
                                                                                                              40
defined benefit JP scheme, and micro-enterprise workers from the mandatory DC JHT scheme .
This reality means that as long as a large share of the workforce remains outside the formal economy,
a massive protection gap will persist, leaving millions of workers without a reliable source of income
in their later years.

For those who are formally covered, the structure of the pension schemes themselves often results in
inadequate retirement income. Many mandatory defined benefit schemes suffer from low accrual
                                                                       6 72
rates, typically ranging from 1% to 2.5% per year of service . This slow build-up of benefits means
that decades of work are required to accumulate a meaningful pension. When coupled with
contribution rates that are sometimes insufficient to generate adequate assets—such as Indonesia's
relatively low 8.7% combined rate or Pakistan's employer contribution of 5% of the minimum wage
                                                                       8 39
—replacement rates fall far short of international standards . Furthermore, many schemes impose
caps on the salary base used for calculations, disproportionately benefiting low-income workers while
                                                                              132 146
leaving high earners with lower returns on their contributions       . This creates a two-tiered system
that undermines the principle of solidarity. The result, as seen in Sri Lanka, is a gross replacement
rate of only 31.9% for men and 21.1% for women, figures that are dangerously low for maintaining a
                                                          137
comfortable standard of living post-retirement .

Another critical driver is the ease of premature access to retirement funds, which systematically
undermines the goal of providing a stable, lifelong income. Several countries' systems encourage or
permit the depletion of long-term savings before retirement. In Indonesia, the JHT defined
contribution scheme allows for full withdrawal after just five years of service, a rule that has led to
                                                                                        36 40
high rates of pre-retirement withdrawals that erode fund accumulation . Malaysia's EPF allows
partial withdrawals from Account 2 at age 50 and full access at 55, again promoting liquidity over
                              83 136
long-term security . Perhaps most damaging is the practice of providing lump-sum gratuity
payouts to workers with less than the required 15 years of contributions, as seen in Thailand's SSF
64 146
  . This immediate cash-out encourages a consumption mindset and leaves individuals vulnerable to
