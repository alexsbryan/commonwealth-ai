Analysis of Mary Lou Williams's Educational
Work (1970-1981)
Educational Activities and Teaching Philosophy at Duke University
and Beyond
Mary Lou Williams’s final decade, from the early 1970s until her death in 1981, represents a period
of profound transformation in her career, shifting her focus from that of a pioneering performer to
                                                                                                                        6 10
that of a dedicated educator, historian, and spiritual guide for the art form she had helped shape .
This phase of her life was characterized by an intense, mission-driven commitment to preserving and
                                                                    1
transmitting the lineage, history, and soul of jazz . Her educational activities were not confined to
traditional classroom settings but expanded into a dynamic and multifaceted pedagogical practice that
                                                                                                            1 15
included university residencies, nationwide lecture-recitals, and intimate private lessons . Central to
this endeavor was her appointment as the first artist-in-residence at Duke University in 1977, a
position that served as both a culmination of her artistic journey and a powerful platform for her
                   7 8 11
educational vision . Her teaching philosophy was deeply personal, intuitive, and experiential,
emphasizing a holistic understanding of jazz that transcended technical proficiency to embrace its
                                            13 15
cultural and spiritual foundations                  . She believed that to truly play jazz, one must first feel it, a
                                                                                                                    1 13
principle she instilled through storytelling, live performance, and rigorous historical grounding                          .

Williams’s tenure at Duke University from 1977 until her passing in 1981 was a landmark event in
                                  78
the history of jazz education . Appointed as the university's inaugural artist-in-residence, she was
also named director of the Duke Jazz Ensemble, a role that allowed her to directly influence a new
                            678
generation of musicians . This appointment was more than an academic honor; it was the
realization of a long-held ambition to create a structured environment where young people could
                                                                1
learn about jazz and appreciate its deep lineage . Her impact on the campus was immediate and
transformative. Students flocked to her classes, which often had long waiting lists, indicating their
                                                                        8
popularity and the perceived value of her instruction . One student described her as a "treasure,"
                                                                                                7
highlighting her ability to help them grasp the complex history of the music . Williams became a
pivotal figure on campus, emerging as "one of the focal points for Black students and culture" during
                                             78
a time of significant social change . Her presence symbolized the university's broader efforts to
                                                                            7
foster inclusivity and welcome Black faculty and students . The enduring legacy of her work at Duke
is most powerfully embodied by the Mary Lou Williams Center for Black Culture, established in
                                       7 10 11
1983, two years after her death  . Originally known as "The Lou," the center has served as a central
hub for Black student life, community-building, and cultural expression for four decades, standing as
                                                                        78
a testament to her lasting influence on the university and its students . Today, the center continues
                                                                                                         8
her educational mission through weekly "Jazz@ the Mary Lou" performances held in her honor .

Beyond directing the ensemble, Williams co-taught a comprehensive course on jazz history with
Father Peter O’Brien, a Jesuit priest who had become her close friend, spiritual advisor, and
         3 13 28
manager    . This collaboration was crucial, blending O’Brien’s theological and administrative
acumen with Williams’s unparalleled lived experience as a musician who had witnessed and
                                                  13 29
participated in the evolution of jazz firsthand . Their partnership ensured that her lectures were
not merely historical accounts but were imbued with the moral and spiritual depth she believed was
essential to the music. Williams’s curriculum at Duke evolved from her earlier public
performances, such as her solo show reflecting on her decades in the industry, which later formed
                                                               9
the basis of her educational program at the university . Her teaching extended beyond the university,
as she engaged in lecture-recitals and workshops across the country, particularly during college
                                                                                  1
breaks, further cementing her role as a national advocate for jazz education . She leveraged major
media platforms, including appearances on 'The Today Show' and 'Mr. Rogers’ Neighborhood', to
                                                                    6
promote jazz and raise public awareness of its cultural significance . Through these varied activities,
Williams constructed a robust pedagogical framework designed to create educated audiences and
                                                                             12
ensure the survival of what she considered a sacred American art form .

The hallmark of Mary Lou Williams's teaching methodology was the "lecture-recital," a unique and
                                                                                               1 15
immersive format that combined narrative exposition with live musical demonstration . Unlike
conventional lectures, her sessions were performative histories, transforming abstract timelines into
                                                          15
visceral, embodied experiences for her audience . During these recitals, she would narrate the story
of jazz's evolution while simultaneously playing key pieces from each era, effectively acting as both
                                    15
instructor and orchestra conductor . Student Bill Cunliffe described the experience as informal and
                                                                                          13
deeply connected, emphasizing the sense of community over formal instruction . She would often
                                                                                                      13 15
incorporate imitations of famous musicians, bringing their styles vividly to life for her listeners .
For example, she might demonstrate the stride piano of Fats Waller or the bebop innovations of Bud
                                                                                  14 15
Powell, using her own virtuosic playing to illustrate historical developments . This method allowed
her to connect students not just to the notes on a page but to the living traditions and personalities
that shaped them. Her 1977 live lecture at the Keystone Korner, titled "The History of Jazz
According to Mary Lou," exemplifies this approach, recorded and later released as part of the album
                               20
'Live at the Keystone Korner' . Similarly, her Carnegie Hall duet with avant-garde pianist Cecil
Taylor in 1977 was framed as a historical performance, attempting to bridge the gap between
                                                          18
traditional and experimental jazz through music . At Duke, these class-based lecture-recitals were
immensely popular, attracting over 700 students and solidifying her reputation as a beloved and
                   29
effective teacher .

At the heart of Williams’s pedagogical philosophy was a belief in learning through feeling and
             1
immersion . She argued that true understanding of jazz required an emotional connection to its
                                             34
origins in suffering and spiritual expression . To facilitate this, she insisted that students sing
through each era of jazz, from spirituals and blues to bop, believing that internalizing the emotional
                                                                   1
content was a prerequisite for authentic performance . She understood that without this emotional
                                                                                          5
engagement, music risked becoming mechanical and devoid of its essential spirit . If a student’s
blues performance sounded cold, she would repeat the material, sometimes returning to the spirituals
                                                               1
to re-establish the foundational emotional context . This cyclical approach underscored her view
that jazz was a continuous tradition where the past was always present. Her teaching was intuitive
and story-based, drawing on her vast personal knowledge and anecdotes about legendary figures like
                                                13
Erroll Garner and Dizzy Gillespie . This experiential method was starkly different from academic
                                                                                              13
analysis; she prioritized connection and lived experience over intellectual abstraction . Even in her
private lessons, this philosophy was evident. When teaching pianist Sumi Tonooka in her Harlem
apartment in the late 1970s, Williams began by demanding a thorough understanding of jazz history
                                          13
before any playing commenced . She required students to comprehend the historical significance of
the very spaces where the music was born, such as the salons in her Harlem apartment where bebop
                                                                                         13
pioneers like Thelonious Monk, Bud Powell, and Dizzy Gillespie once gathered . Her teaching
method involved physical closeness and imitation, having students sit beside her as she played,
mirroring how she herself learned from her mother—a process she described as intense, non-
                                               13
intellectual, and deeply embodied .

This emphasis on historical grounding and foundational skills was a cornerstone of her teaching. She
championed a linear progression through jazz history, believing that skipping developmental steps
led to superficial musicianship. A telling anecdote from 1980 illustrates this principle perfectly. While
mentoring a National Endowment For The Arts Artist-In-Residence, she listened for only 30
                                                                       14
seconds to his fast bebop playing before stopping him . She instructed him to abandon the complex
bebop lines and return to the fundamentals of stride piano, specifically citing Fats Waller as a critical
                                   14
root he needed to master . She reinforced this lesson by stating, "You need to go back to Fats
Waller," a mantra she applied consistently to other musicians, guiding pianist Hilton Ruiz "all the way
                       14
back to Fats Waller" . This insistence on mastering early jazz styles before advancing to more
                                                                            14
complex ones reflects her belief in building a "solid foundation" . This principle was not limited to
music; she extended it metaphorically to societal issues, suggesting that progress in areas like public
                                                                                                         14
health, racial justice, and climate change also required a firm grasp of foundational principles . By
positioning herself as a direct link to the founders of bebop—stating she had taught Bud Powell and
Monk—she established her own authority as a pedagogue and emphasized her role as a custodian of
                            7 14
the music's core values . Her entire educational project, therefore, can be seen as a missionary
effort to transmit these values, ensuring that future generations of jazz musicians would not only be
technically proficient but also spiritually attuned to the music's profound heritage.

The 'Jazz Tree': A Visual Blueprint for Music History and Moral
Values
Mary Lou Williams’s most iconic and enduring contribution to jazz historiography is undoubtedly
the 'Jazz Tree,' a visual diagram she developed and used extensively in her lectures and writings
                      124
during her final years . Created in collaboration with artist David Stone Martin in 1977, the Jazz
Tree is far more than a simple timeline; it is a comprehensive philosophical and theological statement
                                                                     11 29
about the origins, evolution, and ultimate purpose of jazz . The diagram serves as a powerful
mnemonic device, encapsulating her entire worldview on the music, codifying her beliefs about its
                                                                                                        6 11
sacred origins, its healthy lineage, and the corrupting influences she perceived as threats to its soul .
Its structure is meticulously symbolic, with each component—the roots, trunk, branches, and leaves
—carrying a specific meaning that visually articulates her unique perspective on the African
                                                         23
American experience and the art form born from it . By analyzing the detailed components of this
diagram, one gains profound insight into Williams’s role as a guardian of jazz history, tasked with
                                                                               4 15
preserving its spiritual integrity against what she saw as modern deviations          .
The foundational element of the Jazz Tree is its roots, which are unequivocally labeled
                   3 11 29
'SUFFERING'                  . This single word, repeated across multiple sources, encapsulates Williams’s
                                               3
core belief about the genesis of jazz . She asserted that the music could never be separated from the
pain and hardship of the African-American experience, tracing its deepest spiritual dimension directly
                                                                                   4 11
back to the suffering of enslaved people and the legacies of slavery . As she stated, "only out of
suffering is a true thing born," a sentiment that grounds the entire edifice of her historical narrative
                                           4
in the crucible of human struggle . This concept of suffering as the source of artistic creation is
central to her theology of jazz. It explains why she viewed jazz as inherently spiritual and healing; the
music was born from a place of profound pain, yet it expressed sincere joy and outcry, offering a
                                                        24
path toward catharsis and spiritual uplift . The blues, which run up both sides of the tree, are
                                                                                                     3
depicted as originating from these roots, representing the manifestation of this suffering . For
Williams, the blues was the "spiritual feeling" in jazz, the "healing" component that transformed pain
                                                        3 21
into something beautiful and redemptive . This deep-seated conviction—that jazz's power derives
directly from its painful origins—was a recurring theme in her essays, diaries, and public statements,
                                                                34
forming the bedrock of her entire educational mission .

Ascending from these foundational roots is the trunk of the tree, which represents the main body of
                                                                        123
jazz and its progressive evolution through distinct stylistic eras            . The trunk is marked in ascending
                                                                                                              123
order with the key stages of this development: Spirituals, Ragtime, Kansas City Swing, and Bop .
This sequence constitutes what Williams defined as the healthy, vital lineage of jazz, the path that
                                                         1 11
leads to its greatest expressions of genius . The inclusion of Spirituals as the first stage emphasizes
her belief that they are the purest, most foundational form of the music, stemming directly from the
                               34
suffering of the roots . Following the Spirituals, Ragtime represents an important transitional phase,
incorporating European harmonic structures with distinctly African American rhythmic sensibilities
12
     . Kansas City Swing is highlighted as a crucial bridge, connecting the big-band orchestration of the
                                                                              13
swing era with the harmonic and rhythmic complexities of bebop . Finally, Bebop (Bop) is
presented as the pinnacle of this evolutionary line, representing a highly sophisticated and innovative
                         13
stage of the music . The outer part of the trunk is framed by the Blues, underscoring its integral role
                                                   11
in fueling the growth of the entire tree . This carefully curated lineage traces a path from raw,
communal spiritual expression to a complex, individualistic art form, all while maintaining a
connection to the foundational blues. Williams's emphasis on this specific path reveals her preference
for a jazz rooted in rhythm and emotion, evolving toward greater complexity without severing ties to
its origins.

Sprouting from the healthy branches of the trunk are the leaves of the tree, which bear the names of
                                                                                                              124
the great jazz musicians and composers who made significant contributions to this core lineage                      .
                                                                                                         11
These names serve as a genealogy of jazz genius, creating a visual family tree of the art form .
Among those listed on the leaves are towering figures such as Duke Ellington, J.P. Johnson, Fats
                                                                14
Waller, Charlie Parker, John Coltrane, and Miles Davis . The inclusion of these artists, from the
masters of the swing and early bebop eras to the revolutionary innovators of the mid-century,
signifies Williams's recognition of individual brilliance within the broader cultural context. She
celebrated the creativity of these individuals as the fruit of the healthy tree, the culmination of the
tradition's rich history. This aspect of the diagram highlights her performer-centered perspective on
                                                                                                 15
jazz scholarship, valuing the contributions of the artists who brought the music to life . However,
the diagram also contains a stark and dramatic contrast: on one side of the trunk, there are barren
                                                                                                  123
limbs bearing the labels of styles and concepts she deemed detrimental to jazz's vitality . These
severed branches represent what she called the "dead ends" of jazz, a clear moral judgment cast upon
certain contemporary trends. The labels on these dead limbs include 'avant-garde,' 'cults and black
                                                                  1 2 3 29
magic,' 'commercial rock,' 'mere exercises,' and 'classical books'    . This imagery is not a neutral
historical categorization; it is a condemnation. She viewed these movements as spiritually empty,
commercially corrupted, or intellectually sterile deviations that drained the feeling from the music
                                  1 15
and threatened its sacred essence . By visually separating these styles from the main trunk, she
positioned them as external parasites rather than legitimate offshoots of the tradition, reinforcing her
belief that true jazz must remain grounded in its emotional and spiritual core.

In summary, the Jazz Tree is a complete and self-contained world view. Its structure visually validates
                                                                                            28
Williams’s assertion that "Jazz and the spirituals are the only American-born art" . The roots of
'SUFFERING' provide the necessary conditions for the emergence of the Spirituals, which then feed
the growth of the trunk, representing the healthy progression of jazz through Ragtime, Kansas City
Swing, and Bop. The blues, running up the sides, nourishes this growth, leading to the flourishing
leaves of great individual artists. The dead limbs, laden with pejorative terms, serve as a constant
warning against straying from this path. The diagram was a practical tool used in her lectures at
churches and schools, but it was also a declaration of her identity as a historian and an evangelist for
    4
jazz . It was her way of sharing 'The History of Jazz' with audiences, often distributing printed
                                  5
handouts that outlined her views . Through this powerful image, she communicated a message that
was consistent throughout her final years: that the soul of jazz is inseparable from the African
American experience of suffering and resilience, and that its future depended on a return to these
spiritual roots.

Core Music Historiography: The Spiritual Origins and Evolution of
Jazz
Mary Lou Williams’s perspective on jazz history was not a detached academic exercise but a deeply
                                                                           4 15
felt, spiritual narrative rooted in her own biography and Catholic faith . Her historiography
provided a coherent and compelling explanation for the origin, nature, and purpose of jazz, a
                                                                 16
narrative that underpinned her entire educational mission . At the core of her worldview was the
unshakeable belief that jazz was fundamentally a product of the African American experience, born
                                                                                      34
from a wellspring of suffering and infused with a profound spiritual energy . She traced the music's
lineage with a clear, almost biblical, progression, identifying the Negro spiritual as its purest origin
and viewing subsequent styles like ragtime and Kansas City swing as natural evolutions of this
                           23
foundational expression . This narrative was not merely descriptive; it was evaluative. Williams used
her historical framework to praise styles that she felt remained true to the music's emotional and
spiritual core, while she delivered scathing critiques of post-bop developments like avant-garde jazz,
which she believed had abandoned its sacred roots in favor of technical abstraction and
                1 15
commercialism . Her historiography was thus a moral code, defining "good jazz" as music that
emanated from the mind, through the heart, and to the fingertips, expressing genuine feeling and
                                           35
serving a healing function for the soul .

The genesis of jazz, according to Mary Lou Williams, was inextricably linked to the spirituals, which
                                                      25
she identified as the main origin of American jazz . She articulated this belief forcefully, stating,
                                                            28
"Jazz and the spirituals are the only American-born art" . For Williams, this distinction was
paramount, as she saw the spirituals as a unique fusion of African rhythms and the deeply religious
                                    25
background of Black Americans . This combination, she believed, enabled the expression of sincere
                                                                                  2
joy and sorrow, creating a music that was inherently spiritual and healing . She explained that the
suffering experienced by enslaved people gave rise to the spirituals, which in turn provided the
                                                4
spiritual dimension for all subsequent jazz . This suffering was not merely a historical footnote but
the active source of the music's power. She wrote, "the suffering that gives jazz its spiritual
                                                                      4
dimension," and "Only out of suffering is a true thing born" . This perspective reframed the study
of jazz history as an exploration of the human capacity to find beauty and hope in the midst of
profound adversity. Her own spiritual conversion in the 1950s, which led her to compose sacred jazz
masses, profoundly influenced this view, allowing her to see her musical talent as a divine gift and her
                                         4 29
work as a vocation of service to God . This fusion of secular history and personal spirituality is
what gave her historiography its distinctive urgency and moral authority.
Within this framework, the blues occupied a central and indispensable role. Williams viewed the
blues not as mere "downtrodden songs" but as the living embodiment of the spiritual feeling that
                                         3 13
flowed from the roots of the Jazz Tree          . She described the blues as "really important—this is your
                                   3
healing and love in the music" . In her lectures, she often demonstrated this point by asserting,
                                                 21
"Without the blues in the music, it's cold" . This statement encapsulated her core pedagogical
principle: jazz without emotional depth was no longer jazz in her eyes. The blues was the vital
current that sustained the entire tree, connecting the foundational suffering to the creative branches
of the trunk. Improvisation, the defining characteristic of jazz, was for her a sacred process that
                                                                    35
flowed from the mind, through the heart, to the fingertips . She believed that without this mental
                                                                                            5
and emotional engagement, a performance became mechanical and spiritually hollow . True
improvisation, she suggested, was a form of telepathy among musicians, a shared spiritual
                                            3
communion that resulted in "good jazz" . This view placed a premium on feeling and sincerity above
technical virtuosity, a standard by which she judged the musicians and styles of her time. Her critique
of performers who were technically proficient but emotionally empty was a direct application of this
                                                                                3
principle, rejecting any definition of jazz that divorced sound from soul .

Williams’s evaluation of specific historical styles followed logically from her core beliefs. She traced
a clear and positive developmental arc through the major eras of jazz history. After the spirituals, she
recognized ragtime as a significant step forward, representing an early synthesis of African and
                              12
European musical traditions . Her particular emphasis on Kansas City swing was notable,
                                                                                                                  13
highlighting a vibrant and influential scene that bridged the big-band era and the bebop revolution .
She saw this style as a crucial link in the chain of jazz evolution, demonstrating a healthy progression
toward greater harmonic and rhythmic sophistication. Bebop, or Bop, was presented as the
culmination of this healthy lineage, representing the zenith of innovation and complexity within the
         13
tradition . She praised the musicians of this era, including Charlie Parker and Dizzy Gillespie, whom
                                                       7 13
she mentored and who she regarded as geniuses . Her inclusion of John Coltrane on the leaves of
her Jazz Tree further underscores her appreciation for the artistic heights reached during this period
1
 . Her historiography celebrates this specific path because it aligns with her vision of jazz as a music
that grew organically from its roots, developing increasingly complex structures while maintaining a
strong emotional core.

Conversely, her criticism was reserved for styles that she perceived as deviating from this sacred
                                                                                                       1 15
path. The most vehement and consistent target of her critique was Avant-Garde or "free" jazz                  .
                                                                         1 15
She labeled it a "dead end" that drained feeling from the music . In a widely cited quote describing
the genre, she claimed it was filled with "hate, bitterness, hysteria, black magic, confusion, discontent,
empty studies, musical exercises by various European composers, sounds of the earth, no ears, not
                     18
even relative pitch" . This language reveals a deep-seated rejection of what she saw as the nihilistic
and anti-emotional tendencies of some free jazz practitioners. She condemned it for reducing music
                                                                                                1 18
to sterile exercises and for abandoning the very idea of communication through feeling . Another
major target was commercial rock music, which she saw as a corrosive influence that weakened jazz's
                                                               14
artistic integrity and drew talent away from the tradition . Her opposition to these styles was not
merely aesthetic; it was moral and spiritual. She believed that true creativity required exposure to
great, feeling-based music, not to technical instruction alone or to the empty sounds of
                1
commercialism . This critical stance positioned her as a conservative force within the jazz world of
the 1970s, advocating for a return to the music's roots and defending a specific, emotionally resonant
conception of its identity. Her historiography, therefore, was not just a record of the past but a
manifesto for the future, arguing that the health of jazz depended on its unwavering connection to
the spiritual and emotional truths that gave it birth.


Evaluations of Contemporary Styles and Musicians: From Avant-
Garde Confrontation to Mainstream Legacy
Mary Lou Williams's final years were marked by a series of high-profile engagements that served as
public arenas for her passionate defense of jazz tradition. Her evaluations of contemporary styles and
musicians were not abstract opinions but were forged in the crucible of performance, collaboration,
and confrontation. The most dramatic and revealing of these was her infamous 1977 Carnegie Hall
concert with avant-garde pianist Cecil Taylor, a performance that crystallized her complex and often
                                                                                       18
contradictory relationship with the so-called "New Thing" movement . While her primary criticism
of free jazz remained uncompromising, her respect for certain individual artists like John Coltrane
revealed a nuanced understanding of the genre's potential. In contrast, her collaborations with
mainstream icons like Benny Goodman showcased a different facet of her artistic range,
                                                                                  18
demonstrating her mastery across the entire spectrum of jazz history . Throughout these
interactions, a consistent thread emerges: her judgments were guided by her core belief that jazz's
value lies in its capacity for spiritual expression and emotional communication. Any style or musician
that failed to meet this criterion was subject to her harshest critique, while those who could still
evoke the spirit of the tradition, regardless of their stylistic innovations, were granted a measure of
her respect.

The centerpiece of Williams's engagement with the avant-garde was the April 17, 1977, Carnegie Hall
                                                                                                 18
concert, a dual-piano event with Cecil Taylor that resulted in the album 'Embraced' . Williams
conceived the concert as an ambitious attempt at reconciliation, seeking to bridge the chasm between
                                                                                                 18
traditional jazz and the radical experimentation of the New York avant-garde scene . She arranged
for Taylor to perform her written arrangements of spirituals in the first half of the concert, framing
                                                                             18
the piece within her familiar "history of jazz" pedagogical approach . However, rehearsals quickly
revealed irreconcilable differences in artistic philosophy. Taylor objected to being given written parts
and to the inclusion of Williams's own rhythm section (bassist Bob Cranshaw and drummer Mickey
                                                                                            18
Roker) without consultation, leading to a breakdown in collaborative trust . The resulting
                                                        18
performance was described by critics as a "tug of war" . During the piece "Back to the Blues,"
Taylor ventured into extreme avant-garde territory, forcing Williams to use her powerful left-hand
                                                             18
playing to forcefully reclaim the melody and restore order . When overwhelmed, she famously
"called in the troops" by bringing in her rhythm section to defend the structure of the piece, a
                                                                   18
moment that underscored the tension between their approaches . The concert was poorly attended,
                                                                        18
and despite her fundraising efforts, it barely broke even financially . Backstage, tensions escalated
into near-violence, with her manager Peter O’Brien slamming the door on Taylor and Williams
                                                                         18
delivering a blistering account of the ordeal to O’Brien afterward . In a private letter to Taylor
two years later, she expressed deep hurt, interpreting his actions as spiritually destructive and
                                                                              18
questioning his motives for repeatedly visiting her club performances . Yet, this harsh critique is
complicated by a crucial nuance: Williams maintained that she still loved Taylor and respected him
precisely because, unlike many of his peers, he could still play "within the tradition" even amidst his
                          18
experimental work . This suggests her criticism was aimed not at musical experimentation itself, but
at what she perceived as a complete abandonment of jazz's emotional and spiritual core.

Despite her fierce criticism of much of the avant-garde movement, Mary Lou Williams held John
Coltrane in the highest regard. He was one of the few musicians whose name appeared among the
greats on the leaves of her 'Jazz Tree' diagram, a testament to her immense respect for his
                     1
contributions . Her admiration for Coltrane stemmed from her belief in a specific model of artistic
                                                                                                      1
development—one that emerged from lived musical experience rather than formal education . She
noted that Coltrane, along with fellow bebop innovators like Dizzy Gillespie, was a self-taught genius
                                                             1
whose work was forged in the crucible of performance . This aligned perfectly with her own
pedagogical philosophy, which emphasized immersion in the historical lineage of the music before
advancing to new frontiers. Her ability to separate her critique of the direction some free jazz took
from her profound respect for Coltrane himself demonstrates the complexity of her critical
framework. She could acknowledge the negative aspects of a style while still honoring the individual
genius who pushed its boundaries. This duality is a key aspect of her legacy as an educator and
historian; she was not simply a reactionary figure defending the past, but a discerning critic who
valued innovation as long as it was rooted in the foundational spirit of the music.

In stark contrast to her avant-garde confrontations, Williams’s performance with swing-era icon
Benny Goodman at a Carnegie Hall reunion concert in January 1978 offered a different kind of
             3 18
validation          . Although the performance was described as disappointing and underrehearsed, the act
                                                                                                 18
of collaborating with Goodman served a powerful symbolic purpose for Williams . It allowed her to
present a mainstream, establishment-facing pole of her career, demonstrating her mastery of the very
tradition that had made Goodman a household name. This performance, coupled with the avant-
garde concert, allowed her to make a bold claim: "Now I can really say I played all of it," signifying
her comprehensive command of jazz history from its earliest forms to its most modern expressions
18
 . This dual engagement was a strategic move, proving her artistic range and cementing her status as
a definitive interpreter of the entire jazz canon. While she declined Goodman’s later proposal to
record Fats Waller tunes together, the concert itself was a successful demonstration of her ability to
