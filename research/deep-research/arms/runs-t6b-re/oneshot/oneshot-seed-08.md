A US court found Google's search business to be a monopoly because it violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act by engaging in exclusionary conduct through default-distribution deals [Source: ev-1]. Specifically, Judge Amit Mehta ruled on August 5, 2024 that Google paid companies such as Apple, Samsung, and Mozilla billions of dollars to secure default search engine placement [Source: ev-1]. Evidence indicated that Google spent approximately $26.3 billion on distribution in 2021 and paid Apple roughly 36% of Safari search-ad revenue [Source: ev-1]. The court determined this practice created a self-reinforcing loop where defaults generated scale, which produced data, improved quality, and protected the default status against rivals [Source: ev-1].

Regarding remedies, on August 5, 2 conduct was deemed exclusionary [Source: ev-1]. On August 5, 2000 (note: evidence states "Microsoft case of 2000" as historical context for divestiture rarity, but the remedy date is 2025), Judge Mehta ordered structural remedies including the forced sale of Chrome browser, marking the first such divestiture of a major tech company since the Microsoft case in 2000 [Source: ev-1]. Additionally, Google was banned from paying for default search placement or entering revenue-sharing agreements and faced limits on acquisitions and AI-related investments [Source: ev-1]. The final outcome remained pending through 2025 as Google appealed to the DC Circuit [Source: ev-1].
