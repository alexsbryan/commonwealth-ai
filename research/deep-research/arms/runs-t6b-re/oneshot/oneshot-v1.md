Based on the evidence provided, American cities underwent significant transformation between 1980 and 2024, characterized by accelerated gentrification, widening income inequality, severe housing affordability crises, and racially stratified displacement.

### Gentrification Trends
Gentrification has become significantly more common since 2000 compared to previous decades. The rate of gentrification—defined as lower-income tracts experiencing sharp increases in home values and educational attainment—doubled from **9%** in the **1990s** to nearly **20%** following the **2000 Census** [Source: ev-1]. While still rare nationally (affecting only **8%** of all neighborhoods reviewed), it is concentrated in specific urban cores [Source: ev-1].

*   **High-Gentrification Cities:** A select group of cities experienced extensive transformation. Portland, OR had the highest rate at **58.1%**, with **36** tracts gentrifying out of **142** total [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11]. Washington, D.C. followed at **51.9%**, Minneapolis, MN at **50.6%**, and Seattle, WA at **50%** [Source: ev-1]. In New York City, which has a much larger tract count (**2,152**), **128** tracts gentrified since 2000 [Source: ev-1].
*   **Low-Gentrification Cities:** Conversely, cities like Detroit (2.8%), Las Vegas (2%), El Paso (0%), and Arlington, TX (0%) experienced little to no gentrification in their eligible lower-income areas [Source: ev-1].
*   **Historical Context:** Research indicates that income-based gentrification was rare in the 1970s and 1980s but became dominant by 2000–2020, following a long-term trend of increase from its lowest levels in the 197 term [Source: ev-10] [Source: ev-11]. The term "gentrification" itself dates back to 1963 [Source: ev-1].

### Income Inequality
Income inequality has risen sharply across U.S. cities over four decades, with urban cores often exhibiting higher disparity than their surrounding suburbs or rural areas.

*   **National Metrics:** The national Gini coefficient reached record levels recently, hitting **0.485** in **2018**, rising to **0.488** in **2020**, and **0.494** in **2021** (pre-tax money income) [Source: ev-3] [Source: ev-11]. This contrasts with a lower baseline of approximately 0.40 recorded around 2013 [Source: ev-11].
*   **City-Level Disparity:** In 2014, the "95/20" ratio (income gap between top 5% and bottom 20%) was **9.3** nationally but rose to **11.8** for large cities within major metros [Source: ev-5]. Between 2007 and 2014, this national ratio increased from **8.5** to **9.3**, driven largely by substantial drops in low-income household earnings while high incomes remained stable or declined modestly [Source: ev-5] [Source: ev-11].
*   **Top Inequality Cities:** New Orleans exhibits some of the highest inequality among major U.S. cities, with an income ratio of **7.87** (top quintile earning ~$110,820 vs. bottom quintile at $14,078) [Source: ev-4] [Source: ev-11]. Boston follows closely with a ratio of **7.81** ($172,476 vs. $22,095) [Source: ev-4]. Other high-disparity areas include Newark (**6.80**) and Jersey City (**6.09**) in the New York metro area [Source: ev-4].
*   **Metro Trends:** 57 of the 100 largest metropolitan areas saw significantly higher inequality in 2014 compared to 2007 [Source: ev-5] [Source: ev-11]. Cities like Atlanta show extreme internal disparities, with a city-level ratio of **17.5**, double that of its wider metro area [Source: ev-5].

### Housing Affordability
The gap between income growth and housing costs has widened dramatically since 2000, creating affordability crises particularly on the West Coast.

*   **Price vs. Income Growth:** Since 2000, median household incomes grew by **92%** (from $41,990 to $80,610), while median home prices nearly tripled, jumping **177%** (from $122,775 to $339,937) [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. On an inflation-adjusted basis, incomes rose only **8.5%**, whereas home prices increased by more than **56%** [Source: ev-6].
*   **Regional Extremes:** The national price-to-income ratio is approximately 4.6–4.7, but this varies drastically by region [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. California cities face the most extreme pressures; Los Angeles, San Jose, Long Beach, San Francisco, and San Diego have ratios ranging from **9.6** to **12.2** [Source: ev-6]. Eight of the top ten states for high price-to-income ratios are in the West, with ratios exceeding 5.8 [Source: ev-6].
*   **Market Indicators:** The Case-Shiller Index reached **325.78** in July 2024, representing a **225%** increase since January 2000 (inflation-adjusted context implied by comparison) [Source: ev-11].

### Displacement and Demographic Shifts
Gentrification has led to distinct demographic changes and displacement patterns that disproportionately affect minority communities.

*   **Demographic Changes in Gentrifying Areas:** Since 2000 gentrified tracts have become whiter and wealthier. They recorded an average population increase of **6.5%**, with the non-Hispanic white share rising by **4.3** percentage points and poverty rates dropping slightly (**-0.7** pp) [Source: ev-1]. In contrast, lower-income neighborhoods that did *not* gentrify saw population declines (-2.4%), a decrease in white residents (-5.0 pp), and significant increases in poverty (+6.7 pp) [Source: ev-1].
*   **Urban vs. Rural Demographics:** Urban cores have become more diverse; 53% of urban counties are now majority nonwhite, compared to only 10–11% of suburban or rural counties [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. The share of college-educated adults is higher in urban areas (**35%** with bachelor’s degrees or more) than in suburbs (31%) or rural areas (19%) [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11].
*   **Racially Stratified Displacement:** Research on Philadelphia indicates that displacement effects differ by race. Poor residents moving from historically Black gentrifying neighborhoods tend to move to other poorer, disadvantaged neighborhoods within the city, facing fewer options due to racial stratification and discriminatory lending practices [Source: ev-9]. In contrast, residents moving from non-predominantly Black gentrifying areas often benefit by moving to wealthier neighborhoods [Source: ev-9]. This suggests gentrification reconfigures urban landscapes in a way that shrinks residential options for disadvantaged minority groups while expanding them for more advantaged groups [Source: ev-9] [Source: ev-11].
*   **Economic Structural Shift:** The underlying driver of these changes includes a shift away from manufacturing (jobs fell from 19.5 million in 1979 to under 11.5 million) toward finance and professional services (growing from 12 million to 32 million), which concentrated high-income, young workers in large metro areas since 1980 [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11]. Nearly 80% of the country’s growth in its under-45 population has been concentrated in metros with over one million residents during this period [Source: ev-8].

### Conclusion
From 1980 to 2024, American cities transformed through a combination of deindustrialization and service-sector growth that attracted educated professionals to urban cores. This influx accelerated gentrification after 2000, particularly in Pacific Northwest and Northeast corridor cities. The result was a sharp rise in income inequality—measured by Gini coefficients and percentile ratios—and housing costs that outpaced wage growth, leading to displacement patterns where minority communities face constrained residential mobility compared to their white counterparts.
