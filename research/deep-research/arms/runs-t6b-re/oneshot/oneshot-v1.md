Based on the evidence provided, here is a synthesis of how American cities changed across four decades (1980–2024) regarding gentrification, inequality, affordability, and displacement.

### 1. Gentrification: Acceleration and Geographic Concentration
Gentrification has accelerated significantly since 2000, transforming urban landscapes in specific regions while remaining rare nationally.

*   **Acceleration Rates:** The rate of gentrification more than doubled after 2000 compared to the 1990s [Source: ev-1]. Specifically, nearly 20% of lower-income neighborhoods experienced gentrification since the 2000 Census, compared to only 9% during the 1990 [Source: ev-1]. Despite this acceleration, gentrification remains a phenomenon largely confined to select areas; only 8% of all reviewed tracts nationwide have gentrified since 200 [Source: ev-1].
*   **Leading Cities:** A small group of cities has seen extensive transformation. Portland, OR had the highest rate, with 58.1% of eligible tracts gentrifying (36 out of 62 eligible) [Source: ev-1]. Washington, D.C. followed at 51.9% (54 tracts), Minneapolis at 50.6% (39 tracts), and Seattle at 50% (7 tracts) [Source: ev-1]. In terms of raw numbers, New York City saw the most tracts gentrify, with 128 since 2000 [Source: ev-1].
*   **Historical Trends:** Research indicates that income-based gentrification was rare in the 1970s and 1980s but became dominant by the 2000s [Source: ev-10][Source: ev-11]. Gentrification levels were lowest in the 197 [Source: ev-10] and increased every decade until at least 201 [Source: ev-10]. The term "gentrification" itself dates back to a British sociologist who coined it in 1963, though its modern acceleration is linked to recent urban lifestyle trends [Source: ev-1].
*   **Demographic Shifts:** Since 2000, gentrifying tracts have seen an average increase of 4.3 percentage points in their non-Hispanic white population share and a decrease of 0.7 percentage points in poverty rates [Source: ev-1][Source: ev-11]. Conversely, lower-income neighborhoods that did *not* gentrify experienced slight population losses (average -2.4%) and saw their poverty rates climb by nearly 7 percent (specifically +6.7 percentage points) [Source: ev-1][Source: ev-11].

### 2. Inequality: Widening Gaps at the Metro Level
Income inequality has widened significantly across major metropolitan areas, with urban cores showing steeper disparities than suburbs or rural areas.

*   **National Metrics:** The U.S. Gini coefficient reached record levels of 0.485 in 2018, rising to 0.494 in 2021 according to pre-tax money income data from the Census Bureau [Source: ev-3]. By 2013, the national average was noted as 0.40, while New York City’s Gini index stood at a much higher 0.5469 due to its reliance on high-salary earners [Source: ev-2][Source: ev-11].
*   **Metro Disparities (95/20 Ratio):** In 2014, the 95/20 income ratio for large cities was 11.8, compared to 9.7 for metro areas and 9.3 nationally [Source: ev-5]. This represents an increase from a nationwide ratio of 8.5 in 2007 [Source: ev-5][Source: ev-11]. Between 2007 and 2014, 57 of the 100 largest metro areas saw significantly higher inequality ratios [Source: ev-5][Source: ev-11].
*   **Top Inequality Cities:** New Orleans has one of the highest levels of income inequality among major U.S. cities, with an 80/20 ratio (top 80% vs bottom 20%) of 7.87; top earners make $110,820 while the bottom quintile earns just $14,078 [Source: ev-4][Source: ev-11]. Boston follows closely with a ratio of 7.81 ($172,476 for the 80th percentile vs. $22,095 for the 20th) [Source: ev-4][Source: ev-11]. Other high-disparity cities include Newark (ratio 6.80), Jersey City (6.09), and New York City (approx. 5.8) [Source: ev-4].
*   **Regional Patterns:** Urban cores concentrate both high-earning professionals and low-income service workers, creating stark divides unlike the more moderate distributions in suburbs [Source: ev-11]. For instance, Atlanta exhibited a city-level disparity where high-income households earned 17.5 times that of low-income counterparts in 2014—twice the metro average [Source: ev-5].

### 3. Affordability: The Housing Crisis
Housing costs have outpaced income growth dramatically since 2000, leading to severe affordability crises in major coastal cities.

*   **Income vs. Home Prices:** Since 2000 median household income grew by 92% (from $41,990 to $80,610), but on an inflation-adjusted basis, it increased only 8.5% [Source: ev-6][Source: ev-11]. In contrast, median home prices jumped 177% from $122,775 to $339,937; adjusted for inflation, this is a >56% increase [Source: ev-6][Source: ev-11]. The Case-Shiller Index reached 325.78 in July 2024, representing a 225% increase since January 2000 [Source: ev-11].
*   **Price-to-Income Ratios:** The national price-to-income ratio is approximately 4.6 (or 4.7 depending on the source) [Source: ev-6][Source: ev-11]. However, five of the six large U.S. cities with the highest ratios are in California—Los Angeles, San Jose, Long Beach, San Francisco, and San Diego—with ratios ranging from 9.6 to 12.2 [Source: ev-6][Source: ev-11]. Other high-pressure areas include New York City, Miami, and Boston [Source: ev-6].
*   **Economic Drivers:** This shift coincides with a broader economic transformation where manufacturing jobs fell from 19.5 million in 1979 to under 11.5 million during the pandemic, while finance and professional services grew from 12 million to 32 million jobs [Source: ev-8][Source: ev-11]. This clustering of white-collar service industries in large cities has driven up costs for housing and living [Source: ev-8].

### 4. Displacement and Demographic Realignment
Gentrification is not just an economic change but a demographic one that disproportionately affects minority communities through displacement.

*   **Displacement Patterns:** Research on Philadelphia shows that poor residents moving out of historically Black gentrifying neighborhoods tend to move to poorer non-gentrifying areas within the city, whereas residents from other (non-predominantly Black) gentrifying areas move to wealthier neighborhoods or suburbs [Source: ev-9][Source: ev-11]. This suggests that racial stratification limits mobility options for Black residents during gentrification [Source: ev-9].
*   **Racial Composition Changes:** Since 2000 whites have become the minority in most urban counties; 53% of urban counties are now majority nonwhite, compared to only about one-in-ten suburban and rural counties [Source: ev-7][Source: ev-11]. The white share of the population fell by 8 percentage points in suburbs and 7 points in urban cores since 2000 (though some data notes a decline of only 3 points in rural areas) [Source: ev-7][Source: ev-11].
*   **Educational Attainment:** Urbanization has correlated with higher education levels. Today, 35% of urban residents have a bachelor’s degree or more, compared to 31% in suburbs and 19% in rural counties [Source: ev-7][Source: ev-11]. Since 1980 nearly 80 percent of the country's growth in its under-45 population was concentrated in metro areas with over one million residents [Source: ev-8][Source: ev-11].
