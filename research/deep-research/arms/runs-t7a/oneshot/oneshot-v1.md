Based on the evidence provided, American cities underwent significant transformation between 1980 and 2024 across four key dimensions: gentrification, inequality, affordability, and displacement.

### Gentrification
Gentrification accelerated dramatically in the decades following 1980, particularly after the year 2000 [Source: ev-1]. The term "gentrification" was originally coined by a British sociologist in 1963 [Source: ev-1]. In the 1970s and 1980s, income-based gentrification was rare, but it became more common as decades progressed until reaching its peak levels around 2010 [Source: ev-10]. Between 1990 and 2000, only **9%** of lower-income neighborhoods experienced gentrification; this rate doubled to nearly **20%** for the period following the year **2000** Census [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11].

The phenomenon remained geographically concentrated. Nationally, only **8%** of all reviewed neighborhoods gentrified since **2000**, with less than one in ten tracts affected when including wealthier areas not eligible for classification [Source: ev-1]. Specific cities showed extreme variation in intensity:
*   **Portland, OR:** Had the highest rate at **58.1%**, where **36** out of its eligible tracts gentrified (out of a total of **142**) [Source: ev-1].
*   **Washington, DC:** Followed closely at **51.9%**, with **54** gentrifying tracts out of **179** total [Source: ev-1].
*   **Minneapolis, MN:** Recorded a rate of **50.6%** (**39** tracts) and **Seattle, WA:** recorded **50%** (**7** tracts), both indicating that at least half of their lower-income neighborhoods transformed [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11].
*   **New York, NY:** Had the highest raw count with **128** gentrified tracts, though its percentage was lower at **29.8%** due to having **2,152** total tracts reviewed [Source: ev-1].

In contrast, cities like Detroit (rate of **2.8%**) and Las Vegas (rate of **2%**) experienced minimal change, while El Paso and Arlington recorded **0%** [Source: ev-1]. Gentrifying tracts were defined by increases in inflation-adjusted median home values and the percentage of adults with bachelor’s degrees, both rising into the top third percentile compared to other metro areas [Source: ev-1].

### Inequality
Income inequality widened significantly across U.S. metropolitan areas during this period. The national 95/20 income ratio rose from **8.5** in **2007** to **9.3** in **2014**, indicating that high earners made nearly nine times more than low earners at the end of the decade compared to eight-and-a-half times before [Source: ev-5]. This trend was driven by substantial drops in bottom incomes; in **81** of the **100** largest metro areas, incomes near the bottom fell between **2007** and **2014**, while top incomes declined or remained stable in only **33** of those metros [Source: ev-5].

City-level data highlights extreme disparities. New Orleans had the highest income inequality ratio among major cities at **7.87**, where the 80th percentile earned $110,820 versus just $14,078 for the 20th percentile [Source: ev-4] [Source: ev-11]. Boston followed with a ratio of **7.81** ($172,476 vs. $22,095) and Newark ranked third at **6.80** [Source: ev-4]. In terms of Gini coefficients, New York City exhibited high inequality with a coefficient of **0.5469**, compared to the national average which was approximately **0.40** in **2013** [Source: ev-2] [Source: ev-11]. By **2018**, the Census Bureau recorded a record U.S. Gini coefficient of **0.485**, rising further to **0.494** by **2021** [Source: ev-3].

The 95/20 ratio for big cities within large metros reached **11.8** in **2014**, significantly higher than the aggregate metro area average of **9.7** and the national figure of **9.3** [Source: ev-5]. Atlanta showed one of the largest gaps between city and region, with high-income households earning **17.5** times more than low-income counterparts within the city limits [Source: ev-5].

### Affordability
Housing affordability deteriorated sharply as home prices outpaced income growth. Since **2000**, median household income grew by **92%**, rising from $41,990 to $80,610 in nominal terms; however, on an inflation-adjusted basis, incomes increased by only **8.5%** [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. During this same period, median home prices nearly tripled, jumping **177%** from $122,775 to $339,937 (a **56%** increase adjusted for inflation) [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11].

The national price-to-income ratio stood at **4.6**, but eight of the top ten states—mostly in the West—had ratios of **5.8** or higher [Source: ev-6]. California cities faced the most severe crisis, with five of the six large U.S. cities having the highest price-to-income ratios located there; Los Angeles, San Jose, Long Beach, San Francisco, and San Diego had ratios ranging from **9.6** to **12.2** [Source: ev-6]. The Case-Shiller Index reached **325.78** in July **2024**, representing a **225%** increase since January **2000** [Source: ev-11].

### Displacement and Demographic Shifts
Gentrification led to distinct demographic shifts and displacement patterns. Gentrifying neighborhoods experienced population increases of an average **6.5%**, while non-gentrifying lower-income tracts saw slight losses of **-2.4%** [Source: ev-1]. Racially, gentrifying areas became whiter, with the share of non-Hispanic white residents increasing by an average of **4.3** percentage points, whereas poverty rates dropped slightly (**-0.7** percentage points) in these areas compared to a rise of **+6.7** percentage points in comparable non-gentrified low-income tracts [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11].

Displacement affected minority communities disproportionately. A study of Philadelphia found that poor residents moving from historically Black gentrifying neighborhoods tended to move to other poorer, non-gentrifying neighborhoods within the city, facing fewer housing options than their counterparts in non-Black gentrifying areas who moved to wealthier locations or suburbs [Source: ev-9] [Source: ev-11]. This disparity is attributed to racially stratified housing markets and discriminatory lending practices [Source: ev-9].

Broader demographic trends also shifted urban composition. Since **2000**, the white share of the population fell by **7** points in urban cores and **8** points in suburbs; consequently, **53%** of urban counties are now majority nonwhite, compared to only **10%** of suburban counties [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. Additionally, nearly **80%** of the country’s growth in its under-**45** population between **1980** and recent years was concentrated in metro areas with over one million residents, driven by a shift from manufacturing (which fell from **19.5** million jobs in **1979**) to finance and professional services (growing from **12** million to **32** million) [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11].
