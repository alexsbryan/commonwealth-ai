A US court found Google's search business a monopoly because Judge Amit Mehta ruled on 2024-08-05 that the company violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act by acting as a monopolist in general search services and text advertising [Source: ev-1]. The primary theory of harm was "default-distribution deals," where Google paid billions to companies like Apple, Samsung, and Mozilla to be their default search engine [Source: ev-1]. Evidence showed that Google’s CEO Sundar Pichai testified the company spent about $26.3 billion for distribution in 2021, and specifically paid Apple roughly 36% of Safari search-ad revenue [Source: ev-1]. The court determined this conduct created an exclusionary loop where defaults create scale, which creates data, leading to quality improvements that protect the default position against rivals [Source: ev-1].

The remedies ordered by Judge Mehta on 2025-08-05 include a structural divestiture requiring Google to sell its Chrome browser, marking the first forced divestiture of a major tech company since the Microsoft case of 2000 [Source: ev-1]. Additionally, Google is prohibited from paying for default search placement through revenue-sharing agreements and faces limits on acquisitions and AI-related investments [Source: ev-1]. Although Google appealed these decisions in 2025 arguing they punished procompetitive innovation, the final outcome remained pending through 2025 [Source: ev-1].
