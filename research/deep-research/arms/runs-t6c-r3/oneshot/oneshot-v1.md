American cities have undergone a fundamental transformation from 1980 to 2024, characterized by accelerating gentrification, widening income inequality, severe housing affordability crises in major metros, and racially stratified displacement patterns.

### Gentrification Trends (1970–2013)
Gentrification has become significantly more common over the last four decades, with rates doubling after 2000 compared to the 1990s [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11]. While the term was coined in 1963, academic analysis of Census data indicates that gentrification levels were at their lowest in the 1970s and increased every decade until 2010 [Source: ev-10]. Income-based gentrification, which was rare in the 1970-80s, became dominant by the 2000s [Source: ev-10] [Source: ev-11].

*   **Acceleration:** Nearly 20% of lower-income neighborhoods experienced gentrification since 2000, compared to only 9% during the 1990 [Source: ev-1]. Rates increased in 39 of the 50 largest cities reviewed [Source: ev-1].
*   **Geographic Concentration:** Gentrification remains largely confined to select regions. In the majority of cities, less than one-fifth of poorer neighborhoods gentrified [Source: ev-1]. Cities like Detroit (2.8%), El Paso (0%), and Las Vegas (2%) saw minimal change [Source: ev-1].
*   **Top Affected Cities:** A small group of cities experienced extensive transformation since 2000:
    *   Portland, OR: 58.1% of eligible tracts gentrified (36 out of 62 eligible) [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11].
    *   Washington, D.C.: 51.9% (54 tracts) [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11].
    *   Minneapolis, MN: 50.6% (39 tracts) [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11].
    *   Seattle, WA: 50% (7 tracts) [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11].
    *   New York, NY had the highest raw total with 128 gentrified tracts, though its rate was lower at 29.8% due to a larger base of eligible areas [Source: ev-1].

### Demographic and Economic Shifts in Neighborhoods
Neighborhoods that gentrified since 2000 recorded population increases (+6.5%) and became whiter, with the non-Hispanic white share increasing by an average of 4.3 percentage points [Source: ev-1]. Conversely, lower-income neighborhoods that did not gentrify experienced slight population losses (-2.4%), saw minority concentrations increase (white share dropped 5.0 points), and faced rising poverty rates (+6.7 points) compared to a -0.7 point drop in gentrifying areas [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11].

Broader urban demographic trends show that the white share of the population fell 8 percentage points in suburbs and 7 points in urban cores since 2000 [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. Today, 53% of urban counties are majority non-white, compared to only 10% of suburban counties [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. Urban areas have also become more educated; 35% of urban residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, compared to 31% in suburbs and 19% in rural areas [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11].

### Income Inequality
Inequality has widened significantly across American cities. The U.S. Gini coefficient reached record levels, with values rising from 0.485 in 2018 to 0.494 in 2021 per pre-tax money income [Source: ev-3]. Metropolitan areas generally exhibit higher inequality than the national average.

*   **Metro vs. National:** In 2014, the national 95/20 income ratio was 9.3, while for the 100 largest metro areas it was 9.7, and for their big cities, it was 11.8 [Source: ev-5].
*   **Trends (2007–2014):** The national 95/20 ratio rose from 8.5 in 2007 to 9.3 in 2014 [Source: ev-5] [Source: ev-11]. This increase occurred because top incomes were stable or declined modestly, while bottom-quintile incomes dropped substantially; low-income households earned significantly less in 81 of the 100 largest metros by 2014 compared to 2007 [Source: ev-5].
*   **City-Specific Inequality:** Major cities show extreme disparities. New York City has a Gini coefficient of 0.5469, higher than the national average of ~0.40 in 2013 [Source: ev-2] [Source: ev-11]. Atlanta and Miami follow closely with coefficients around 0.57, and New Orleans at 0.56 [Source: ev-11].
    *   Using an 80/20 ratio (top 80% vs bottom 20%), New Orleans had the highest inequality among major U.S. cities at 7.87, where the top earners made $110,820 versus $14,078 for the bottom quintile [Source: ev-4] [Source: ev-11].
    *   Boston followed with a ratio of 7.81 ($172,476 vs $22,095) [Source: ev-4] [Source: ev-11].
    *   Newark ranked third at 6.80, and Jersey City fourth at 6.09 [Source: ev-4].
    *   The most equal major city was Chesapeake, VA, with a multiplier of only 3.8 [Source: ev-4].

### Housing Affordability Crisis
The gap between income growth and housing costs has widened dramatically since 2000. While median household income grew by 92% (from $41,990 to $80,610), median home prices nearly tripled, jumping 177% from $122,775 to $339,937 [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. On an inflation-adjusted basis, incomes increased just 8.5%, while home prices rose more than 56% [Source: ev-6].

*   **Price-to-Income Ratios:** The national price-to-income ratio is approximately 4.6–4.7 [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. However, five of the six large U.S. cities with the highest ratios are in California (Los Angeles, San Jose, Long Beach, San Francisco, and San Diego), ranging from 9.6 to 12.2—more than double the national average [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11].
*   **Case-Shiller Index:** By July 2024, the Case-Shiller Index reached 325.78, representing a 225% increase since January 2000 [Source: ev-11].

### Displacement and Racial Stratification
Gentrification does not affect all residents equally; displacement is racially stratified. Research on Philadelphia indicates that poor residents moving from historically Black gentrifying neighborhoods tend to move to poorer non-gentrifying areas within the city, whereas residents from other (non-predominantly Black) gentrifying neighborhoods often move to wealthier areas in cities or suburbs [Source: ev-9] [Source: ev-11]. This suggests that for minority communities, there are additional constraints leading them into a "shrinking set" of affordable yet disadvantaged neighborhoods [Source: ev-9]. The study analyzed over 50,000 adult residents and found that financially disadvantaged movers from predominantly non-Black areas benefited by accessing more advantaged locations, while those from Black areas did not [Source: ev-9].

### Structural Economic Drivers
These changes align with broader economic shifts since 1980. Manufacturing jobs fell from 19.5 million in 1979 to under 11.5 million during the pandemic, while finance and professional/business services employment soared from 12 million to 32 million [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11]. This shift concentrated high-income professionals in large urban cores. Consequently, nearly 80% of the country’s growth in its under-45 population has been concentrated in metro areas with over a million residents since 1980, while rural counties lost this demographic [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11].

In summary, American cities have polarized economically and demographically. While overall wealth has increased, it is unevenly distributed, driving up housing costs that displace lower-income and minority residents from gentrifying neighborhoods into less advantaged areas, thereby exacerbating racial and socioeconomic segregation [Source: ev-11] [Source: ev-9].
