Based on the evidence provided, American cities underwent significant transformation between 1980 and 2024, characterized by accelerated gentrification, widening income inequality, deteriorating housing affordability relative to incomes, and racially stratified displacement.

### Gentrification: Acceleration and Geographic Concentration
Gentrification has become more prevalent but remains concentrated in specific regions rather than being a nationwide phenomenon [Source: ev-1]. The term was coined in 1963, but rates particularly accelerated after 2000 as Americans pursued urban lifestyles [Source: ev-1][Source: ev-10].

*   **Rate of Change:** Since the 2000 Census, nearly 20% of lower-income neighborhoods with low home values experienced gentrification, compared to only 9% during the 1990s [Source: ev-1]. Despite this acceleration, gentrification is still rare nationally; only 8% of all reviewed neighborhoods gentrified since 2000 [Source: ev-1].
*   **Leading Cities:** A select group of cities saw extensive transformation. Portland, OR had the highest rate, with 58.1% (36 out of eligible tracts) gentrifying [Source: ev-1][Source: ev-11]. Washington, D.C. followed at 51.9%, Minneapolis at 50.6%, and Seattle at 50% [Source: ev-1][Source: ev-11]. In terms of raw numbers, New York City recorded the most gentrified tracts (128) since 2000 due to its large base population [Source: ev-1].
*   **Lagging Cities:** Conversely, cities like Detroit (2.8%), Las Vegas (2%), El Paso (0%), and Arlington, TX (0%) experienced little to no gentrification among eligible lower-income tracts [Source: ev-1].
*   **Types of Gentrification:** Research indicates that income-based gentrification was rare in the 1970s and 1980s but increased substantially by the 2000s, becoming a dominant form alongside educational and occupational gentrification [Source: ev-10][Source: ev-11].

### Demographic Shifts in Urban Cores
Urban areas have become demographically distinct from suburban and rural regions over the last four decades.

*   **Racial Composition:** The white share of the population fell by 7 percentage points in urban cores since 2000, compared to an 8-point drop in suburbs and a 3-point drop in rural counties [Source: ev-7][Source: ev-11]. Consequently, whites are now a minority in most urban counties (53% of them), whereas only about 10% of suburban or rural counties are majority nonwhite [Source: ev-7][Source: ev-11].
*   **Education Levels:** Urban areas have higher educational attainment than other regions; today, 35% of urban residents hold a bachelor’s degree or more, compared to 31% in suburbs and 19% in rural counties [Source: ev-7][Source: ev-11].
*   **Migration Patterns:** Since 1980, nearly 80% of the country's growth in the under-45 population has been concentrated in metro areas with over one million residents [Source: ev-8][Source: ev-11]. This trend coincides with an economic shift where manufacturing jobs fell from 19.5 million (in 1979) to under 11.5 million during the pandemic, while finance and professional services jobs soared from 12 million to 32 million, clustering high-income professionals in urban cores [Source: ev-8][Source: ev-11].

### Income Inequality
Income inequality widened significantly across major metropolitan areas between 2007 and 2014, continuing a long-term trend of divergence since the 1980s [Source: ev-5][Source: ev-11].

*   **National Trends:** The U.S. Gini coefficient reached record levels, rising from 0.485 in 2018 to 0.494 in 2021 based on pre-tax money income [Source: ev-3]. Historically, the national average was noted at 0.40 in 2013 [Source: ev-11].
*   **Metro vs. National Averages:** In 2014, large metro areas were more unequal than the nation as a whole. The "95/20 ratio" (income of the top 5% versus bottom 20%) was 9.7 for the 100 largest metros and 11.8 for their big cities, compared to a national ratio of 9.3 [Source: ev-5]. Between 2007 and 57 of these 100 metro areas saw significantly higher inequality in 2014 [Source: ev-5][Source: ev-11].
*   **City-Specific Disparities:**
    *   **New Orleans** had the highest income inequality among major U.S. cities, with an 80/20 ratio of 7.87 (top earners making $110,820 vs. bottom earners at $14,078) [Source: ev-4][Source: ev-11].
    *   **Boston** followed closely with a ratio of 7.81 ($172,476 for top quintile vs. $22,095 for bottom) [Source: ev-4][Source: ev-11].
    *   **New York City** has one of the highest Gini coefficients among major cities at 0.5469 [Source: ev-2][Source: ev-11]. Other high-inequality areas include Atlanta and Miami (Gini ~0.57) and New Orleans (Gini 0.56) [Source: ev-11].
    *   In terms of extreme ratios, some metros like Atlanta exhibited a city-level disparity where high-income households earned 17.5 times more than low-income counterparts in 2014, double the metro-wide disparity [Source: ev-5].

### Housing Affordability Crisis
The gap between income growth and housing costs has widened dramatically since 2000.

*   **Price vs. Income Growth:** From 2000 to recent years, median household income grew by 92% (from $41,990 to $80,610), while median home prices nearly tripled, jumping 177% from $122,775 to $339,937 [Source: ev-6][Source: ev-11]. On an inflation-adjusted basis, incomes rose only 8.5%, whereas home prices increased over 56% [Source: ev-6].
*   **Extreme Ratios in Coastal Cities:** Five of the six large U.S. cities with the highest price-to-income ratios are in California (Los Angeles, San Jose, Long Beach, San Francisco, and San Diego), ranging from 9.6 to 12.2 [Source: ev-6]. This is more than double the national average ratio of approximately 4.6 or 4.7 [Source: ev-6][Source: ev-11]. The Case-Shiller Index reached 325.78 in July 2024, representing a 225% increase since January 2000 [Source: ev-11].
*   **Mobility Impacts:** Economic mobility for low-income families worsened in 48 of the 50 largest metros over a 15-year period; only Houston showed improvement (a 1.1% gain) for those growing up in the 1990s versus the 1980s compared to prior decades [Source: ev-11].

### Displacement and Racial Inequity
Gentrification does not affect all groups equally, with significant racial disparities in displacement outcomes.

*   **Neighborhood Changes:** Gentrifying tracts typically see population increases (+6.5%), an increase in non-Hispanic white residents (+4.3 percentage points), and a slight decrease in poverty (-0.7 percentage points) [Source: ev-1][Source: ev-11]. Conversely, lower-income neighborhoods that did *not* gentrify experienced population losses (-2.4%) and sharp rises in poverty rates (+6.7 percentage points) [Source: ev-1][Source: ev-11].
*   **Racially Stratified Displacement:** A study of Philadelphia found that poor residents moving from historically Black gentrifying neighborhoods tend to move into other poorer, non-gentrifying areas within the city, often facing fewer options due to discriminatory lending practices and housing market stratification [Source: ev-9][Source: ev-11]. In contrast, disadvantaged residents moving from non-predominantly Black gentrifying areas tended to move to wealthier neighborhoods or suburbs [Source: ev-9]. This dynamic shrinks residential options for minority communities while expanding them for more advantaged groups [Source: ev-9].
