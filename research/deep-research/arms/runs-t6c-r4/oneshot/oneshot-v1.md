American cities have undergone a fundamental transformation between 1980 and 2024, characterized by the acceleration of gentrification, widening income inequality, deteriorating housing affordability relative to wages, and racially stratified displacement.

### Gentrification Trends
Gentrification accelerated significantly after 2000, with rates doubling compared to the 1990s [Source: ev-1]. While the term was coined in 1963, recent decades saw a sharp increase as more Americans pursued urban lifestyles [Source: ev-1]. According to an analysis of Census tract data for the nation’s **50** largest cities, nearly **20%** of neighborhoods with lower incomes and home values experienced gentrification since **2000**, compared to only **9%** during the **1990**s [Source: ev-1]. Nationally, however, gentrification remains rare if all neighborhoods are considered, with only **8%** of all reviewed tracts experiencing it since the **2000** Census [Source: ev-1].

A select group of cities experienced extensive transformation. Portland, OR had the highest rate, with **58.1%** of its eligible tracts gentrifying (specifically **36** out of **142** total tracts) [Source: ev-1]. Washington, D.C. followed at **51.9%** (**54** gentrified tracts), Minneapolis at **50.6%** (**39** tracts), and Seattle at **50%** (**7** tracts) [Source: ev-1]. In terms of raw totals, New York City saw the most tracts change status, with **128** gentrified neighborhoods [Source: ev-1]. Conversely, cities like Detroit (**2.8%**, **7** tracts), Las Vegas (**2%**, **1** tract), El Paso (0%, 0 tracts), and Arlington, TX (0%, 0 trasts) experienced practically no gentrification [Source: ev-1].

Research indicates that income-based gentrification was rare in the 1970s and 1980s but became dominant by the 2000s, following a long-term trend where levels increased every decade from their lowest point in the 197**0**s to **2010** [Source: ev-10].

### Income Inequality
Income inequality has widened substantially across U.S. cities over this period. The national Gini coefficient reached record levels of **0.485** in **2018**, rising to **0.488** in **2020** and **0.494** in **2021** based on pre-tax money income [Source: ev-3]. Metropolitan areas often exhibit higher inequality than the nation as a whole; for instance, New York City had a Gini index of **0.5469**, compared to the national average of approximately **0.40** in 2013 [Source: ev-2] [Source: ev-11]. Other major cities with high Gini coefficients include Atlanta (**0.57**) and Miami/New Orleans (approx. **0.56**-**0.57**) [Source: ev-11].

Using the "95/20 ratio" (comparing top 5% incomes to bottom 20%), inequality rose from **8.5** in **2007** to **9.3** nationally by **2014** [Source: ev-5]. In that same year, the ratio for big cities within large metro areas was even higher at **11.8**, compared to **9.7** for the metros themselves [Source: ev-5**. Between 2007 and 2014, **57** of the **100** largest metropolitan areas saw significantly higher income inequality ratios [Source: ev-5] [Source: ev-11].

SmartAsset’s analysis of **98** major U.S. cities using an 80/20 income ratio (top 80% vs bottom 20%) found New Orleans had the highest inequality with a ratio of **7.87**; top earners made at least $110,**820**, while the bottom quintile earned less than $14,**078** [Source: ev-4]. Boston followed closely with a ratio of **7.81** ($172,476 vs $22,095) [Source: ev-4] [Source: ev-11]. Newark and Jersey City also ranked highly in the top 10 for inequality [Source: ev-4]. In contrast, Chesapeake, VA showed the most equal distribution with a multiplier of only **3.8** [Source: ev-4**.

### Affordability Crisis
Housing costs have outpaced income growth dramatically since 2000. Median household income grew by **92%** (from $41,**990 to $80,**610), while median home prices jumped **177%** (from $122,**775 to $339,**937) [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. On an inflation-adjusted basis, incomes increased by just **8.5%**, whereas home prices rose more than **56%** [Source: ev-6**. The Case-Shiller Index reached **325.78** in July **2024**, representing a 225% increase since January 2000 [Source: ev-11].

This disparity is most acute on the West Coast. Five of the six large U.S. cities with the highest price-to-income ratios are in California—Los Angeles, San Jose, Long Beach, San Francisco, and San Diego—with ratios ranging from **9.6 to 12.2** [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. This compares to a national average ratio of approximately **4.6** (or **4.7**) [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11**. The top 10 states for high ratios include eight Western states, all with ratios of **5.8** or higher [Source: ev-6**.

### Demographic Shifts and Displacement
The economic shift away from manufacturing toward service industries drove demographic changes. Manufacturing jobs fell from **19.5 million in 1979 to under 11.5 million during the pandemic**, while finance and professional services grew from **12 million to 32 million** [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11]. Since 1980, nearly **80%** of the country’s growth in its under-**45** population was concentrated in metro areas with over a million residents [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11**.

Urban cores have become more diverse but also more segregated by income and race. The white share of the population fell **7 points** in urban cores and **8 points** in suburbs since 2000; however, only **3 points** declined in rural counties [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. Today, **53%** of urban counties are majority nonwhite, compared to just **10%** of suburban counties [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11**. Urban areas now have **35%** of residents with bachelor’s degrees or higher, compared to **31%** in suburbs and **19%** in rural areas [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11**.

Gentrifying neighborhoods recorded population increases (**6.5%**) and became whiter (non-Hispanic white share increased by an average of **4.3** percentage points), while poverty rates dropped slightly (**0.7** percentage point decrease) [Source: ev-1]. In contrast, lower-income tracts that did *not* gentrify experienced population losses (**2.4%**) and saw their non-Hispanic white shares decline by **5.0** percentage points, with poverty rates climbing nearly **7 percent** (specifically a 6.**7** percentage point increase in the data table) [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11**.

Displacement is racially stratified. A study of Philadelphia involving over **50,000** residents found that poor residents moving from historically Black gentrifying neighborhoods tended to move to poorer non-gentrifying areas within the city, whereas those leaving other gentrifying neighborhoods moved to wealthier locations [Source: ev-9]. This suggests that while gentrification expands residential options for advantaged groups, it shrinks them for disadvantaged minority communities due to factors like discriminatory lending and housing market constraints [Source: ev-9].
