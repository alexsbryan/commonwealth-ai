Based on the evidence provided, American cities underwent significant transformation between 1980 and 2024, characterized by accelerated gentrification in specific urban cores, widening income inequality, a growing gap between housing costs and wages, and racially stratified displacement patterns.

### Gentrification Trends (1970–2013)
Gentrification has become more prevalent over four decades, with rates accelerating significantly after 2000 [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-10]. While the term was coined in 1963 [Source: ev-1], academic research indicates that gentrification levels were at their lowest in the 1970s and increased every decade until 2010 [Source: ev-10]. Income-based gentrification, which was rare in the 1970 and 1980s, became substantially more common in later decades [Source: ev-10].

*   **Acceleration Post-2000:** The rate of gentrification (the share of eligible lower-income tracts experiencing change) doubled from the 1990s to the period following the 2000 Census. Specifically, nearly 20% of neighborhoods with lower incomes and home values experienced gentrification since 2000, compared to only 9% during the 1990 [Source: ev-1].
*   **Geographic Concentration:** Gentrification remains rare nationally; if all city neighborhoods are considered, less than one in ten tracts gentrified [Source: ev-1]. However, a select group of cities saw extensive changes. In the nation’s 50 largest cities analyzed using Census tract data from the 2009–2013 American Community Survey and US2010 Longitudinal Tract Data Base:
    *   **Portland, OR** had the highest rate, with 58.1% of eligible tracts (36 out of 142 total) gentrifying [Source: ev-1].
    *   **Washington, D.C.** followed at 51.9%, with 54 tracts gentrifying out of 179 total [Source: ev-1].
    *   **Minneapolis, MN** recorded a 50.6% rate, with 39 tracts gentrifying [Source: ev-1].
    *   **Seattle, WA** reached a 50% rate, though this applied to only 7 gentrified tracts due to many areas being ineligible by the start of the decade [Source: ev-1].
    *   In terms of raw numbers, **New York, NY** had the highest count of gentrified tracts (128), despite a lower percentage share of 29.8% because it has a much larger total tract population (2,152) [Source: ev-1].
*   **Demographic Shifts in Gentrifying Areas:** Compared to non-gentrifying low-income areas, gentrifying Census tracts recorded increases in the non-Hispanic white population and declines in poverty rates [Source: ev-1]. On average, gentrifying tracts saw their non-Hispanic white share increase by 4.3 percentage points and their poverty rate decrease by 0.7 percentage points [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11]. In contrast, lower-income neighborhoods that did not gentrify experienced slight population losses (-2.4%), a decline in white population share (-5.0 percentage points), and an increase in poverty (+6.7 percentage points) [Source: ev-1].

### Economic Transformation and Income Inequality
The economic landscape shifted from manufacturing to service industries, concentrating wealth in urban cores while widening the gap between high and low earners.

*   **Industrial Shift:** Manufacturing jobs peaked at 19.5 million in 1979 but fell to under 11.5 million during the pandemic (approx. 2020–2023 period referenced) [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11]. Conversely, employment in finance and professional/business services soared from 12 million to 32 million [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11]. This shift concentrated population growth in large metros; since 1980, nearly 80% of the country’s growth in its under-45 population was concentrated in metro areas with over a million residents [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11].
*   **Rising Inequality Metrics:** Income inequality reached record levels. The U.S. Gini coefficient for pre-tax money income rose to 0.485 in 2018, 0.488 in 2020, and 0.494 in 2021 [Source: ev-3]. This contrasts with earlier data showing a national average Gini of 0.40 in 2013 [Source: ev-11].
    *   **Metro Disparities:** In 2014, the "95/20 ratio" (income gap between top 5% and bottom 20%) was 9.3 nationally, but reached 9.7 for the aggregate of the 100 largest metro areas and 11.8 for big cities within those metros [Source: ev-5]. Between 2007 and 2014, this ratio rose from 8.5 to 9.3 nationwide as low-income households saw substantial income drops while high incomes remained stable or declined modestly in many areas [Source: ev-5] [Source: ev-11].
    *   **Specific City Inequality:** New York City exhibits extreme inequality with a Gini index of 0.5469 [Source: ev-2] [Source: ev-11]. Other major metros like Atlanta and Miami have Gini coefficients around 0.57, and New Orleans reaches 0.56 [Source: ev-11]. Using an "80/20" ratio analysis (top 80% vs bottom 20%), New Orleans has the highest income inequality among major U.S. cities at 7.87, meaning top earners make $110,820 while the bottom quintile earns only $14,078 [Source: ev-4] [Source: ev-11]. Boston follows closely with a ratio of 7.81 ($172,476 for the 80th percentile vs $22,095 for the 20th) [Source: ev-4] [Source: ev-11].
    *   **Urban Core vs Suburbs:** Urban cores concentrate both high-earning professionals and low-income service workers, leading to higher inequality than suburbs which maintain more moderate distributions [Source: ev-11]. For instance, in Atlanta, city-level income disparity (high earners making 17.5 times that of low earners) is twice as large as the metro-wide disparity [Source: ev-5].

### Affordability Crisis
Housing costs have outpaced wage growth significantly since 2000.

*   **Price-to-Income Gap:** Since 2000, median household income in the U.S. grew by 92% (from $41,990 to $80,610), while median home prices nearly tripled, jumping 177% from $122,775 to $339,937 [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. On an inflation-adjusted basis, incomes increased just 8.5%, whereas home prices rose more than 56% [Source: ev-6].
*   **Regional Extremes:** The national price-to-income ratio is approximately 4.6 or 4.7 [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. However, five of the six large U.S. cities with the highest ratios are in California (Los Angeles, San Jose, Long Beach, San Francisco, and San Diego), ranging from 9.6 to 12.2—more than double the national average [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. Other major cities like New York City, Miami, and Boston also face significant pressures where home prices far outpace local incomes [Source: ev-6]. The Case-Shiller Index reached 325.78 in July 2024 representing a 225% increase since January 2000 [Source: ev-11].

### Displacement and Racial Stratification
Gentrification has led to displacement that disproportionately affects minority communities due to structural housing market constraints.

*   **Racial Demographics:** Urban areas have become less white; the share of non-Hispanic whites fell by 7 points in urban cores and 8 points in suburbs since 2000 [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. Today, 53% of urban counties are majority nonwhite, compared to only about one-in-ten suburban (10%) and rural (11%) counties [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. Conversely, gentrifying neighborhoods specifically became whiter during their transformation [Source: ev-1].
*   **Displacement Patterns:** Research on Philadelphia indicates that poor residents moving from historically Black gentrifying neighborhoods tend to move to poorer non-gentrifying neighborhoods within the city. In contrast, residents moving from other (non-predominantly Black) gentrifying neighborhoods often move to wealthier areas in the city or suburbs [Source: ev-9] [Source: ev-11]. This suggests that minority communities face "additional constraints" when displaced, limiting them to a shrinking set of affordable yet disadvantaged neighborhoods [Source: ev-9].
*   **Educational Attainment:** Urban cores have seen an increase in educational attainment associated with this demographic shift; 35% of urban residents and 31% in suburbs hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, compared to 19% in rural counties [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11].

In summary, from 1980 to 2024, American cities experienced a fundamental transformation where service-industry growth fueled gentrification in major metro cores. This process widened income inequality (with Gini coefficients rising above 0.49 nationally) and created severe housing affordability crises (price-to-income ratios up to 12.2). The resulting displacement has been racially stratified, with minority communities facing fewer residential options than their white counterparts [Source: ev-3] [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-9]
