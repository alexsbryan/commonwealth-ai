A US court found Google's search business a monopoly because it violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act by engaging in exclusionary conduct through default-distribution deals [Source: ev-1]. Specifically, Judge Amit Mehta ruled on August 5, 2024, that Google is a monopolist in general search services and general search text advertising [Source: ev-1]. The theory of harm was that Google paid billions to companies like Apple, Samsung, and Mozilla to be their default search engine; for instance, CEO Sundar Pichai testified that Google spent approximately $26.3 billion on distribution in 2021, and evidence showed Google paid Apple roughly 36% of Safari search-ad revenue [Source: ev-1]. This practice created a self-reinforcing loop where defaults generated scale, which led to data collection, improved quality, and protected the default status against rivals [Source: ev-1].

The remedies ordered by Judge Mehta on August 5, 2 marking the first forced divestiture of a major tech company since the Microsoft case of 2000, included requiring Google to sell its Chrome browser [Source: ev-1]. Additionally, Google was prohibited from paying for default search placement through revenue-sharing agreements and faced limits on acquisitions and AI-related investments [Source: ev-1]. While Google appealed to the DC Circuit in 2025 arguing that the ruling punished procompetitive innovation, the final outcome remained pending through 2025 [Source: ev-1].
