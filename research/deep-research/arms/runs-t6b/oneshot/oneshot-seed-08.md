A US court found Google’s search business a monopoly because Judge Amit Mehta ruled on 2024-08-05 that the company violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act by acting as a monopolist in general search services and text advertising [Source: ev-1]. The primary theory of harm was that Google engaged in exclusionary conduct through default-distribution deals, paying companies like Apple, Samsung, and Mozilla billions to be their default search engine [Source: ev-1]. This practice created a self-reinforcing loop where defaults generated scale, which produced data, improved quality, and protected the default status against rivals [Source: ev-1]. Evidence showed this involved significant payments; for instance, CEO Sundar Pichai testified that Google paid about $26.3 billion for distribution in 2021, and trial evidence indicated Google paid Apple roughly 36% of Safari search-ad revenue [Source: ev-1].

The remedies ordered by Judge Mehta on 2025-08-05 included structural changes requiring Google to sell its Chrome browser, marking the first forced divestiture of a major tech company since the Microsoft case of 2000 [Source: ev-1]. Additionally, the court prohibited Google from paying for default search placement or engaging in revenue-sharing agreements, and imposed limits on acquisitions and AI-related investments [Source: ev-1]. While Google appealed these rulings to the DC Circuit in 2025 arguing they punished procompetitive innovation, the final outcome remained pending through 2025 [Source: ev-1].
