A US court found Google’s search business to be a monopoly because it violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act by engaging in exclusionary conduct through default-distribution deals [Source: ev-1]. Judge Amit Mehta ruled on August 5, 2024, that Google is a monopolist in general search services and general search text advertising [Source: ev-1]. The theory of harm centered on Google paying companies like Apple, Samsung, and Mozilla billions of dollars to secure its status as the default search engine [Source: ev-1]. Evidence showed that Google paid approximately $26.3 billion for distribution in 2021, with CEO Sundar Pichai testifying to these figures [Source: ev-1]. Specifically, trial evidence indicated that Google paid Apple roughly 36% of Safari search-ad revenue [Source: ev-1]. The court determined this created a self-reinforcing loop where defaults create scale, which creates data, leading to quality improvements that protect the default position—a barrier rivals could not break [Source: ev-1].

Additionally, Google was prohibited from paying for default search placement or entering into revenue-sharing agreements and faces limits on acquisitions and AI-related investments [Source: ev-1]. Google appealed this decision to the DC Circuit Court of Appeals, arguing that the ruling punished procompetitive innovation, with the final outcome remaining pending through 2025 [Source: ev-1].
