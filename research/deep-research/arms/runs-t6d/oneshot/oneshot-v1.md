Based on the evidence provided, American cities have undergone a fundamental transformation between 1980 and 2024, characterized by accelerating gentrification, widening income inequality, deteriorating housing affordability, and racially stratified displacement.

**Gentrification Trends (1970–2013)**
Gentrification has increased steadily since the 1970s, with levels at their lowest in that decade before rising every subsequent year through 2010 [Source: ev-10]. The term was first coined in 1963 [Source: ev-1]. In the decades following 1980, large metro areas drew an increasing share of young adults, with nearly 80% of the country’s growth in its under-45 population concentrated in metros over one million residents between 1980 and later periods [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11].

*   **Acceleration:** Gentrification greatly accelerated after 2000. Nearly 20% of lower-income neighborhoods experienced gentrification since 2000, compared to only 9% during the 1990s [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11]. Rates increased in 39 of the 50 cities reviewed by Governing [Source: ev-1].
*   **Prevalence:** Despite acceleration, gentrification remains rare nationally; only 8% of all neighborhoods reviewed (including those ineligible) experienced it since the 2000 Census [Source: ev-1]. In most cities, less than one-fifth of poorer neighborhoods gentrified [Source: ev-1].
*   **City-Specific Extremes:** A select group of cities saw extensive change. Portland, OR had the highest rate with 58.1% of eligible tracts gentrifying (36 out of 142 total tracts in that category context), followed by Washington, D.C. at 51.9%, Minneapolis, MN at 50.6%, and Seattle, WA at 50% [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11]. New York City recorded the highest raw number of gentrified tracts (128) among all cities reviewed [Source: ev-1]. Conversely, El Paso, TX and Arlington, TX saw 0% gentrification rates [Source: ev-1].
*   **Types:** Income gentrification was rare in the 1970s and 1980s but became dominant by the 2000s; occupational gentrification was most common in the 1970 [Source: ev-10] [Source: ev-11].

**Income Inequality Expansion**
Inequality has widened significantly across U.S. metropolitan areas over four decades. The national Gini coefficient reached a record level of 0.485 in 2018, rising to 0.488 in 2020 and 0.494 in 2021 per pre-tax money income data from the Census Bureau [Source: ev-3]. Federal Reserve data tracking over 3,140 inequality series confirms that inequality reached record levels by 2018–2021, with the U.S. showing the sharpest increase among advanced economies since the 198 [Source: ev-11].

*   **Metro vs. National:** In 2014, the "95/20" household income ratio (top 5% to bottom 20%) was 9.3 for the United States, but rose to 9.7 for the 100 largest metro areas and 11.8 for big cities within those metros [Source: ev-5].
*   **Trends Since Recession:** Between 2007 and 2014, national inequality increased as top incomes remained stable or declined modestly while low-income households suffered substantial drops; specifically, low-income household earnings fell in 81 of the 100 largest metropolitan areas during this period [Source: ev-5] [Source: ev-11].
*   **Extreme Inequality Cities:** New York City exhibits a Gini coefficient of 0.5469, significantly higher than the national average of roughly 0.40 recorded around 2013 [Source: ev-2] [Source: ev-11]. Atlanta and Miami follow with coefficients near 0.57, and New Orleans at 0.56 [Source: ev-11]. Using an 80/20 income ratio analysis (top 80% vs bottom 20%), New Orleans has the highest inequality among major cities with a ratio of 7.87, where the 80th percentile earns $110,820 compared to $14,078 for the 20th percentil [Source: ev-4] [Source: ev-11]. Boston follows closely with a ratio of 7.81 ($172,476 vs $22,095) [Source: ev-4] [Source: ev-11]. In terms of extreme disparities, Atlanta and Washington D.C. have seen 95/20 ratios reach 18:1 in recent analyses [Source: ev-11].

**Housing Affordability Crisis**
The gap between income growth and housing costs has widened dramatically since 200 [Source: ev-6].

*   **Price vs. Income:** Since 2000, median household income grew by 92% (from $41,990 to $80,610), while median home prices jumped 177% (from $122,775 to $339,937) [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. On an inflation-adjusted basis, incomes rose only 8.5%, whereas home prices increased more than 56% [Source: ev-6].
*   **Ratios:** The national price-to-income ratio is approximately 4.6 or 4.7 depending on the source cited [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. However, five of the six large U.S. cities with the highest ratios are in California (Los Angeles, San Jose, Long Beach, San Francisco, and San Diego), ranging from 9.6 to 12.2—more than double the national average [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. Other major cities like New York City, Miami, and Boston also face severe pressures where prices outpace incomes [Source: ev-6].
*   **Market Index:** The Case-Shiller Index reached 325.78 in July 2024, representing a 225% increase since January 200 [Source: ev-11].

**Demographic Shifts and Displacement**
Gentrification has altered the racial and educational demographics of urban cores while displacing long-time residents in racially uneven ways.

*   **Racial Composition:** Since 2000, the white share of the population fell by 7 points in urban cores and 8 points in suburbs [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. Consequently, 53% of urban counties are now majority nonwhite, compared to only about one-in-ten (10%) suburban countie [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. Among urban residents, 44% are white, whereas rural counties remain 79% whit [Source: ev-7].
*   **Education:** Urban areas have seen an increase in educational attainment; today, 35% of urban residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, compared with 31% in suburbs and 19% in rural area [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. Gentrifying tracts specifically recorded increases in the percentage of adults with bachelors’ degrees as part of their eligibility criteria for gentrification status [Source: ev-1].
*   **Economic Outcomes by Neighborhood Type:** Gentrifying neighborhoods experienced population growth (average +6.5%) and saw poverty rates drop slightly (-0.7 percentage points), while becoming whiter (+4.3 percentage points) [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11]. In contrast, lower-income areas that did *not* gentrify suffered slight population losses (-2.4%), became less white (-5.0 percentage points), and saw average poverty rates climb nearly 7 percent (+6.7 percentage point [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11].
*   **Displacement Patterns:** Research on Philadelphia indicates that displacement is racially stratified. Poor residents moving from historically Black gentrifying neighborhoods tend to move to poorer non-gentrifying neighborhoods within the city, facing a shrinking set of affordable options due to discriminatory lending practices and housing market constraints [Source: ev-9] [Source: ev-11]. In contrast, financially disadvantaged residents moving from non-predominantly Black gentrifying areas often benefit by moving to more advantaged locations in cities or suburb [Source: ev-9]. This dynamic exacerbates neighborhood inequality by race and class, as "gentrification is reconfiguring the urban landscape by shrinking residential options... for disadvantaged residents" while expanding them for others [Source: ev-9].

**Economic Structural Shifts**
The underlying economic engine driving these changes shifted away from manufacturing toward service industries. Manufacturing jobs peaked at 19.5 million in 1979 but fell to under 11.5 million during the pandemic era, while employment in finance and professional/business services soared from 12 million to 32 millio [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11]. This shift concentrated high-income professionals in large metro areas where white-collar service industries cluster, further fueling income polarization and housing demand in urban core [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11].
