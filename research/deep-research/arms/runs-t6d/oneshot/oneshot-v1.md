Based on the evidence provided, American cities underwent significant transformation between 1980 and 2024 (with data points extending to mid-2024), characterized by accelerated gentrification, widening income inequality, deteriorating housing affordability relative to wages, and racially stratified displacement.

### Gentrification Trends
Gentrification has increased substantially over four decades, accelerating sharply after 2000 [Source: ev-1]. The term was coined in 1963 [Source: ev-1], but academic analysis of Census data from 1970–2010 shows that while levels were lowest in the 1970s, they rose every decade until 2010 [Source: ev-10]. Specifically, the rate of gentrification (the share of eligible lower-income tracts experiencing change) doubled from **9%** in the 1990s to nearly **20%** for the period following the 2000 Census [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11].

*   **Acceleration:** In **39** of the **50** largest cities reviewed by *Governing*, gentrification rates increased compared to previous decades [Source: ev-1].
*   **Intensity by City:** A select group of cities experienced extensive transformation. Portland, OR had the highest rate with **58.1%** of its eligible tracts gentrifying (affecting **36** out of **142** total tracts) [Source: ev-1]. Washington, D.C. followed at **51.9%** (**54** tracts), Minneapolis at **50.6%** (**39** tracts), and Seattle at **50%** (**7** tracts) [Source: ev-1] [Source: ev-11]. New York City recorded the highest raw number of gentrified tracts at **128**, despite a lower percentage rate of **29.8%** due to its large base of eligible neighborhoods [Source: ev-1].
*   **Types:** Income-based gentrification was rare in the 1970–1980s but became dominant by 2000, while occupational gentrification (increases in professional occupations) was most common in the early decades [Source: ev-10] [Source: ev-11].

### Widening Inequality
Income inequality has widened significantly across U.S. cities and metros from 2007/2013 through 2024.

*   **National Metrics:** The national Gini coefficient reached a record high of **0.485** in 2018, rising to **0.488** in 2020 and **0.494** in 2021 according to pre-tax money income data [Source: ev-3]. This contrasts with the national average Gini of approximately **0.40** cited for 2013 [Source: ev-11].
*   **Metro vs. City Disparities:** In 2014, the "95/20" ratio (income at the 95th percentile divided by the 20th) was **9.3** nationally but rose to **9.7** across the 100 largest metro areas and **11.8** within their large cities [Source: ev-5]. Between 2007 and 2014, this national ratio increased from **8.5** to **9.3**, with **57** of the 100 largest metros showing significantly higher inequality in 2014 than in 2007 [Source: ev-5] [Source: ev-11].
*   **Specific City Inequality:** New York City exhibits extreme inequality with a Gini coefficient of **0.5469** [Source: ev-2] [Source: ev-11]. Using an "80/20" income multiplier (top quintile vs. bottom quintile), New Orleans has the highest ratio at **7.87** (income at 80th percentile is $110,820; at 20th is $14,078) [Source: ev-4] [Source: ev-11]. Boston follows closely with a ratio of **7.81** ($172,476 vs. $22,095) [Source: ev-4]. Newark (**6.8**) and Jersey City (**6.1**) also rank in the top tier for inequality among major cities [Source: ev-4].
*   **Drivers:** Inequality rose because while high incomes were stable or declined modestly post-recession, bottom-quintile incomes dropped substantially; low-income households saw significant income declines in **81** of 100 large metros between 2007–2014 [Source: ev-5].

### Housing Affordability Crisis
The gap between wage growth and housing costs has widened dramatically since 2000.

*   **Price vs. Income Growth:** From 2000 to recent years (data cited through mid-2024 context), median household income grew by **92%** (from $41,990 to $80,610) or just **8.5%** on an inflation-adjusted basis, while median home prices jumped **177%** (from $122,775 to $339,937) [Source: ev-6]. The Case-Shiller Index reached **325.78** in July 2024, a **225%** increase since January 2000 [Source: ev-11].
*   **Price-to-Income Ratios:** The national price-to-income ratio is approximately **4.6** (or **4.7**) [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11]. However, top states and cities show much higher ratios. Five of the six large U.S. cities with the highest ratios are in California—Los Angeles, San Jose, Long Beach, San Francisco, and San Diego—with ratios ranging from **9.6** to **12.2**, more than double the national average [Source: ev-6] [Source: ev-11].

### Demographic Shifts and Displacement
The economic shift away from manufacturing toward service industries has altered demographics and driven displacement that disproportionately affects minority communities.

*   **Economic & Demographic Realignment:** Manufacturing jobs fell from **19.5 million** in 1979 to under **11.5 million** during the pandemic, while finance and professional services grew from **12 million** to **32 million** [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11]. This concentrated high-income professionals in urban cores. Since 1980, nearly **80%** of the country’s growth in the population under age **45** was concentrated in metro areas with over one million residents [Source: ev-8] [Source: ev-11].
*   **Racial Composition Changes:** The white share of the population fell by **7 points** in urban cores and **8 points** in suburbs since 2000 (compared to only **3 points** in rural counties) [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. Consequently, **53%** of urban counties are now majority nonwhite, compared to only **10%** of suburban counties and **11%** of rural counties [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11]. Urban areas also have higher educational attainment, with **35%** of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or more, versus **31%** in suburbs and **19%** in rural areas [Source: ev-7] [Source: ev-11].
*   **Displacement Patterns:** Gentrifying neighborhoods saw population increases (**6.5%**) and became whiter (non-Hispanic white share increased by an average of **4.3** percentage points), while poverty rates dropped slightly (**0.7** percentage point decrease) [Source: ev-1]. In contrast, lower-income tracts that did *not* gentrify experienced population losses (**2.4%**) and saw their non-Hispanic white share decline by **5.0** percentage points, with poverty rates climbing nearly **7%** (specifically a **6.7** percentage point increase in the data table for eligible non-gentrifying tracts) [Source: ev-1].
*   **Racially Stratified Outcomes:** Research on Philadelphia indicates that displacement is not uniform. Poor residents moving from historically Black gentrifying neighborhoods tend to move to poorer non-gentrifying areas within the city, whereas those from other gentrifying neighborhoods often move to wealthier suburbs or cities [Source: ev-9] [Source: ev-11]. This suggests that while gentrification may offer mobility benefits for some disadvantaged groups, it exacerbates inequality and limits options for Black communities due to racially stratified housing markets [Source: ev-9].
