A US court found Google’s search business to be a monopoly because Judge Amit Mehta of the US District Court for the District of Columbia ruled on 2024-08-05 that Google violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act by acting as a monopolist in general search services and general search text advertising [Source: ev-1]. The primary theory of harm was that Google engaged in exclusionary conduct through default-distribution deals, paying companies like Apple, Samsung, and Mozilla billions of dollars to remain the default search engine [Source: ev-1]. Evidence indicated that Google paid approximately $26.3 billion for distribution in 2021 and gave Apple roughly 36% of Safari search-ad revenue [Source: ev-1]. Judge Mehta determined this created a self-reinforcing loop where defaults generated scale, which produced data, improved quality, and protected the default status against rivals [Source: ev-1].

On 2025-08-05, the court ordered structural remedies requiring Google to sell its Chrome browser, marking the first forced divestiture of a major tech company since the Microsoft case of 2000 [Source: ev-1]. Additionally, Google was prohibited from paying for default search placement or entering into revenue-sharing agreements, and it faces limits on acquisitions and AI-related investments [Source: ev-1]. While Google appealed to the DC Circuit in 2025 arguing the ruling punished procompetitive innovation, the final outcome remained pending through 2025 [Source: ev-1].
