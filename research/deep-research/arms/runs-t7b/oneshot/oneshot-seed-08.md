A US court found Google’s search business to be a monopoly because Judge Amit Mehta of the US District Court for the District of Columbia ruled on 2024-08-05 that Google violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act by acting as a monopolist in general search services and text advertising [Source: ev-1]. The primary theory of harm was that Google engaged in exclusionary conduct through default-distribution deals, paying companies like Apple, Samsung, and Mozilla billions to remain their default search engine [Source: ev-1]. Evidence indicated that CEO Sundar Pichai testified Google paid approximately $26.3 billion for distribution in 2021, with trial evidence showing payments to Apple amounted to roughly 36% of Safari search-ad revenue [Source: ev-1]. The court determined these practices created a self-reinforcing loop where defaults generated scale, data, and quality advantages that protected the monopoly position from rivals [Source: ev-1].

Regarding remedies, on 2025-08-05, Judge Mehta ordered structural measures requiring Google to sell its Chrome browser, marking the first forced divestiture of a major tech company since the Microsoft case of 2000 [Source: ev-1]. Additionally, Google was prohibited from paying for default search placement or entering into revenue-sharing agreements with distributors [Source: ev-1]. The ruling also imposed limits on future acquisitions and AI-related investments by the company [Source: ev-1]. Although Google appealed these decisions to the DC Circuit in 2025 arguing they punished procompetitive innovation, the final outcome remained pending through 2025 [Source: ev-1].
