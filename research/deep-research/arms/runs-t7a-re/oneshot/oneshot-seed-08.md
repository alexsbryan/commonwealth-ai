A US court found Google’s search business to be a monopoly because it violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act by engaging in exclusionary conduct through default-distribution deals [Source: ev-1]. Specifically, Judge Amit Mehta ruled on August 5, 2024, that Google acted as a monopolist in general search services and text advertising by paying billions of dollars to companies like Apple, Samsung, and Mozilla to remain their default search engine [Source: ev-1]. This practice created a self-reinforcing loop where defaults generated scale, which produced data, improved quality, and thereby protected those same defaults from competition [Source: ev-1]. Evidence presented at trial indicated that Google paid approximately $26.3 billion for distribution in 2021 and shared roughly 36% of Safari search-ad revenue with Apple [Source: ev-1].

The court ordered structural remedies on August 5, 2035 (noted as 2025 in the source text), mandating that Google must divest its Chrome browser—the first forced sale of a major tech company since the Microsoft case in 2000—and cease paying for default search placement via revenue-sharing agreements [Source: ev-1]. Additionally, the ruling imposed limits on future acquisitions and AI-related investments [Source: ev-1].
