A US court found Google's search business a monopoly because Judge Amit Mehta of the US District Court for the District of Columbia ruled on 2024-08-05 that Google violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act by acting as a monopolist in general search services and general search text advertising [Source: ev-1]. The primary theory of harm was "default-distribution deals," where Google paid companies like Apple, Samsung, and Mozilla billions to remain their default search engine [Source: ev-1]. Trial evidence indicated that Google’s CEO Sundar Pichai testified the company spent about $26.3 billion on distribution in 2021, and specifically paid Apple roughly 36% of Safari search-ad revenue [Source: ev-1]. The court determined this conduct was exclusionary because it created a self-reinforcing loop where defaults generate scale, which creates data, improves quality, and protects the default status against rivals [Source: ev-1].

The remedies ordered by Judge Mehta on 2025-08-05 included structural changes requiring Google to sell its Chrome browser, marking the first forced divestiture of a major tech company since the Microsoft case of 2000 [Source: ev-1]. Additionally, Google is prohibited from paying for default search placement through revenue-sharing agreements and faces limits on acquisitions and AI-related investments [Source: ev-1]. Although Google appealed these decisions to the DC Circuit in 2025, arguing that the rulings punished procompetitive innovation, the final outcome remained pending as of 2025 [Source: ev-1].
