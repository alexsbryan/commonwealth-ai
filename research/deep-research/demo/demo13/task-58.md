# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants and Animals: Beyond Microbial Paradigms

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT), the movement of genetic material between organisms without sexual reproduction, has been recognized as a major evolutionary force in prokaryotes for decades. However, recent genomic studies have revealed that HGT is far more common in eukaryotes, particularly plants and animals, than previously assumed. This report examines the increasing evidence for non-microbial HGT, its mechanisms, evolutionary significance, and functional impacts. Notably, while once considered rare in complex multicellular organisms, advanced genomic analyses now suggest that 90.2% of studied eukaryotes host horizontally transferred genes, challenging our traditional understanding of genetic inheritance and evolution in plants and animals[^1_10].

## The Shifting Paradigm: HGT in Eukaryotic Evolution

Horizontal gene transfer has traditionally been viewed as a process primarily relevant to prokaryotes, with limited evolutionary significance in complex multicellular eukaryotes such as animals and plants[^1_3]. This perception stemmed partly from the presumed barriers to gene transfer in organisms with a segregated germline and complex reproductive systems. However, the advent of next-generation sequencing technologies has dramatically transformed this understanding, revealing that HGT events are much more widespread in eukaryotes than previously recognized[^1_1][^1_2].

Recent genome analyses have demonstrated that HGT is not merely an occasional occurrence but a dynamic process that has been continuously shaping eukaryotic genomes throughout their evolutionary history[^1_1]. The acquisition of foreign genetic material has proven to be an important mechanism for introducing genetic novelties, facilitating phenotypic variation, and enabling adaptation to changing environments. As one researcher succinctly noted, HGT allows organisms to "spread evolutionary success" by rapidly acquiring beneficial traits that evolved in distantly related species[^1_3].

The increasing recognition of HGT's importance in eukaryotic evolution reflects a broader shift away from the strictly vertical inheritance model that has dominated biological thought since Darwin. While vertical transmission through reproduction remains the primary mode of genetic inheritance in eukaryotes, the complementary role of horizontal gene movement is proving to be far more significant than once believed[^1_5].

## HGT in Plants: From Acquisition to Adaptation

### Evidence and Prevalence

Plants have emerged as significant recipients of horizontally transferred genetic material. The evidence for plant HGT ranges from ancient transfers that facilitated major evolutionary transitions to recent acquisitions that continue to shape plant genomes. Genome analyses have identified numerous gene families in plants that originated from prokaryotes, fungi, or viruses[^1_1].

In the moss *Physcomitrella patens*, researchers identified 57 families of nuclear genes that were acquired from prokaryotes, fungi, or viruses[^1_3]. Many of these gene families were transferred to the ancestors of green or land plants, suggesting that HGT played a pivotal role in early plant evolution. Experimental evidence indicates that these horizontally acquired genes are involved in essential or plant-specific functions such as xylem formation, plant defense, nitrogen recycling, and the biosynthesis of starch, polyamines, hormones, and glutathione[^1_3].

### Mechanisms of Plant HGT

Several mechanisms facilitate horizontal gene transfer in plants. One well-studied example is the transfer of tumor-inducing genes (T-DNAs) from *Agrobacterium* species into host plant genomes[^1_2]. This natural genetic engineering process has been documented in *Nicotiana*, *Linaria*, and *Ipomoea* species, where the transferred genes have been maintained in the germline and appear to have evolved roles distinct from their original disease-causing functions[^1_2].

Plant parasitism represents another major pathway for HGT. Parasitic plants form intimate connections with their hosts through specialized structures called haustoria, which enable them to extract nutrients-and occasionally genetic material-from their hosts[^1_4][^1_14]. The observation of massive mRNA movement between parasitic plants like *Cuscuta* and their hosts suggests a likely route for HGT via mRNA intermediates[^1_4].

### The Special Case of Parasitic Plants

Parasitic plants serve as particularly interesting models for studying HGT in eukaryotes. Research on parasitic members of the Orobanchaceae family has revealed a striking correlation between the degree of host dependence and the frequency of HGT events[^1_4][^1_14].

A comparative study of three parasitic Orobanchaceae members (*Triphysaria versicolor*, *Striga hermonthica*, and *Phelipanche aegyptiaca*) and one non-parasitic member (Lindenbergia) found 52 instances of non-sexual DNA transfers between the parasitic plants and their hosts[^1_14]. The frequency of these transfers increased with the degree of parasitism: only one likely HGT event was detected in the non-parasitic Lindenbergia, two in the facultative hemiparasite *T. versicolor*, ten in the obligate hemiparasite *Striga*, and thirty-four in the obligate holoparasite *Phelipanche*[^1_4].

This pattern can be explained by several factors. Obligate parasites, which require host plant-induced germination stimulation, come into contact with host plants from a very early developmental stage, increasing the chances that cells experiencing HGT events will develop into germ-line tissues[^1_4]. Additionally, some obligate parasites like *P. aegyptiaca* form direct phloem connections with their hosts, creating pathways for more extensive genetic exchange[^1_4].

## HGT in Animals: Breaking Barriers

### Evidence and Diversity of Animal HGT

While historically considered rare in animals, evidence for HGT has been documented across diverse animal lineages, including sponges, cnidarians, rotifers, nematodes, molluscs, arthropods, and vertebrates[^1_12]. These transfers originate from various donors, including bacteria, fungi, plants, and other animals.

In sponges, research on *Astroclera willeyana* revealed an ancient bacterial gene transfer that contributed to biomineralization processes[^1_12]. In cnidarians, the starlet sea anemone (*Nematostella vectensis*) acquired bacterial genes involved in the shikimic acid and glyoxilate pathways, while the *Hydra magnipapillata* genome contains 71 genes of bacterial origin, many encoding sugar-modifying enzymes[^1_12].

Arthropods present numerous examples of HGT associated with specific adaptations. Many plant-feeding insects and mites possess genes acquired through HGT that enable them to counter plant defenses and efficiently assimilate plant-produced metabolites[^1_7]. In 2021, researchers reported the first known example of horizontal gene transfer between a plant and an animal: the whitefly (*Bemisia tabaci*) acquired a gene from plants it feeds on that protects it from plant toxins, allowing it to feed on otherwise defended plants[^1_15].

Even vertebrates, once thought to be largely immune to HGT due to their sequestered germline, show evidence of gene acquisition. Type II antifreeze proteins are found in three distantly related fish lineages (Atlantic herring, smelts, and sea ravens), with genetic analyses suggesting that smelts received an antifreeze protein gene from herring more than 150 million years after their ancestors diverged[^1_6].

### The Remarkable Case of Bdelloid Rotifers

Bdelloid rotifers represent perhaps the most dramatic example of HGT in animals. These microscopic invertebrates, which reproduce exclusively through parthenogenesis (asexual reproduction), have acquired a remarkable number of genes from bacteria, fungi, and plants[^1_12][^1_13].

Analysis of the *Adineta vaga* genome revealed that approximately 8% of its genes are of foreign origin, with evidence of both ancient and recent transfers[^1_12]. A similar study of the *Adineta ricciae* transcriptome confirmed that around 10% of expressed genes originated from foreign sources, with potential contributions from as many as 533 donor organisms[^1_12].

Many of these acquired genes encode enzymes involved in biochemical functions absent in other metazoans, such as toxic compound degradation, resource acquisition, and antioxidant biosynthesis[^1_12]. It has been suggested that this massive horizontal gene transfer provides genetic variability that compensates for the absence of sexual reproduction in bdelloid rotifers and may contribute to their extraordinary tolerance to desiccation[^1_12].

The mechanism behind this extensive HGT may relate to the rotifer life cycle. During periods of desiccation and subsequent rehydration, gut cells may enter a transient competent state for DNA uptake, allowing them to acquire foreign DNA from food particles or free DNA absorbed on mineral surfaces[^1_12]. The close proximity of the gut and ovary in these animals could facilitate the integration of this foreign DNA into the germline[^1_12].

## Functional Impacts of HGT in Plants and Animals

### Adaptation to New Environments

Horizontally transferred genes have contributed significantly to environmental adaptation in both plants and animals. In early land plants, HGT appears to have facilitated the transition from aquatic to terrestrial environments[^1_3]. Many of the genes acquired by the moss *Physcomitrella patens* from prokaryotes, fungi, and viruses are involved in activities essential for terrestrial life, such as xylem formation and plant defense[^1_3].

In animals, HGT has enabled adaptation to specialized ecological niches. Fish living in icy waters have acquired antifreeze proteins through HGT that lower the freezing point of their blood by a full degree Celsius[^1_6]. Similarly, the acquisition of enterobactin-producing genes by nectar-dwelling yeasts from insect gut bacteria enhances their iron acquisition capabilities in the nectar environment[^1_6].

### Metabolic Innovations

Many horizontally transferred genes encode enzymes that expand the metabolic capabilities of recipient organisms. In bdelloid rotifers, a significant fraction of acquired genes encodes glycosyl hydrolases, which may contribute to their diverse nutritional strategies[^1_12]. Similarly, in *Hydra magnipapillata*, many horizontally acquired genes encode sugar-modifying enzymes that likely expand its metabolic repertoire[^1_12].

In parasitic plants, HGT has contributed to their specialized metabolism. The transfer of active hAT DNA transposon elements from Brassicaceae to *Phelipanche* and *Orobanche* appears to have played a role in their divergence from a common ancestor[^1_14]. The acquisition of the albumin 1 KNOTTIN-like protein gene by dodder (a parasitic plant) from legumes represents another example of metabolic innovation through HGT[^1_14].

### Defense and Counter-Defense

HGT has been pivotal in evolutionary arms races between plants and their consumers. Plants have acquired genes involved in defense mechanisms, while herbivores have countered by acquiring genes that neutralize plant toxins.

A striking example is the acquisition of bacterial toxin genes of the aerolysin family by cnidarians, which provides them with potent defense mechanisms[^1_12]. On the other side of this evolutionary conflict, phytophagous arthropods have acquired genes that allow them to detoxify plant defensive compounds[^1_7]. The whitefly's acquisition of the *BtPMaT1* gene from plants enables it to neutralize phenolic glycosides, toxins that many plants produce to defend themselves against insect pests[^1_15].

## Mechanisms and Vectors of Eukaryotic HGT

### Direct DNA Transfer

Direct DNA transfer can occur through various mechanisms, including the uptake of exogenous DNA from the environment. In bdelloid rotifers, periods of desiccation and rehydration may induce a transient competence state for DNA uptake in gut cells, allowing the acquisition of foreign genetic material[^1_12]. Similarly, some plants may acquire DNA directly from their environment, though this process is less well understood than in bacteria.

### Vectors and Intermediaries

Various vectors can facilitate HGT between eukaryotes. Parasites and symbionts often serve as bridges for genetic material. For example, parasitic plants can acquire genes from their hosts, while hosts occasionally acquire genes from their parasites[^1_4][^1_14]. Endosymbionts like *Wolbachia* have been recognized as major sources of DNA in the nuclei of their arthropod hosts[^1_5].

Transposable elements play a significant role in eukaryotic HGT. The transmission of transposons between rice and millet species represents a clear example of transposon-mediated genetic exchange in plants[^1_14]. In animals, the *BovB* retrotransposon, which comprises nearly one-fifth of the cow genome, has moved between mammals and reptiles through parasitic vectors like ticks and leeches[^1_6].

More recently identified mediators of HGT include exosomes, apoptotic bodies, and cell-free DNA, which may have clinical implications in addition to their evolutionary significance[^1_14].

## Detecting HGT: Challenges and Opportunities

The identification of HGT events in eukaryotes presents significant challenges. The primary method involves detecting phylogenetic incongruence-genes whose evolutionary history differs markedly from that of the organism in which they reside. Advanced genomic approaches, including comparative genomics and phylogenomics, have greatly enhanced our ability to identify these events[^1_1][^1_2].

However, several factors can complicate HGT detection. Contamination of DNA samples can lead to false positives, as demonstrated by a critical reevaluation of claimed interspecific genetic exchanges in bdelloid rotifers[^1_13]. Additionally, extensive gene loss in some lineages can create patterns that mimic HGT, requiring careful phylogenetic analysis to distinguish between these scenarios[^1_12].

Despite these challenges, next-generation sequencing technologies have opened new perspectives for studying HGT through comparative genomic approaches[^1_1]. Systematic evaluations of HGT between viruses and eukaryotes have identified thousands of transfers, revealing their frequency, taxonomic distribution, and projected functions[^1_11].

## Evolutionary Implications and Future Perspectives

### Rethinking Eukaryotic Evolution

The prevalence of HGT in eukaryotes challenges traditional views of evolution that emphasize vertical inheritance and gradual change. As one study noted, while vertical transmission through reproduction ensures the maintenance of species integrity, HGT introduces genetic novelties that can rapidly accelerate adaptation[^1_1].

HGT appears to have played a particularly important role during major evolutionary transitions. The acquisition of foreign genes contributed to the transition of plants from aquatic to terrestrial environments[^1_3] and to the adaptation of phytophagous arthropods to herbivory[^1_7]. These findings suggest that HGT may be especially significant during periods of ecological opportunity or stress.

### Future Research Directions

Several key questions remain regarding HGT in plants and animals. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which genetic material moves between eukaryotes, particularly in cases not involving parasitism or symbiosis. The relative contributions of different vectors and the cellular processes that facilitate the integration of foreign DNA into eukaryotic genomes also warrant investigation.

Another important area for future research concerns the regulation and expression of horizontally transferred genes. How are these genes integrated into existing regulatory networks, and what factors determine their retention over evolutionary time? The answers to these questions will provide deeper insights into the evolutionary significance of HGT in eukaryotes.

## Conclusion

The growing evidence for horizontal gene transfer in plants and animals fundamentally alters our understanding of eukaryotic evolution. Far from being rare or inconsequential, HGT has contributed significantly to the genetic repertoire and adaptive capabilities of diverse eukaryotic lineages. From facilitating the colonization of new environments to providing novel metabolic capacities and defense mechanisms, horizontally acquired genes have played various essential roles in plant and animal evolution.

The mechanisms of eukaryotic HGT are diverse, involving direct DNA transfer, vector-mediated transfer, and the movement of transposable elements. While some pathways, such as those involving parasitism or endosymbiosis, are relatively well understood, others remain to be elucidated. As genomic technologies continue to advance, our understanding of HGT in eukaryotes will undoubtedly deepen, revealing even more examples of genetic exchange across the tree of life.

This evolving perspective on genetic inheritance highlights the interconnectedness of all living organisms. Beyond the vertical transmission of genes from parent to offspring, the horizontal movement of genetic material creates a complex network of genetic relationships that transcends traditional taxonomic boundaries. Appreciating this complexity is essential for a comprehensive understanding of evolution in the natural world.
