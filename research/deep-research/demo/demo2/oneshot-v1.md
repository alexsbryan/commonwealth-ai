Based on the evidence provided, here is how American cities changed from 1980 to 2024 across gentrification, inequality, affordability, and displacement:

**Gentrification**
*   **Acceleration:** Gentrification accelerated significantly after 2000. The rate of eligible lower-income neighborhoods experiencing gentrification doubled in the period following the 2000 Census (reaching nearly 20%) compared to the 1990s (9 percent) [Source: ev-1]. This trend was part of a long-term increase that began in the 1970s when levels were at their lowest [Source: ev-10].
*   **Definition and Types:** Academic definitions vary, but generally involve lower socioeconomic status neighborhoods seeing an influx of higher socioeconomic households through income, educational, or occupational changes [Source: ev-1], [Source: ev-10]. Income-based gentrification became rare in the 197s and 80s but increased substantially in later decades, becoming dominant by the 2000–2020 period [Source: ev-10], [Source: ev-11].
*   **Demographic Shifts:** Gentrifying tracts recorded increases in the non-Hispanic white population (an average increase of 4.3 percentage points) and declines in poverty rates (-0.7 percentage point change). In contrast, lower-income areas that did not gentrify saw population losses, an increase in minority concentration (+5.0 percentage-point change in non-white share), and a rise in poverty (+6.7 percentage points) [Source: ev-1].
*   **Geographic Concentration:** Gentrification remained rare nationally, affecting only 8 percent of all neighborhoods reviewed since 2000, but was intense in specific cities. Portland had the highest rate at 58.1% of eligible tracts, followed by Washington D.C. (51.9%), Minneapolis (50.6%), and Seattle (50%) [Source: ev-1], [Source: ev-11]. Cities like Detroit, El Paso, and Las Vegas experienced practically no gentrification [Source: ev-1].

**Inequality**
*   **National Trends:** U.S. income inequality reached record levels in recent years, with Gini coefficients rising from 0.485 in 2018 to 0.494 in 2021 [Source: ev-3]. The national 95/20 income ratio rose from 8.5 in 2007 to 9.3 in 2014 [Source: ev-5], [Source: ev-11].
*   **Urban vs. Metro Disparities:** Large metropolitan areas and their central cities tend to be more unequal than the nation as a whole. In 2014, the aggregate 95/20 ratio for the 100 largest metro areas was 9.7, while big cities within them averaged 11.8 [Source: ev-5]. Of the 97 cities analyzed, 84 had higher inequality ratios than their wider metro areas; Atlanta exhibited the largest difference, with an 18:1 ratio in the city versus a lower disparity in the region [Source: ev-5], [Source: ev-11].
*   **City-Specific Rankings:** New Orleans has the highest income inequality among major U.S. cities (ratio of 7.87), followed closely by Boston (7.81). Newark and Jersey City also rank high due to large gaps between top earners and those at the bottom [Source: ev-4], [Source: ev-11]. Cities like Chesapeake, VA have smaller discrepancies (3.8 times) due to higher incomes across quintiles [Source: ev-4].
*   **Drivers:** The shift from manufacturing jobs (which peaked in 1979 and fell below 11.5 million during the pandemic) to finance and professional services (growing from 12 million to 32 million) concentrated wealth in urban cores where white-collar industries cluster [Source: ev-8], [Source: ev-11].

**Affordability**
*   **Income vs. Housing Costs Gap:** Since 2000, median home prices nearly tripled (up 177%), while median household income grew by only 92% nominally (or just 8.5% inflation-adjusted). On an inflation-adjusted basis, home prices increased more than 56%, far outpacing income growth [Source: ev-6], [Source: ev-11].
*   **Price-to-Income Ratios:** The national price-to-income ratio is approximately 4.6–4.7. However, California cities face extreme pressures; Los Angeles, San Jose, Long Beach, San Francisco, and San Diego have ratios ranging from 9.6 to 12.2, more than double the national average [Source: ev-6], [Source: ev-11]. New York City, Miami, and Boston also see housing costs far outpace local incomes [Source: ev-6].

**Displacement and Racial Stratification**
*   **Unequal Displacement Outcomes:** Gentrification disproportionately affects minority communities with fewer relocation options. In Philadelphia, poor residents moving from historically Black gentrifying neighborhoods tended to move to poorer non-gentrifying neighborhoods within the city. Conversely, those moving from other gentrifying areas often moved to wealthier neighborhoods in the city or suburbs [Source: ev-9].
*   **Constraints on Minority Residents:** Research indicates that racially stratified housing markets and discriminatory lending practices limit where disadvantaged Black residents can go when displaced, reconfiguring the urban landscape by shrinking residential options for them while expanding them for more advantaged (often white) residents [Source: ev-9], [Source: ev-11].
*   **Policy Implications:** To combat these inequities, researchers suggest policies such as property tax relief for long-time homeowners and sustained investment in non-gentrifying neighborhoods to prevent disadvantaged movers from being concentrated into high-crime, low-quality school areas [Source: ev-9].
