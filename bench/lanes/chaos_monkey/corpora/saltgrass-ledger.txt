THE SALTGRASS LEDGER

A Tale of Glasswater Stave


CHAPTER I

The lock basin at Glasswater Stave held its water the way the village held its opinions: behind heavy gates, and grudgingly. At slack water on a grey morning the basin was a rectangle of pewter, walled in dressed stone, crossed by the shadows of mooring posts, and smelling of weed and tar and the cold iron of the sluice machinery. It was Tabb Orrison who found the harbormaster floating in it, a little after dawn, while the gulls were still arguing over the fish-gutting boards on the quay.

Tabb was the lock-keeper's apprentice, seventeen years old and not yet used to the idea that the sea kept no appointments. He had come down to grease the pawls of the eastern gate, as he did every third morning, and he saw first the harbormaster's coat — a good drab coat with horn buttons, spread on the water like a chart of some unlucky coast — and then the white hair, and then the face. He did not shout at once. He told Constable Marlock afterwards that the silence of it had seemed to forbid shouting, that the basin at slack water was like a church with the sexton drowned in the font. Then the cold sense of things returned to him and he ran up the quay yelling for help with his grease-can still in one hand.

Corwin Pellow had been harbormaster of Glasswater Stave for twenty-one years. He had a square, patient face, a habit of writing everything down, and a reputation along the whole Hethering coast for being un-bribable, which in a salvage port is a reputation with enemies attached to it. The village brought him out of the basin with a pair of gaffs and laid him on the weighing platform by the fish scales, and by seven o'clock the whole of Glasswater Stave knew that the harbormaster was drowned, and by half past seven the whole village had privately decided it was no accident, though nobody would say so above a whisper until the doctor had had his look.

Maren Ashquill heard the bell going on the quay and knew before anyone told her. She was the tide-registrar of Glasswater Stave — keeper of the tide ledger, recorder of every opening and shutting of the lock, every vessel through the gates, every height of water against the painted gauge on the basin wall. Her office was a square stone room at the head of the quay, between the harbormaster's office and the chandlery, and from its window she had watched Corwin Pellow cross the cobbles every working morning for eleven years, always at the same hour, always pausing at the gauge to verify her morning figure against his own eye. He trusted records the way other men trusted luck. It was the thing they had agreed about without ever needing to say so.

She came down to the weighing platform with her shawl pinned crooked and stood at the edge of the crowd while two fishermen kept the gulls off. The harbormaster's eyes were shut. There was a wound at his right temple, washed bloodless by the basin, the lips of it grey.

"He fell," said someone in the crowd, without conviction. "Slipped on the coping in the dark and fell."

Maren looked at the wound, and at the basin, and at the great closed gates of the lock, and said nothing at all. But she went back up to her office, and before the doctor had even been sent for she had taken down the tide ledger and turned to the night pages, because it was her habit, in any confusion, to begin with what was written down.

CHAPTER II

Glasswater Stave is not on the railway, and the coast road reaches it as if by afterthought, dropping off the saltgrass downs in eleven bends that the carrier's van takes at a walk. The village is one street deep along the harbor and two streets deep behind it, built of the grey stone of the district, roofed in slate the weather has rubbed to the color of a herring's back. What it has always had, and the reason there is a village at all, is the inner harbor: a pool of still water behind lock gates, where a vessel can lie afloat at any state of the tide while the sea outside falls away down the mud at low water for half a mile.

The lock is the village's heart, and the village keeps its heart under administration. The gates and the sluice belong to the harbor trust; the lock-keeper works them; the harbormaster commands them; and the tide-registrar records them. Strangers find the arrangement quaint until they have wintered there. On the Hethering coast the difference between a harbor with honest books and a harbor without them is the difference between credit and none: the underwriters' agents, the salvage buyers, the fish merchants up the coast — all of them deal with Glasswater Stave on the strength of its paper. A master who declares his catch there is believed at the weighbridge in Saltern Cross. A salvage lot surveyed there sells unseen. The village does not put it this way, but its ledgers are its fishery as much as the sea is.

The tide ledger is the oldest of these instruments. There has been a registrar at Glasswater Stave since the trust was chartered, and the grey canvas volumes stand shoulder to shoulder in the registrar's safe going back beyond living memory, each ruled in the same columns: date, hour, height of water, vessel, master, remarks. Heights are taken from the painted gauge on the basin wall, a ladder of black and white feet that the village children learn to read before their letters. Times are taken from the official chronometer in the harbormaster's office. Twice a year the trust's auditor comes out from Saltern Cross, sets his watch by that chronometer, walks the gauge at slack water, and signs the ledger's flyleaf; and twice a year the village treats him with the wary courtesy owed to a man who could, with one withheld signature, make every paper in the port worth less.

Salvage is the district's other trade, and the less talked of. The Saltgrass shoals east of Wrack Point have been breaking ships since ships came to the coast, and the law of what may be raised, by whom, and on what shares, is older and more argued than the fishery's. The custom of the coast is plain enough in outline: a wreck bought from the underwriters belongs to its buyer, but everything landed must pass the harbormaster's survey, lot by lot, weight by weight, before it is sold on; the survey fixes the duty owed to the trust and the truth owed to everyone else. A broker who lands his recoveries at Glasswater Stave pays for the privilege of being believed. So the village understood, better than any stranger could, what it meant that the Merrow Bell's gear had been coming up all summer and the survey of her cargo had twice been put off; and the village said nothing aloud, because the broker held the salvage rights of half the bad ground on the coast, and winter is long, and work is work.

Of the sea itself the village holds the views of people who live by a great unreliable neighbor. The tides at Glasswater Stave run twenty feet at the springs and barely nine at the neaps, and every trade in the port is timed to that fortnightly breathing. Spring tides scour the basin, float the deep-laden coasters over the bar, and fill the saltgrass creeks where the wildfowlers go; neap tides are the quiet of the month, when the water barely troubles the gauge and the lock may stand unworked for a day together. The almanac hangs by a string in every kitchen. It is the one book in Glasswater Stave that nobody has ever thought worth forging, because the moon audits it nightly.

CHAPTER III

Doctor Imbrey Fosk came over from Saltern Cross in his rattling motor-trap and made his examination on the weighing platform with the crowd pushed back to the chandlery wall. He was a long, dry, exact man who had been coroner's surgeon for the district longer than anyone could remember, and he did not talk while he worked. When he had finished he washed his hands in a bucket of seawater, dried them on his handkerchief, and gave Constable Marlock his findings in a voice pitched for the constable alone, though half the quay contrived to hear it.

Drowning, said Doctor Fosk, was the cause of death; the harbormaster's lungs had taken water, and he had been alive when he went in. But the wound at the temple had been got before the water, not in it. The coping stones of the basin were rounded and weeded; a fall against them would bruise and graze. This wound was narrow, deep at one end, clean-edged — struck, in the doctor's opinion, by an iron edge swung with force. A man so struck might be stunned past saving himself, and the basin would do the rest.

"Then it's murder," said Constable Marlock. He was a heavy, careful man who had policed the village for nine years, mostly against poachers and Saturday fights, and he said the word the way a man picks up an unfamiliar tool.

"It is drowning, with a blow before it," said Doctor Fosk. "The arrangement of those two facts is your trade, Constable, not mine."

The constable began at the beginning, which in Glasswater Stave meant the lock. The basin was tidal only through the sluice and the gates; the gates had been shut since the evening of the fourteenth, when the ketch Saltwillow had come through on the flood and Tabb Orrison had wound them to behind her. The harbormaster had been seen alive at nine that night, leaving The Cold Lantern after his usual half-pint, walking — as several drinkers swore — not towards home, but down towards the harbor office and the basin.

Widow Hetch, who kept The Cold Lantern, gave her evidence at her own bar with her arms folded. The harbormaster had sat in his corner from eight until nine, she said, and had spoken to nobody except to answer the time of day, and had seemed heavy in his mind. He had been heavy in his mind, she added, for a fortnight — ever since the salvage men had begun raising gear over the wreck of the Merrow Bell. She would say no more than that, but she looked, as she said it, at the harbor office, and the look travelled through the village faster than the words did.

Maren Ashquill spent that first day at her ledger desk, doing her work as if the bell had never rung, because work was the shape her grief naturally took. But twice she stopped still in the middle of a line. The first time was when she verified the basin gauge for the morning and found the water two hands higher than the shut gates and a neap tide could account for. The second was when she went to the harbormaster's office next door — Constable Marlock had asked her to say whether anything was missing — and saw at once that the Lyle-Hannett chronometer was gone from its case on the wall.
CHAPTER IV

The Lyle-Hannett chronometer was the only valuable thing in the harbormaster's office, and the only beautiful one. It was a marine chronometer of the old proud kind, brass-bound in a case of black walnut, made by the firm of Lyle & Hannett whose name was engraved across its face in letters like a ship's transom. It had come off a wrecked Indiaman sixty years before, had been bought at the salvage auction by the village trust, and had hung on the office wall ever since as the official timepiece of the port of Glasswater Stave. Every opening of the lock was timed by it. Every entry in Maren's tide ledger carried a time taken from it. Corwin Pellow wound it himself every Monday noon, with a ceremony he pretended was not a ceremony.

Now the walnut case hung open and the chronometer was gone, and the dust on the shelf below showed the clean square where its travelling box had stood.

"Thieves, then," said Constable Marlock, writing in his notebook. "He surprised a thief at the office, and was struck, and put in the basin."

"Then the thief," said Maren, "knew enough to take the travelling box, which was kept in the cupboard, and to take the winding key, which was kept in the desk; and knew nothing about the cash drawer, which was not even locked. That is a thief with strange priorities, Constable."

The constable looked at the cash drawer, which held four pounds and some silver, untouched, and wrote in his notebook again, more slowly.

Maren went back to her own office and sat before the tide ledger with her hands flat on the binding, in the manner of a woman about to open a door into a dark room. The ledger was the whole law of her working life. It was a tall book bound in grey canvas, ruled by the printers into columns for date, hour, height of water, vessel, master, and remarks, and she wrote it up in the office's iron-gall ink, which came in stone bottles from the chandlery and dried to a brown-black that bit into the page. She had kept it for eleven years and there was not a blot in it she could not date.

She turned to the night page for the fourteenth.

CHAPTER V

To understand what Maren found there, the village would have said, you must first understand about the Merrow Bell — because everything that autumn led back to the Merrow Bell the way all the lanes of Glasswater Stave led back to the quay.

The Merrow Bell was a coasting ketch of ninety tons that had gone down in the October gales the year before, on the Saltgrass shoals two miles east of Wrack Point, in weather so thick that no boat could stand out to her. Her crew had come ashore in their own dinghy, her master swearing the seas had opened her garboards; and there she lay all winter in eight fathoms, with her insured cargo in her hold — and the insurance had been paid out on cargo and hull alike, a sum the village repeated to itself with reverence.

In the spring the underwriters had sold their interest in the wreck, as underwriters do, to whoever would gamble on raising what the sea had kept. The gambler was Severin Quenholt.

Quenholt was the salvage broker of Glasswater Stave. He was a wide, soft-stepping man of fifty with a fair beard going grey and a manner of agreeing with everyone so pleasantly that it took a careful listener to notice he had promised nothing. He held the salvage rights of half the bad ground on the Hethering coast; he bought wrecks the way other men bought standing timber, and his lugger and his hired divers worked them through the fair months. He kept the net-loft at the south end of the quay as his store, and what came up from the sea — chain, anchors, copper, cargo — went into the net-loft and out again to buyers up the coast, and a fixed percentage went, as the law required, through the harbormaster's survey.

That was the rub, and the village knew it without being told. For a salvage cargo landed at Glasswater Stave, Corwin Pellow's signature was worth money: his survey fixed what had been recovered and what it weighed, and the harbormaster's book and the broker's book had to agree. For twenty-one years they always had. But the Merrow Bell was different, said the quay; the Merrow Bell was raising gear and divers' stages all summer, and yet the survey kept being put off; and the harbormaster had been heavy in his mind for a fortnight; and now the harbormaster was drowned in his own basin with the port's chronometer missing, and whatever else you might say of the arrangement of those facts, said the quay, the sea never struck any man with an iron edge.

Maren knew more than the quay did, though she had told no one yet. She knew that on his last living afternoon, Corwin Pellow had stood in her office doorway with his hat in his hand and said that he would want her ledger on Monday — the original, not a copy — for the Merrow Bell survey; and that when she had asked him why the original, he had looked at her for a moment with his patient square face and said: "Because paper is honest, Maren, and I am beginning to think nothing else on this quay is."

CHAPTER VI

The night page for the fourteenth had five entries, and Maren Ashquill had written four of them.

She knew her own hand the way she knew her own walk, and she knew the office ink. The first four entries — the Saltwillow through the lock on the flood, the gates wound to behind her, the height at the gauge, the watch handed to the night man — stood in iron-gall brown, bitten well into the paper, already going dark with age as iron-gall does in its first weeks. The fifth entry was not hers.

It was a good forgery; she gave it that much, professionally, the way a wronged carpenter might admire the joinery of the man who robbed him. The hand was a studied copy of her own upright round-hand. The ruling was respected, the abbreviations were correct, even the registrar's tick in the margin was copied. It recorded, in eleven words, that the lock sluice had been opened at half past eleven on the night of the fourteenth to scour weed from the basin, on the harbormaster's verbal order, and shut again at midnight.

A scouring of the basin: water moving in the dark, the sluice run wide, the lock working — everything, in short, that would explain a man slipping at the sluice in the wet and the noise, alone at midnight on lawful business. The entry was an accident, written before the accident happened.

Two things were wrong with it, and Maren sat very still and looked at them both.

The first was the tide. The fourteenth had been a neap tide — the moon at her quarter, the slackest water of the month, the painted gauge barely moving between high and low. Nobody scours a basin on a neap tide; there is no head of water to scour with. It was the one night of the month when the order made no sense, and the one fact a forger copying the shape of the ledger would never think to check against the almanac. Whoever wrote that line knew her hand, knew the office forms, knew the routine of the lock — and did not know the sea.

The second was smaller, and she bent close to the page to be sure of it. The ink of the fifth entry had not bitten into the paper. It sat upon the surface, glossier than the rest, with a faint violet cast at the thin strokes where iron-gall would have gone grey-brown. She had seen that cast before, on bills of lading that came up the coast: logwood ink, the cheap quick-black of pocket bottles and travelling men, an ink no stone bottle in her office had ever held.

Maren Ashquill closed the ledger, locked it in the registrar's safe, and put the key on the cord around her neck. Then she went to find Constable Marlock, walking neither fast nor slow, while behind her on the wall of the office the empty walnut case of the Lyle-Hannett chronometer hung open like a question.
CHAPTER VII

The inquest on Corwin Pellow was held on the third day, in the long room of The Cold Lantern, because the village had no other room that would hold the crowd. The coroner came out from Saltern Cross; the jury were seven men and two women of the village, sworn on the inn's own Bible with the fish-scale still under their nails; and Widow Hetch shut the bar for the duration, which the older men counted the most serious mark of the occasion.

Tabb Orrison gave his evidence first, twisting his cap, and was treated gently. He told how he had come down at first light to grease the pawls of the eastern gate; how he had seen the coat on the water and then the white hair; how he had run up the quay shouting with the grease-can still in his hand. Asked by the coroner whether the basin had shown anything else amiss, he said the water had stood high on the gauge for the time of month, which he had thought strange even in his fright, the tides then being at the neaps, and the gates shut fast since the Saltwillow came through on the evening of the fourteenth.

Doctor Imbrey Fosk gave the medical evidence in the dry, complete manner of long practice, and the long room was silent under it. Death by drowning, the lungs taking water, the man alive when he entered the basin. A wound at the right temple, got before the water: narrow, deep at one end, clean-edged, made by an iron edge swung with force, and not to be had from any fall against rounded and weeded coping stones. A man so struck, said the doctor, might be insensible past saving himself in two feet of water, let alone the basin's twelve.

The coroner asked whether the wound could have been self-got, and the doctor said that a man may do many things, but he does not commonly strike himself above the ear with an iron edge and then arrange to drown.

Constable Marlock testified to the state of the lock: gates shut and pawled, sluice shut, the harbormaster's office found locked the next morning with the cash drawer untouched, and the port's chronometer — here the long room stirred — missing from its case, travelling box and winding key gone with it. The registrar, called briefly, confirmed the heights of water in her ledger for the fourteenth and was not asked about its pages further, the constable having requested, in the interest of an inquiry then proceeding, that the documents not be canvassed in open court; and the coroner, an old hand, led the questions elsewhere.

The jury were out twenty minutes and returned the only verdict the evidence allowed: wilful murder, by a person or persons unknown. The coroner adjourned the inquest sine die, released the body for burial, and was driven back over the downs before dark. The Cold Lantern reopened its bar within the hour, and the long room, which had heard the word murder said aloud at last, said it over to itself all evening in every corner, quietly, the way the sea works at a stone.
CHAPTER VIII

Wrack Point light stood three miles east of the village on a shoulder of black rock, and Lessa Pellow kept it. She had been keeper there for six years — the Trinity men had made difficulties about a woman at first, and had then discovered, as Glasswater Stave could have told them, that difficulties made with a Pellow were difficulties wasted. She was the harbormaster's sister, younger by a dozen years, with the same square patient face, and she had the news of his death from the relief boat before noon of the first day.

Maren walked out to Wrack Point on the second evening, because the constable's inquiry moved at the speed of the constable and there were questions only a Pellow could answer. She found Lessa in the lamp room, cleaning the lens carriage with a chamois, working as Maren herself had worked, because the Pellows and the Ashquills grieved in the same dialect.

"He came out here on the Sunday," Lessa said, not stopping her work. "He sat where you are sitting and he asked me how a man should act when doing his plain duty would ruin half his neighbors. I told him a lighthouse answer: the light doesn't choose who it shines for. He laughed and said the trouble with the shore was that everything on it had two sets of books."

"Did he name anyone?"

"He named the Merrow Bell." Lessa folded the chamois and looked at her. "He told me he had put the survey off twice already, and would not put it off a third time. He said when a wreck spends a winter in eight fathoms and the broker is in no hurry to land her cargo, the cargo is the thing to look at. And he said — " she stopped, and her hands were still for the first time. "He said: if anything odd should happen at the lock, Lessa, make them look at my book and Maren's book side by side. Paper is honest. He was fond of saying that, our father said it before him."

The two women sat in the lamp room while the light came round and round above them, sweeping the Saltgrass shoals where the Merrow Bell lay in her eight fathoms, and below the rock the ebb ran east, dark and quiet, the slack ebb of a neap tide.

CHAPTER IX

In the middle of that week the village contrived to frighten itself with a second mystery, which is worth setting down because of what it shows about the temper of a place where murder has come ashore.

The chandlery at Glasswater Stave is kept by the Brisk family, and has been since the trust was chartered; it sells everything a port wants, from stone bottles of the registrar's ink to tarred twine, and its store-room runs back into the rock of the hill itself. On the Wednesday morning young Mrs Brisk opened the store-room and found the brass spirit-lamp gone from its shelf — the big swinging lamp off the wrecked steamer Carnholm, which had hung there a dozen years waiting for a buyer who never came. The shelf was dusty; the dust was disturbed; the store-room window, which had not been opened in the memory of the trade, stood unlatched.

In an ordinary month the Brisks would have grumbled and re-latched the window. In that month, the missing lamp went up the street faster than the morning bread. By noon it was known in every kitchen that the murderer had been in the chandlery store-room in the night; by two o'clock he had been heard there, breathing; by four the wildfowlers were saying that a boat had been seen standing off the bar with no lights, and the older women had remembered every unquiet thing the coast had done since the Merrow Bell went down. Constable Marlock, who had murder enough on his hands, walked down to the chandlery with his notebook and the weary patience of a man obliged to take evidence about dust.

The truth surfaced on the Friday, and it was sixteen years old. The Brisks' youngest, a boy of the age when boats are the whole of life, had taken the lamp himself, by the window, at night, to light the shed where he and the curate's nephew were rebuilding a condemned dinghy they had bought for four shillings — and had been too frightened by the uproar to bring it back. He restored it tearfully by the same window, was found out by the dust he disturbed the second time, and was dealt with by his mother in the old style, the constable declining jurisdiction with what he called, privately, the only satisfaction that week had offered him.

The village laughed at itself, but thinly, and the laughter did not last the evening. For the spirit-lamp had shown Glasswater Stave the state of its own mind: every shadow on the quay now had a name it dared not say. The harbormaster was newly buried; the inquest verdict stood at murder by persons unknown; and the person unknown, the village understood without one word being exchanged over one counter, was not unknown at all. He was a man they met in the street, whose voice they heard agreeing pleasantly in the inn of an evening; and until the constable's slow notebook closed around a name, every missing lamp and unlatched window on the Hethering coast would be laid at his shadowed door. A village can live with a mystery, the saying on that coast goes, but not with a suspicion; a mystery sits in the parlor, but a suspicion eats at table.

It was in those same days that Maren Ashquill was twice seen walking the quay after dark, from the registrar's office to the head of the lock and back, stopping a long while at the painted gauge; and the village, which missed nothing, drew its conclusions there too. The registrar was reading the water, they said, the way her sort read paper. The tide ledger had gone into the safe and not come out; the constable had been again to Saltern Cross; something was being added up, in iron-gall figures, in that square stone office; and Glasswater Stave, which kept its heart under administration, waited for the books of the matter to balance with a confidence it could not have explained to a stranger. Paper is honest, the harbormaster had been used to say. The village had buried the man and kept the saying.
CHAPTER X

Constable Marlock, meanwhile, had arrested no one, but he had come as near to it as made the village hold its breath: he had taken Tabb Orrison into the back room of the constabulary cottage and kept him there an afternoon.

The constable's reasoning was the reasoning of a careful man with one ledger entry and no imagination. The forged line recorded a sluice opening; the sluice was the lock-keeper's machinery; the lock-keeper was old and slept like the dead, and his apprentice held the keys and the grease-can and knew every pawl and winch of the gates. Tabb had access to the basin at all hours. Tabb had found the body — and the man who finds the body, said the constable's notebook, must always account for himself. And Tabb, it now appeared, had been seen on the quay past eleven on the night of the fourteenth, when by his own first account he had been abed.

The boy did himself no good. He went white and then red; he contradicted himself about the eleven o'clock errand; and when the constable pressed him on whether he could copy the registrar's hand, he said, with the awful honesty of the very frightened, that anyone in the port could try, for the ledger lay open on the registrar's counter half of every working day.

It was Maren who took the boy's part, and she did it with the ledger itself. She walked Constable Marlock through the forged entry word by word, and then she put her finger on the column where the heights of water stood, and asked the constable to send to any almanac in the village for the tides of the fourteenth. A neap tide, she said, while the constable looked up the unfamiliar word; the slackest water of the month. Tabb Orrison had greased every pawl of those gates since he was fourteen; he had the tides in his body the way he had his own pulse. Whoever forged a scouring order for a neap tide had a landsman's cunning and a landsman's ignorance, and whatever else might be said of Tabb, the sea had raised him.

"Then who, Mistress Ashquill?" said the constable, heavily. "Who in this port knows the forms of your office and not the ways of the water?"

Maren looked out of the constabulary window, down the quay, to where the net-loft stood at the south end with its doors shut, and did not answer the question aloud.

CHAPTER XI

The basin gave up its second witness on the fifth day, when the constable had the sluice culvert and the silt grate dragged.

The silt grate was an iron lattice at the basin's deep corner, where the scour of years had piled a black ooze of weed and grit, and what came up from it, among the bottle glass and the lost shackles, were two things. The first was a boat-hook — not a quay boat-hook, but a heavy salvage pattern with a riveted iron head, and on the ash shaft, stamped deep and filled with old paint, the letters M.B. Tabb Orrison, who had helped stow the Merrow Bell's recovered gear a dozen times that summer, knew the stamp at sight: it was the mark the salvage crew struck into everything raised from the wreck. The hook was from the Merrow Bell's own salvage gear, out of the net-loft stores.

The second thing was small, and it was Maren who understood it. Caught in the grate's lattice, bright among the black ooze, lay a knurled brass button the size of a thumbnail: the winding-crown of a chronometer, snapped off at the stem. She turned it in her palm and felt the breakage rough under her thumb, and she thought of the empty walnut case on the office wall.

Doctor Fosk, shown the boat-hook, set its riveted head against his measurements of the wound and would commit himself no further than a dry man ever commits himself: the iron edge of such a head, he said, answered the wound at the temple in width, in depth, and in the clean line of it, as the rounded coping stones did not.

So the shape of the night assembled itself, fact by fact, in the constable's notebook. The harbormaster had gone down to his office at nine. Someone had been there before him, or had followed him in — someone who had come for the chronometer, or for the books, or for both. There had been a struggle in which the chronometer's winding-crown was snapped off and a salvage boat-hook was swung once. And then the basin; and then, at some later, cooler hour, a forger's lamp, and eleven words of logwood ink meant to turn the whole of it into weather and bad luck.

"The net-loft," said Constable Marlock at last, closing the notebook. "Everything walks back to the net-loft."

"Everything," said Maren, "walks back to the survey that was never held."
CHAPTER XII

The ink was proven in Doctor Fosk's surgery at Saltern Cross, on a deal table cleared of its bottles, with Constable Marlock and Maren for witnesses and the tide ledger lying open at the night page under the doctor's good lamp.

Doctor Fosk was no chemist by trade, but a coroner's surgeon on a smuggling coast learns paper the way he learns knife wounds. He worked with a camel-hair brush, a saucer of weak acid, and the patience of a man poaching an egg. On a sacrificed corner of blotting paper he showed them first what the office's own iron-gall ink did under the acid: the brown-black line stood fast, bitten into the fibre, only paling a little, because iron-gall writes in rust and rust does not wash.

Then he touched the brush, delicately, to the tail of one letter of the forged entry, where the stroke ran out into the margin's waste.

The black bloomed violet, lifted, and let go of the paper.

"Logwood ink," said Doctor Fosk, in the tone he used over wounds. "A dye-black. It sits on the page; it does not marry it. Iron-gall bites; logwood paints. Your fifth entry, Constable, was written with a travelling man's pocket bottle, and no such ink has ever been issued to the registrar's office — I have Mistress Ashquill's chandlery receipts here for eleven years of stone bottles."

The constable bent over the page a long time. "And no man can say when it was written."

"The ink can," said the doctor. "Logwood fades from the first week; this is barely settled. And iron-gall darkens; the registrar's four entries are a fortnight old by their color and the forged line could not be much older than the death. Write it in your book, Constable: the page says the line was added after the fourteenth, in an ink the office never owned, by a hand that was not the registrar's, recording a sluice-opening the tide makes nonsense of. Paper is honest. It is only people who lie."

CHAPTER XIII

They searched the net-loft on the eighth day, with a magistrate's warrant got at Saltern Cross, and Severin Quenholt stood in the doorway while they did it and agreed with everyone pleasantly.

The net-loft was the long low store at the south end of the quay, dark as a chapel and crowded like a wrecked hold: coils of recovered chain, anchors red with sea-rust, stacked copper sheathing, cargo blocks, and along the south wall the salvage barrels, each stenciled with the name of the wreck it had been raised from. The constable's men worked down the row of Merrow Bell barrels one by one — rope, scrap brass, broken lamp gimbals — and found nothing a salvage store should not hold.

It was Maren who stopped at the salt barrel.

It stood apart, an old provision barrel marked for coarse salt, of the kind every store on the coast kept for salting down lines and hides; nothing about it asked attention. But its lid sat fractionally proud, and when she bent close she saw that the salt-crusted hinges of its lid-hasp had been broken and re-set, the crust of years split along the seam, white and fresh as a cracked tooth. Salt seals what it touches, given time; this seal had been opened in days, not years.

Under a hand's depth of coarse salt, wrapped in oiled canvas and a salvage flag, lay the travelling box of the Lyle-Hannett chronometer, and in the box the chronometer itself, brass bright, face intact, the stem of its winding-crown snapped to a rough nub. Beside it, rolled in a tobacco-tin, were papers: the broker's true accounts of the Merrow Bell, in Severin Quenholt's own neat figures.

The accounts were the motive, plain as a chart. The Merrow Bell's insured cargo — the cargo the underwriters had paid for, the cargo the salvage gamble was priced on — was not in the wreck and had not been for months. Quenholt's divers had stripped the hold quietly in the spring, and the goods had gone up the coast in small lots through other ports, sold twice over: once to the insurers in the loss, once to the buyers in the dark. The survey Corwin Pellow would not put off a third time would have opened an empty hold under the eyes of the law and priced the fraud to the shilling. Pellow's book and the broker's book would not have agreed; and on that disagreement, Quenholt's whole standing on the Hethering coast — rights, licenses, credit — would have gone down like the ketch itself.

"He took the chronometer to make it a robbery," said Constable Marlock, slowly, turning the brass over in his hands. "Any thief would take it; it was the only thing worth taking. And then it was too known to sell."

"He took it," said Maren, "because it was too known to sell. A stolen chronometer keeps a robbery alive in everyone's mind. But it would have hanged him in his own loft, so it went under the salt until the talk died." She touched the broken crown-stem with one finger. "He wound it while he waited. Men of figures cannot leave a stopped clock alone."

CHAPTER XIV

The night the Merrow Bell was lost had entered the parish memory before that autumn gave the loss its second meaning, and the account of it belongs here, as the village would tell it.

She had been a familiar of the port for years: a coasting ketch of ninety tons, broad in the bow, slow and sure, working general cargoes up and down the Hethering coast in all weathers. Her master had used Glasswater Stave through every season and knew the Saltgrass shoals, the village would have said, as well as any man who did not actually fish them. On her last passage she had loaded at Saltern Cross and stood east in the afternoon of as dirty an October day as the year produced, with the glass falling and the wind backing southeast, against the advice — this was remembered later, and weighed — of older masters drinking by the chandlery stove.

By dark it was a full gale with rain like shot. The watchers at Wrack Point light — Lessa Pellow had the middle watch that night, a detail the village remembered afterwards with a shiver it could not justify — saw a vessel's lights east of the point laboring badly, losing way, sagging down onto the shoals; saw a flare go up, and then nothing but weather. No boat could stand out to her; the coast has never forgiven itself such nights, and never been able to mend them. Towards midnight the Merrow Bell's own dinghy came in over the bar on the flood, half-swamped, with the whole crew of five aboard, frozen and bailing with their boots; and her master, dripping in the long room of The Cold Lantern, swore his statement there and then before witnesses, because a master who loses his ship learns to begin his accounting early. The seas had opened her garboards on the first touch of the shoal, he said; she had filled too fast for anything but the boat; she had gone down inside the half hour, in eight fathoms by his own last cast of the lead.

The underwriters' men were in the village within the week, dry men in city coats asking wet questions. The cargo was insured, the hull was insured; the master's statement held; the weather was its own witness; and the claims were paid in the spring with the underwriters' usual face, which on that coast is read as easily as the almanac: the face of men paying what they cannot disprove. The sum was repeated in the village with reverence, and the Merrow Bell passed, as wrecks do, from vessel to property — eight fathoms down on the Saltgrass shoals, her insured cargo in her hold, her value now a paper thing, to be sold by the underwriters to whoever cared to gamble against the sea.

That the gambler was Severin Quenholt surprised nobody; salvage was his trade and the shoals were his garden. What was remarked, in the slow way the village remarks things, was the price he paid — generous for a winter-worked wreck, the chandlery thought, on no better knowledge than chandleries have — and the leisure of his season. A broker who pays high for a cargo gambles on raising it quickly; yet the diving stages were not rigged over the Merrow Bell until late spring, and through the fair months that followed, what came ashore to the net-loft was gear and metal, chain and blocks and the ship's furniture, the bones of the vessel and never the meat of her. The survey of cargo, twice arranged, was twice put off — once for weather nobody else had noticed, once for a delay in lighters that the quay could not verify. The harbormaster's face on survey mornings was studied the way the glass is studied before a passage.

All of which the village possessed, that autumn, as pieces only; and pieces, on the Hethering coast, are nobody's business to assemble aloud. It was only afterwards — after the basin, after the silt grate, after the salt barrel gave up what it had been keeping — that the parish memory ran the story backwards and found it had known everything from the first: that a ship may be lost honestly and robbed afterwards; that the sea's verdict can be bought a second time by men who were never aboard; and that of all the cargoes the Merrow Bell ever carried, the heaviest was landed at no quay, in no lighter, under no survey — being the weight of what Severin Quenholt knew, all that pleasant agreeing summer, every time he passed the harbormaster's window and touched his hat to the man whose signature he could not afford.
CHAPTER XV

They took Severin Quenholt at The Cold Lantern that evening, where he sat at his usual table with his usual glass, agreeing pleasantly with his neighbors about the sad state of the times.

He denied nothing for long; that was the strangest part, the village said afterwards, and the part that made it believable. Confronted in the inn's back parlor with the chronometer, the tobacco-tin accounts, the boat-hook stamped M.B., and the doctor's saucer of violet-blooming ink, he sat quiet for a while with his hands folded, like a merchant checking a column twice, and then he balanced the books aloud.

He had gone to the harbor office late on the fourteenth to reason with Pellow, he said — only to reason, to offer the survey a percentage of its own. The harbormaster had listened with his patient square face and then said he would have the registrar's ledger in on Monday, the original, and the survey held in the open, and the books set side by side; paper was honest, said the harbormaster, even on this quay. Then he had turned to take the chronometer's travelling box down — the survey would need official time, he said, everything would be done by the book — and something in the calm of it, said Quenholt, in the man's untroubled certainty that figures must come right, had risen over him like water over a gauge. The boat-hook had been standing against his own barrow of salvage gear by the door. He did not remember deciding.

The sluice he had opened afterwards, and shut again, to put moving water and a wet coping into the night's story; the forged line he had written before dawn at his loft desk, in his pocket ink, copying the registrar's hand from a summer of watching her counter. He had known the forms exactly. He had not thought once of the moon.

"The tide," he said, when the constable read him Maren's evidence, and he said it with a bookkeeper's bitterness at a sum gone wrong by one figure. "Twenty years brokering wrecks, and I never learned the water. Pellow now — Pellow would never have written that line. He'd have looked at the almanac first." It was as near to regret as he came, and nearer than anyone expected.

CHAPTER XVI

The assizes would have Severin Quenholt at the spring sitting, and what the law would do with him the village did not yet know and Maren did not let herself guess. The Merrow Bell's underwriters sent a dry man in a city coat who spent two days with the tobacco-tin accounts and went away again; the salvage rights were forfeit; the net-loft was sealed with the magistrate's wax, and the gulls stood on its roof ridge as if appointed.

Lessa Pellow buried her brother on the headland side of the churchyard, where the stones lean east with the weather, and went back to her light the same evening, because the light does not choose, and does not stop.

Maren Ashquill went back to her ledger.

There was an entry to make that no column had been ruled for, and she made it in the remarks space at the foot of the night page for the fourteenth, beneath the forged line, which the magistrate had ordered must stay in the book — struck through in red, attested by three signatures, but legible, because a record that pretends it was never lied to is itself a lie. She wrote the plain facts: the line above was forged, by whom, proven how; the chronometer recovered and rehung; the survey of the Merrow Bell held at last, an empty hold opened to the light. She wrote it in iron-gall ink, and watched the brown-black bite into the paper, going darker as it dried, the way the truth settles.

The last thing Corwin Pellow had ever written in his own harbor book stood on the facing page, dated the morning of the fourteenth, in his square upright hand: "Sluice gate ironwork wants seeing to." Maren read it through once, and found that it was, like every line the harbormaster had ever entered, exactly true, and like the man himself, attending faithfully to the small iron of the world while the large wrong came up behind him.

She cleaned her pen, ruled off the page, and shut the ledger; and outside the window the tide, which keeps the only book that cannot be forged, came up the painted gauge to the height she had recorded for it, to the half-inch, at the minute the Lyle-Hannett chronometer — rehung that morning, its crown made good by the instrument-maker at Saltern Cross — said it would.
